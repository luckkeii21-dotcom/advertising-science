## 1. The mechanism

Three things decide who wins a Meta auction, and we have treated all three as things that happen before the ad is served. Your bid. The estimated chance this person does the thing you asked for. And ad quality.

Ad quality is the one everybody nods at and nobody can see. Until 28 October the codex held it as a vague bag: hiding the ad, engagement bait, withheld information, sensationalised language. All of it happens on the ad, in the feed, in the first two seconds. Meta has now put a measuring instrument behind that term, and the instrument reaches past the ad, past the click, past the form, and into what happened to the person weeks later.

Meta's sentence, word for word: ad quality "reflects feedback from people who view, click or hide ads, detection of low-quality traits such as engagement bait or withheld information, and **more than 200 million customer survey responses a year covering satisfaction with purchases, shipping delays, unexpected charges, and whether people felt misled**."

Read the second half again. Shipping delays. Unexpected charges. Feeling misled. None of those is an advertising event. Every one of them happens after the money changed hands, and Meta says they feed the term that prices your next impression.

The analogy that maps: a market, and a landlord who keeps a complaints box at the exit. Two traders bid for the same corner pitch and the loud one pays more, so he gets it. Next quarter the landlord reads the box, sees that the loud trader's customers left angry about a price that was not on the sign, and raises his rent. The trader never sees the box and never sees a line on the invoice saying why. He only knows the corner got expensive. That is the structure of this claim, including the part where you cannot audit it.

Meta draws the conclusion itself, and this is the sentence to remember: "The highest bid or the biggest budget doesn't automatically win, quality of ad experience, purchase experience, and product satisfaction matter too... A more relevant ad, backed by a better customer experience, can beat a higher bid."

## 2. The evidence

[[Auction Mechanics & Bidding#AU-102|AU-102]], **T1**. Meta for Business News, 28 October, read at source 29 October. T1 because Meta wrote the sentence on its own property and the sentence is quoted verbatim.

It sits on top of two older T1 claims that it does not replace. [[Auction Mechanics & Bidding#AU-001|AU-001]] holds the three auction factors and already listed viewer feedback and low-quality traits as inputs to ad quality, so AU-102 does not change that list. It adds a limb nobody had seen and a volume behind the whole thing. [[Auction Mechanics & Bidding#AU-002|AU-002]] holds that estimated action rate plus ad quality together make up ad relevance, and that a more relevant ad can beat a higher bid. AU-102 is AU-002 with fulfilment folded into the definition of relevance.

[[Auction Mechanics & Bidding#AU-003|AU-003]] is the contrast worth sitting with. It is **T3**: the roughly $25 against roughly $100 CPM gap between lead-magnet funnels and book-a-call funnels on the identical lead event, with the causal path admitted as speculation and no account data shown.

Here is the tier lesson, and it runs in the direction you will not expect. **AU-102 is T1 and you can do almost nothing with the number. AU-003 is T3 and it predicts a four-times cost difference you could go and look for.** T1 means Meta said it in public and we quoted it correctly. It does not mean measurable, it does not mean quantifiable for a client, and it does not mean actionable this week. Tier is provenance. Usefulness is a separate axis, and lesson 073 made that point from Google's side of the fence with AT-131. This is the same point from Meta's.

Now the guards on AU-102, because they are most of its content.

200 million responses a year is **global, across all of Meta's advertising**. It is not a sample of your account. Meta publishes no per-advertiser threshold, no weighting, no decay, and no indication of how a small advertiser ever accumulates enough responses to be measured at all. There is no quality readout in Ads Manager, no survey count per advertiser, and no way to attribute a CPM move to this. And the post never says the survey limb applies to lead generation, where there is no purchase and no shipping.

The source is a best-practice post, not documentation of the auction formula. One source, Meta's own.

## 3. Our accounts

Our whole book is Meta and our whole book is lead generation, so the loudest half of this claim may not touch us at all. Nobody on a Meta Instant Form bought anything. There is no parcel, no delivery window and no invoice, so shipping delays and unexpected charges have no counterpart in anything we run. Meta is silent on whether the survey limb reaches a lead-gen advertiser, and we are not going to assume it does.

The limb that does reach us is **whether people felt misled**, and we already run a control for it without calling it that. Two accounts carry a claims ledger: SJR Commercial, from the Business Owners Guide ebook, and RK Consumer Services. Each sorts every claim into usable, confirm and banned. SJR's banned bucket holds eleven entries and the reasons read exactly like the inputs Meta just described. The $87,138 cargo-van owner salary and the $130,803 box-truck figure, both earnings claims from February 2023. "As little as $5,000 down", a specific down payment, when the lead filter now sits at $3,000. Savings of $10,000 to $30,000 below book. "Saving 30% on fuel nets $18,000+ a year", unsupported. RK bans "guaranteed approval" and "100% approved" outright. Every one of those would land a buyer in a room where the number on the screen does not match the number in the ad.

Now the scale, because it decides how much of this is even reachable. SJR, sign-ups between 1 June and 31 August against spend in the same window: dump $3,565 and 487 CRM people and 3 sales, which is $1,188 per truck at 0.62%. Vans $3,572 and 613 people and 1 sale, $3,572 at 0.16%. Everything of ours together, $7,970 and 1,269 people and 5 sales, $1,594 a sale at 0.39%. Phoenix matches at about $1,035 a truck at 0.77%.

Sit with the 0.39%. **Of 1,269 people our ads brought in, 1,264 did not buy.** Those are the people a satisfaction survey would reach, and not one of them has a purchase, a shipment or an invoice to be surveyed about. Meta's instrument has nothing to ask them. At our current weekly scale, SJR $767.78 for 136 opt-ins at $5.65 and StayWell $562.63 for 23 at $24.46 in the week of 23 to 29 October, the honest position is that this claim is a reason to keep the claims ledgers strict and nothing more.

**No new check comes out of this lesson, deliberately.** Eight checks from lessons 067 to 073 are still unrun with no owner. Meta publishes no readout for this term, so there is no screen to open and nothing to read. Adding a ninth item to a list nobody is working would be the wrong output.

What this is genuinely for: the next time a client has a fulfilment problem or an expectation problem, it stops being only a retention conversation. Meta states that a worse customer experience can cost you the auction against a lower bid. The honest form of that advice, and the only form: **a client with an expectation-setting problem may be paying more per impression for it, Meta says so, and nobody can show them the line item.** Never quantify it. Never put a number on it in a report.

## 4. The decision rule

Treat every claim in an ad as a media cost as well as a compliance risk, keep the banned bucket strict for that reason as well, and never put a number on what it is worth.

> [!note]- Answer key
>
> **1.** Before the click: feedback from people who view, click or hide the ad; detection of low-quality traits such as engagement bait and withheld information. After the click: satisfaction with purchases, shipping delays, unexpected charges, whether people felt misled. The after group is the new part. The before group restates AU-001, which has held since August. Full marks require naming AU-001 as the thing the first group already said.
>
> **2.** AU-003 is the one you could act on, because it names a mechanism, a direction and a size: lead-magnet funnels at roughly $25 CPMs against book-a-call funnels at roughly $100 on the same lead event, which is a four-times gap you could go and test on a live account by building a high-on-page-action funnel and comparing. It is weakly evidenced, the causal story is admitted guesswork and no account data was shown, so a test is exactly what it deserves. AU-102 is Meta's own sentence and is quoted correctly, and it still yields no action: no readout, no per-advertiser response count, no threshold, no way to attribute a CPM move to it. Correct answers say that confidence in a claim and usability of a claim are different things. No credit for ranking them by who published them.
>
> **3.** Something close to: "Meta states that purchase experience and product satisfaction feed the ad-quality term in its auction, and that a more relevant ad backed by a better customer experience can beat a higher bid. So yes, in Meta's own account of its system, a surprise fee at checkout and a high refund rate work against you on media cost as well as on retention. There is no readout for it anywhere in Ads Manager, no per-advertiser survey count, and no way to separate that effect from a creative change or a seasonal CPM move. So fix the checkout fee because it is costing you refunds, and treat any media-cost improvement as a bonus we will not forecast." Refuse: any number, any percentage, any promised CPM reduction, and any claim that you can measure the before and after. Deduct for quoting the 200 million as if it were a sample of their account.
>
> **4.** The second reason: a specific down payment that the lead filter will not honour puts a buyer in the room expecting $5,000 and hearing a different number, which is the "felt misled" and "unexpected charges" input Meta says feeds the auction quality term, so the line is a potential media cost on top of being a compliance trigger. It cannot be used to justify the ban to the client because there is no evidence to show them: no readout, no line item, no measured effect on our account or any account, and the source does not even say the survey limb applies to lead generation. Telling SJR this line is raising their CPMs would be inventing the only half they care about. The compliance reason is the one that goes in the email. Full marks require both halves, and the strongest answers add that the ledger does not need a second reason to hold.
>
> **5.** At 0.39% buying, 1,264 of 1,269 people have no purchase, no shipment and no invoice, so there is nothing for a satisfaction survey to ask them about, and the handful who did buy bought from the dealer rather than through Meta. A per-advertiser instrument built on purchase satisfaction has almost no surface on an account shaped like ours. The thing Meta never says: whether the survey limb applies to lead-generation advertisers at all. Meta also publishes no per-advertiser threshold, no weighting and no indication of how a small advertiser accumulates enough responses to be measured, and 200 million a year is a global figure across all of Meta's advertising rather than a sample of anybody's account. Any answer that treats the 200 million as evidence about our accounts is wrong.
