# Lesson 060 · The Tighter Ceiling Is Always the Same One

No video today. Sunday.

Yesterday you learned a ceiling on how many ad sets an account is allowed. Today there is a second one, from a completely different place, and the two of them settle an argument you have been having with yourself for months.

## 1. The mechanism

Every ad set you run has a daily cost of admission.

The rule is simple. An ad set's daily budget has to be at least one times the cost of the thing you are buying. If a booked call costs $200, an ad set chasing booked calls needs $200 a day. Not because Meta refuses to spend less. Because below that the ad set buys less than one result a day, and an ad set that buys less than one result a day is a slot machine with a very slow arm.

Think about a market stall. Each stall costs the same rent whether you sell anything or not. One stall, you staff it properly. Four stalls on the same money, and each one sits unattended half the day. The money did not change. What changed is how thin you spread it.

That is the budget ceiling: **your daily spend, divided by what one result costs, is the most ad sets you can afford.**

Now do the algebra, the same way you did yesterday.

Daily spend divided by cost per result is daily results. By definition. The two inputs cancel and only the count survives.

So the budget ceiling is **daily results**. Yesterday's signal ceiling was **weekly results over fifty**.

Put them side by side. Daily results is weekly results over seven. So one ceiling is weekly results over seven and the other is weekly results over fifty. The budget ceiling is always looser, by exactly fifty over seven, which is 7.14.

Always. Every account, every budget, every cost per result. The ratio has no inputs left in it to vary.

This is why the argument settles. When you ask "can I afford another ad set", you are asking the loose question. The tight question is whether the account produces enough conversions to teach one. **The money runs out 7.14 times later than the signal does.**

## 2. The evidence

- **MM-019, T3.** The one-times floor. Four independent operators converge. Heath sets the daily budget at the affordable customer acquisition cost. Shiver says at least 1x cost per booked call, so B2C coaching starts at $100 a day and B2B at $200 to $500. Piliero states the same at the prospecting level. Solutions 8 states it in clicks instead of dollars, because 50 clicks tells you nothing. Four operators, no shown test. T3 is the right tier, and T3 is a named bet.
- **LS-089, T1.** Yesterday's claim. Meta's own published formula. The reduction to weekly results over fifty is ours, written into the claim on 24 October.
- **MM-019's fragmentation note, T3.** Running five offers as five ad sets with one ad each, instead of one ad set with five ads, takes the same test from about $200 a day to closer to $500. The floor multiplies with every ad set you add, because each one pays its own rent.
- **MM-210, T3.** Margin and markup share a numerator and differ only in the denominator. Taught here because MM-019 arrives at two denominators and you have to catch it.
- **MM-018, T3, contested.** Our own live example of that failure. Section 4.

The tiers decide how you talk. The signal ceiling is Meta's own arithmetic and you quote it flatly. The budget floor is four practitioners agreeing without showing their accounts, and you say so.

Same four filed weeklies as yesterday. No new figures, nothing estimated.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SJR Commercial | Oct 16-22 | $779.67 | 95 | $8.21 | 13.57 | 1.90 |
| Phoenix Truxx | Oct 9-15 | $1,336.39 | 95 | $14.07 | 13.57 | 1.90 |
| StayWell | Oct 16-22 | $537.61 | 18 | $29.87 | 2.57 | 0.36 |
| ChiroWorks | Sep 30 - Oct 6, last live week | $463.28 | 17 | $27.25 | 2.43 | 0.34 |

Read the two right-hand columns. SJR could pay for thirteen ad sets and can only teach two. StayWell could pay for two and can teach none. The gap is 7.14 on all four rows, which is the identity doing its work rather than four separate findings.

The caveat, and say it out loud every time you use this table: the budget column substitutes **actual** cost per opt-in for **target** cost per opt-in, because nobody has handed us a target. If a client sets a target below what we currently pay, the budget ceiling gets looser, not tighter, because each ad set then needs less rent. The signal ceiling does not move at all. So on an ambitious account the two ceilings pull further apart and the signal one binds harder.

Two things this answers today. Every budget-based argument for splitting an ad set on these four accounts is answered by the wrong ceiling. And the live ad set count on all four accounts is still uncounted, which has been the cheapest open item on the board since lesson 036 and is worth more now, because both left sides are computed.

## 4. The denominator discipline

MM-019 states its floor twice, at two denominators, and the two rules are not the same rule.

Per day: at least 1x the cost per result. That governs whether the account can learn.

Per month: at least 1x the ticket price of the offer, so a $10,000 offer wants $10,000 a month. That governs whether the month produces a single sale at all. At a $10,000 offer it implies a sample size of one, which can verdict nothing.

Same operator, same shape, different denominator, completely different job. Quote one and apply the other and you will defend a budget for a reason that is not true.

This is the same failure MM-210 documents in pricing. Margin divides by selling price. Markup divides by cost price. A 50% markup is a 33% margin. And "markup" itself is spoken two ways by people who never announce which, so "3x markup" means price is 3x cost to one speaker and 4x cost to another.

We have that bug in our own codex. **MM-018 requires a selling price of at least 3x cost of goods, then calls a $10 product sold at $50 a "5x markup".** Five is the price-to-cost ratio. By the formal definition that product is 4x. The claim is written in keystone, price divided by cost, so anyone applying the formal definition to its "3x" imposes a floor of 4x cost and rejects products the rule meant to allow. The flag is carried on the claim. The rule's direction is unchanged.

The discipline costs four seconds: **a multiple or a floor is unusable until you name what it is divided by.**

## 5. The decision rule

**Before you split an ad set, check the signal ceiling, never the budget ceiling. The money runs out 7.14 times later than the signal does, so on every account we run, affordability is the wrong question.**

1. Both ceilings reduce to the conversion count and nothing else. Why does the budget ceiling carry cost per result in its published form at all, and what does that input actually do once you reduce it?

2. MM-019 is T3 and LS-089 is T1. A client pushes back on a recommendation built on both. Which half do you defend differently, and what do you say about the other one?

3. SJR Commercial, Oct 16-22. The client asks to add a fifth ad set and points out the account spends $111 a day against an $8.21 opt-in, so it can clearly afford it. Answer them in three sentences, using both ceilings.

4. An account runs a $4,000 offer and spends $300 a day. A booked call costs $250. How many ad sets does each ceiling allow? State the figure you need and do not have, and name which denominator trap in MM-019 you had to avoid on the way.

5. A supplier quotes "3x markup" on a product that costs you $40 landed. Give both possible selling prices, name which convention produces which, and say what you ask the supplier for instead.

> [!note]- Answer key
>
> **1.** Cost per result is in the published form because that is how the rule is derived and explained. Each ad set has to be able to buy one result a day, so you divide the money by the price of a result. Once reduced, the input does nothing. Daily spend over cost per result IS daily results, so both the spend and the price cancel and only the count survives. Real in the derivation, inert in the instrument. Same shape as LS-089, where the budget looks like a lever and is not one.
>
> **2.** Defend LS-089 as Meta's own published arithmetic, T1, and quote it flatly. Do not defend MM-019 the same way. It is four practitioners converging with no shown test, so it is a named bet. Say that out loud, say it is the direction four independent operators agree on, and say the consequence of ignoring it is thin ad sets rather than a documented penalty. The recommendation survives either way, because the binding ceiling is the signal one and that is the T1 half.
>
> **3.** Roughly: the budget does allow it, thirteen ad sets' worth, and that is the wrong ceiling. Meta's own formula allows 1.90 ad sets at 95 opt-ins a week, so the account can pay for a fifth and cannot teach a second. Adding one splits the same 95 conversions thinner, the delivery system learns less from each slice, and the gain we would be buying is not there.
>
> **4.** Budget ceiling: $300 a day over $250 a call is 1.2 ad sets. Signal ceiling: weekly booked calls over fifty, and the weekly count is not in the question, so you cannot answer it. That is the figure you need and do not have. You can say that at $300 a day and $250 a call the implied volume is about 8.4 calls a week, which would put the signal ceiling near 0.17, but say it as arithmetic on an implied figure and never as a reading. The denominator trap: the $4,000 offer price belongs to MM-019's MONTHLY floor, not the daily one. Do not divide $300 a day by $4,000, and do not use the monthly rule to judge whether the account can learn. The monthly rule only asks whether the month can produce one sale.
>
> **5.** Keystone convention, price divided by cost: 3x of $40 is **$120**. Formal definition, (price less cost) over cost: price is $40 plus three times $40, which is **$160**. A whole $40 apart on the same four words, and nobody announces which one they mean. Do not accept the multiple. Ask for the landed cost and the selling price as two numbers and compute it yourself, then check whether shipping, fulfilment and transaction fees are inside that cost, because fulfilment at 5% to 20% of revenue is enough to halve an apparent margin. The conversions to keep: margin = markup / (1 + markup), and markup = margin / (1 - margin).
