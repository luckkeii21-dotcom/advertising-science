# Lesson 016 · What a Losing Ad Is Allowed to Disprove

🎬 **Lesson video (2m 34s, silent, watch anywhere):** [[video/2026-09-04-lesson-016.mp4]]

> Lesson 003 said the ad's own row is not the verdict. Lesson 011 said every ad has a ceiling. This one is the question underneath both: when a row does come back bad, what exactly are you allowed to conclude from it?

**If you only answer two questions, answer Q2 and Q4.** Q2 is where our own account produces four zeros and only one of them is a result. Q4 is the guard that stops this lesson turning into an excuse to never kill anything.

## 1. The mechanism

Every ad you launch is a test of something. The problem is that nobody writes down what, so when the ad loses, the loss lands wherever the loudest person in the room wants it to land.

Think about interviewing one candidate for a role. They interview badly. What that disproves is that candidate. It does not disprove the job description, the salary band, or the decision to hire at all. Everyone knows this in a hiring meeting. Nobody applies it to a losing ad. One ad comes back at a bad cost per opt-in and the next meeting relitigates the offer, the price, the audience, the market and whether the whole channel works.

The fix is a specification written before launch, not a post-mortem written after. Four parts.

**One, a hypothesis with a "because" in it.** The because is the mechanism, and it names a business result rather than a platform metric. Charley T's worked example: *"If cold buyers are excluding themselves because they think they're not serious enough about coffee, and 23 of 62 told us they nearly did, then a video that disqualifies expertise brings in first purchases at or below what I'm running now because it removes the barrier before price is considered."*

**Two, what this does not test.** Written down in advance, so a loss cannot later be blamed on something the test never touched.

**Three, what a loss puts in doubt, listed before the result exists.** In the shown case the list has exactly one item: the concept. Not the product, because that came from receipts. Not the customer research, because *"people did say those things and a losing ad can't unsay them."*

**Four, what a win breaks next.** In the example, a win makes the landing page the bottleneck, because the page still opens on a specialty enthusiast while the ad disqualifies expertise. A win that creates a continuity break is a planned next job rather than a surprise.

Part three is the load-bearing one. Without it, every failed ad drags the whole chain of prior decisions back into the argument, and the account relitigates its own research every month.

There is a second half nobody writes either, and it is arithmetic rather than discipline. A row can only disprove something if it was funded enough to produce a result. An ad that never got spend has not lost. It has not been asked.

## 2. The evidence

**CR-222, T3, active, banked 2026-09-03.** The four-part specification above. Shown once, on one account, on screen, with no comparison against teams that do not do it. Nobody has measured whether pre-registration improves anything.

**CR-041, T3, active, amended 2026-09-03.** Nick Theriot's cadence: a written hypothesis in a sheet before every launch, a creative-learnings review exactly seven days after. CR-041 always had the cadence and never specified what the written thing must contain. CR-222 is the contents half. The two now sit together.

**CR-221, T3, active.** The research-side rule that carries over: an objection appearing in exactly one source is a hypothesis, not a finding, and the ad built on it gets labelled as testing that hypothesis. Same logic pointed at a result instead of an input.

**MD-136, T3, active.** The arithmetic guard, and the one that does most of the work below. A zero-spend ad is not failing against target. It is failing against the incumbents, because Meta allocates on what the next dollar returns rather than on what the average dollar returned.

**CR-225, T3, active.** Validate the concept first, then build variations of what survived. Variations of an unvalidated concept spend real money proving nothing.

Hold the tier honestly. Every claim in this lesson is T3, a practitioner claim from real spend with no shown test. There is no data anywhere, from anyone, comparing teams that pre-register their tests against teams that do not. Treat the specification as a discipline that is cheap to adopt, never as a proven lift.

## 3. Our accounts

**We have never written one.** The vault's `experiments`, `decisions` and `questions` folders hold zero files each. The word "hypothesis" appears in exactly eight client-side notes, and in every one it is a label attached to a guess rather than a specification: a note that the cost per opt-in should fall once an ad set has depth, three graded Mattia concepts marked as untested, a budget compromise both sides agreed to treat as a hypothesis. **None of the eight states what a loss would put in doubt.** Fifteen lessons in, this is the cheapest unclaimed improvement on the board.

**Now the account that shows why it matters.** ChiroWorks, Functional Tonal Neurology campaign, our own raw Meta pull for 10 to 20 July.

| Ad set | Spend | Opt-ins | Cost per opt-in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chiropractic | $65.45 | 2 | **$32.73** |
| All Ads | $159.10 | 2 | $79.55 |
| DOCTOR concept | $82.58 | 0 | n/a |
| GET A CHECKUP | $14.35 | 0 | n/a |
| PAIN concept | $12.86 | 0 | n/a |
| Upkeep concept | $10.35 | 0 | n/a |
| **Campaign** | **$344.69** | **4** | **$86.17** |

Four concepts returned zero. Read that table cold and you kill four ideas.

Now do the arithmetic. The campaign bought an opt-in for $86.17. At that price, DOCTOR's $82.58 buys 0.96 expected opt-ins, GET A CHECKUP's $14.35 buys 0.17, PAIN buys 0.15, Upkeep buys 0.12. **Not one of the four zeros was funded to the price of a single opt-in.** If every one of those concepts performed exactly at the campaign average, the chance of seeing zero anyway is 38% for DOCTOR, 85% for GET A CHECKUP, 86% for PAIN and 89% for Upkeep. The zeros are the expected result of the spend, not a verdict on the ideas.

**The one that was funded is the one that won.** Chiropractic returned $32.73 against the campaign's $86.17, which is 38% of it and the best number inside the campaign. Our own audit note calls that "roughly a third"; it is 38%. Then on 21 July we consolidated two testing campaigns into one CBO at $66/day, and the CBO routed money to the cheapest form fill and starved the chiropractic ad set to **$1.28 a day, $8.95 and 156 impressions across seven days.** That is 22 impressions a day. The client's own audit landed the same week and said the chiropractic offer was effectively untested at a chiropractic clinic. They were right about the second half of July and wrong about the first, and we could only answer them because the first half happened to be funded.

**⚠ One correction to that audit, and it is ours.** Its summary row reads $818 spend, 23 opt-ins, 3 booked, and a booking rate of **12.5%**. Three of twenty-three is **13.0%**. 12.5% needs a denominator of 24. The row does not reconcile with itself. Fifth consecutive teaching pass to find a counting or arithmetic error in our own client reporting.

**The opposite case, where a zero was a real result and had nowhere to land.** MetaTechAI ran a high-intent statics campaign with the headline, the form and a $10,000-a-month qualification gate all specified in advance. The people who came through were real business owners with business email addresses who declared $10k a month or more. **Not one replied, answered a call, or opened an email.** That month spent about $8,000 and closed one sale at $1,000; the longer window is about $7,000 producing on the order of a thousand opt-ins and fewer than ten closes. That zero genuinely disproved something, and because nobody had written the loss list in advance, it took a 70-minute post-mortem on 25 August to establish what: not the targeting, not the opt-in quality, the back end. Written down before launch, that list would have had one item on it and the year would have gone differently.

**And the near-miss, for the other direction.** StayWell's X-ray Reveal got **$2.58** in a week and fired the only appointment the pixel recorded that week. On any Monday morning that row reads $2.58 and zero opt-ins. Cut it and you cut the appointment.

## 4. The decision rule

**Before an ad launches, write the single thing its loss is allowed to put in doubt, and refuse to read a zero as a loss until that row has spent at least one opt-in at the campaign's own cost.**

## 5. Quiz

Five questions. Answers go in `_answers-inbox.md`. Partial answers are fine and still get graded.

**Q1.** CR-222 has four parts. Name the third one and say in one sentence why it is the one that changes behaviour.

**Q2 (application).** A new SJR concept ad set spends $41 in a week and returns zero opt-ins. The campaign it sits in has averaged $23 per opt-in over the same week. What does that zero disprove, and what is the smallest amount of spend that would let it disprove the concept? Show the arithmetic.

**Q3 (application).** You are writing the pre-launch specification for a Phoenix Truxx ad testing a new hook on the same offer, same audience, same form. Write part two, "what this does not test," as a list. Three items is enough.

**Q4.** This lesson makes it easy to argue that no ad can ever be killed because nothing was ever funded enough. Give the condition under which killing an ad on a zero is the correct call, and name the number you would check first.

**Q5 (application).** ChiroWorks' chiropractic ad set returned $32.73 against a campaign average of $86.17 and was then starved to $1.28 a day by a CBO. A client asks whether the chiropractic concept works. Answer them in three sentences, and say which part of your answer is evidence and which part is a hypothesis.

> [!note]- Answer key
>
> **Q1.** Part three is the list, written before launch, of what a loss would put in doubt. It changes behaviour because it fixes the blast radius of a failure in advance, so a losing ad cannot drag the offer, the pricing, the customer research or the channel back into the argument. Full marks for naming the mechanism: without it the account relitigates its own prior decisions every time an ad fails.
>
> **Q2.** It disproves nothing. $41 at $23 per opt-in is 1.78 expected opt-ins, and at that expectation the chance of seeing zero anyway is about 17%, which is not far off one week in six. To disprove the concept you need the zero to be unlikely under the assumption that the concept is average. Accept any answer that reasons from expected acquisitions rather than from a flat spend threshold: roughly $70 gives about 3 expected and a 5% chance of a zero, roughly $105 gives about 4.6 expected and a 1% chance. Full marks for saying that the honest report on $41 is "not yet read," never "lost." Bonus for naming MD-136: the ad may be losing to the incumbent on marginal cost, which is a delivery result rather than a creative result.
>
> **Q3.** Any three of: it does not test the offer, the price, the audience, the geography, the form questions, the landing destination, the follow-up speed, the sales process, or whether the truck itself is wanted. The point is that the only variable moving is the hook, so everything downstream of the click is untouched and is out of scope for this result. Mark down any answer that includes the hook itself, and mark down any answer that lists nothing measurable.
>
> **Q4.** Kill on a zero when the row has been funded past the point where a zero is unlikely for an average performer, roughly three expected acquisitions at the campaign's own cost. The first number to check is the row's spend against the campaign's cost per opt-in, not the row's own metrics, because until that ratio clears one there is no result to read. Full marks for also naming the second condition: a fully funded row inside a campaign that is otherwise hitting target is a real loss, and MD-136's remedy applies before the kill does, which is a dedicated campaign to force readable spend through it.
>
> **Q5.** Evidence: in the funded window, 10 to 20 July, the chiropractic ad set returned $32.73 per opt-in against a campaign average of $86.17, the best number in the campaign. Evidence: after 21 July it received $8.95 across seven days, so the second half of July tested nothing. Hypothesis: that the $32.73 holds at real budget, which is unproven because it rests on two opt-ins. Full marks for refusing to claim the concept is proven and for naming the fix, which is an ad-set spend floor or a separate campaign with its own wallet. Mark down any answer that presents $32.73 as a settled result.
