# Lesson 035 · Brand A Does Not Exist

Yesterday's research pass read four posts on Meta for Business News. Same masthead. Same typography. Two of them published within five weeks of each other.

One post opens with a brand that walked into January holding **14,200 incremental conversions at $7.40 per incremental result**, and a budget that grew 20% on the strength of it. It reads like every advertiser case study on that channel. The specificity is there, the CFO is in the story, the numbers are the round-then-precise pairing that real reporting produces.

At the foot of the page, below the sources and below the call to action, in the same small print as the Ipsos citation: *"Disclaimer: This is a fictional example and does not represent a real brand example."*

Brand A does not exist. Neither does the $7.40.

That is [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-126|AT-126]]. It is worth a lesson rather than a laugh because the same channel, in the same reading session, also published figures that are completely real. The masthead does not sort them. You have to.

## 1. The mechanism

Walk onto a truck lot. Four things on that lot carry a number, and they are worth four different amounts.

**The window sticker.** Manufacturer, legally required, states what it measures. Real, and checkable.

**"As low as $299 a month"** on the banner. Also real, generated under conditions printed in eight-point type: twenty percent down, 800 credit score, 72 months. The number is true about somebody who is not you.

**The framed award on the wall.** Best Dealer 2026. Real award, real plaque. The dealership entered it, submitted its own figures, and won partly on those figures. Every dealership that entered and lost is on nobody's wall.

**The family in the brochure.** Smiling, in the truck, delighted. Stock photography. Nobody in that picture bought anything.

Same wall, same day, same brand, four grades of evidence, and one of them can price a deal.

Ad-platform publishing works this way. Yesterday one publisher produced all four.

## 2. The evidence

**The brochure family. [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-126|AT-126]], T1.** "Holiday measurement strategies", 13 August 2026. Brand A, Brand B, the $7.40, the 20% budget rise, disclosed as fictional in a footnote. It sits at T1 because the claim is about the source, and what the source did was directly observed. The rule it produces: **on any Meta for Business post, check whether the advertiser is NAMED before you bank a figure.** A named brand with a quoted executive is a real case. "Brand A" or "a leading retailer" is a narrative device, and its disclaimer lives below the fold.

**The award on the wall. Six claims came out of "Meet the 2026 Meta Agency Award Winners", 21 September 2026, and all six were tiered T3.** Meta published them. Meta is a primary source. They are T3 anyway, because the entrants are agencies competing for an award, the results are agency-submitted, the judging panel is drawn from Meta and its award partners, and the winners were selected partly on those numbers. That is survivorship selection at its maximum. Two of the six:

- [[Creative Science#CR-264|CR-264]]. Boost Mobile, via Ovative Group. 11,428 WhatsApp conversations at **$16.72** each, 447,000 people reached whom no other Boost campaign had touched, **248,000 of them Spanish-speaking**. And the headline: **Spanish creative reached people at roughly half the cost of the English creative and started more than four times as many conversations.**
- [[Scaling Models#SC-170|SC-170]]. Compartés, via elk Marketing. Spend **+99%** year over year across the nine days around Black Friday, revenue **+105%**, cost per purchase **down 9.8%**, on 10 to 20% budget increments gated on returns holding.

Both name the advertiser and the agency. That is why they were banked at all. Neither is a law, because the population is the winners of a contest.

**The $299 banner. Meta's partnership-ads figure, merged yesterday into [[Creative Science#CR-058|CR-058]].** Meta states that partnership ads with creators generated **19% lower acquisition costs and 71% higher brand intent**. Real meta-analysis, stated confidence, published method. Then read the footnote. The comparison is business as usual against business as usual **plus at least 20% of cell spend** on partnership ads. This codex has been recommending roughly 10%, on Ben Heath's arithmetic. **Meta's evidence was generated at double the allocation we were briefing.** The underlying data also runs June 2021 to January 2022 and June 2023 to June 2024, so none of it is post-Andromeda. The number is true about a different test than the one we were designing.

**The window sticker. [[Auction Mechanics & Bidding#AU-094|AU-094]], T1.** Meta's own daily median advertiser rates, across all purchase-optimised ads in all verticals, 1 October to 30 November 2025. Against early October: Black Friday conversion rate **+74%** and cost per acquisition **-14%**. Cyber Monday **+43%** and **-15%**. CPMs rose the whole way through November. Cost per acquisition fell anyway, because conversion rate rose faster than price. This closes a limit [[Auction Mechanics & Bidding#AU-034|AU-034]] has carried for a year.

**Even the window sticker has conditions.** AU-094 is observational. The advertisers buying on Black Friday are a different population, at different budgets, with different offers, from the advertisers buying in early October. Meta publishes no decomposition and profits if you conclude that peak spend is cheap. Read the direction as trustworthy and the magnitude as an upper bound. One line in that footnote still does not parse: the window is October to November 2025 with "rates normalised to 1 October 2024 = 1", a base a full year before the window. The number does not go to a client without that sentence attached.

## 3. Our accounts

Here is the one that should sting.

**On 24 April 2026 we put "83% Spanish" in a Phoenix Truxx client report.**

It came from a tag that marked a call Spanish whenever the phrase "en español" appeared anywhere in the transcript. Our own voice agent says that line in its own opening, on every call it makes. The phrase appears in **748 of 912 transcripts**. In **309 of the calls tagged Spanish, the customer never speaks a word of Spanish.**

The tag was reading our own script back to us.

Measured on what the customer actually said, the real share is **58.6%**. Opt-in forms over the same window: **61.3%**. Facebook opt-ins by July 2026: **25.1%**, falling every month since April.

And 83% did not stay a number. It became a rule. `07 - Ad Targeting Brief.md` turned it into "Spanish-first, English is the 17% minority cut", and that rule shaped every concept briefed on the account for months. The working `CLAUDE.md` carried it alongside "This work is done and verified. Do not redo it." A figure with a plausible source and no stated method, carried forward until it was load-bearing. That is the footnote problem, produced in-house.

**It is still open.** A read of 2,577 CRM contacts on 18 September puts form language at **83% English, 12.8% Spanish**, against the correction note's 61.3% Spanish. Both cannot be right. Neither goes in a deck until one of them survives.

**The codex is mostly the weakest grade of evidence, and you should know the ratio.** 1,231 claims on file this morning. **92 are T1. 109 are T2. 850 are T3**, which is 69% of everything we hold, and 153 are T4. The 109 T2s are the only claims that are ours, measured on our own accounts, and every one cost an export and an afternoon. That is the honest shape of this trade.

**One live decision this changes today.** CR-264 says Spanish creative reached at half the cost. We run Spanish and English on SJR Commercial and Phoenix Truxx, and it is tempting to move budget on that sentence this week. Advertiser named, yes. Method, partly, and only the 447,000 incremental-reach figure had a test under it. Conditions, no: Click-to-WhatsApp on wireless plans is a different funnel from an instant form for a work truck, and the population is contest winners. What CR-264 earns is support for a structure we already use, separate creative per language rather than a translated twin. What it does not earn is a budget split.

## 4. The decision rule

**A published number can move money only when you can name the advertiser, name the method, and show the conditions match yours. Miss any one of the three and it becomes a test you run, never a figure you budget from.**

## 5. Quiz

Drop your answers in `_answers-inbox.md`. Lesson number plus answers is enough. Partial is fine.

**1.** AT-126 and CR-264 came off the same publisher in the same reading session. One is T1 and one is T3. Say what property of each sets its tier, and state the general rule in one sentence.

**2.** Kartik forwards a Meta post headline: "One retailer cut cost per acquisition 31% by consolidating ad sets." He wants it in the StayWell proposal going out Friday. Name the three things you check, in order, and say what the most likely outcome is.

**3.** A buyer proposes a partnership-ads test on ChiroWorks at 10% of the ad set's budget, citing Meta's 19% lower acquisition cost. Name the defect in that test design and the number that fixes it.

**4.** It is late November. SJR Commercial's CPM is up 40% week over week and Kartik wants to cut budget before it gets worse. Name the claim you cite, the number you check instead, and the result that would actually justify the cut.

**5.** "83% Spanish" passed through six vault notes and one client report before anyone caught it. Name the single property it was missing, and describe the one check that would have caught it on day one.

> [!note]- Answer key
>
> **1.** AT-126 is T1 because the claim is about what the document says, and that was directly observed: the post was read in full, the fictional example is on the page, the disclaimer is on the page. It is a fact about an artefact. CR-264 is T3 because the claim is about what a tactic does, and the evidence is agency-submitted results from a population of contest winners who were selected partly on those results. **The general rule: the tier attaches to the observation, never to the publisher.** Meta publishing something makes it T1 for what Meta says happened. It does not make the mechanism a law.
>
> **2.** In order: **(1) Is the advertiser named?** "One retailer" is unnamed, so on this channel it may be a narrative device, and AT-126 says the disclaimer for that lives at the foot of the page. Read the small print before the number. **(2) What is the comparison and the window?** 31% against what baseline, over what period, and was there a holdout. **(3) Do the conditions match StayWell?** Vertical, spend scale, funnel, optimisation event. **Most likely outcome: it does not go in the proposal.** Even if it survives all three it is a figure from a different vertical at a different scale, and a client proposal is the worst place in the world for a number you cannot defend when asked. If it does survive, it goes in as a test we are proposing, with the advertiser named inside the sentence.
>
> **3.** The test is designed below the level at which the effect was measured. Meta's 19% came from business as usual against business as usual plus **at least 20% of cell spend** on partnership ads. At 10% you are testing a condition Meta never ran, so a null result disconfirms nothing and a positive result is not the same finding. **20% of cell spend is the floor with evidence under it.** Full marks if you also flagged that the underlying data runs June 2021 to January 2022 and June 2023 to June 2024, all of it pre-Andromeda, which makes even a correctly sized test a genuine test rather than a confirmation.
>
> **4.** Cite **AU-094**. Meta's own medians show CPMs rising through November while cost per acquisition fell 14% on Black Friday and 15% on Cyber Monday, because conversion rate rose faster, +74% and +43%. **CPM is a price. Check cost per opt-in**, against SJR's own October baseline. **The cut is justified when cost per opt-in rises, not when CPM rises.** Two things worth saying out loud in the same breath: AU-094 is observational and Meta gains if we believe peak is cheap, so the direction is usable and the magnitude is an upper bound; and no account on our book has ever pushed a peak ramp, so we have no in-account precedent either way and should watch the number daily rather than predict it.
>
> **5.** It was missing **a stated method**. "83% Spanish" had a source, 912 call transcripts, and no statement of the rule that turned a call into a Spanish call. **The check: ask what rule produced the label, then run that rule against one call you already know is in English.** The phrase "en español" appears in 748 of 912 transcripts because our own agent says it in its opening line, and a single English call read end to end would have shown the tag firing on our own script. Also accept the general form: any measurement where our own behaviour can set the label is measuring us, so the labelling rule has to be independent of anything we do.
>
> **Carry forward.** Three open items from this lesson. The Phoenix Truxx language share is still contested, 61.3% Spanish against 83% English on a later read of 2,577 contacts, and neither figure is usable until one survives. Any partnership-ads brief we write is sized at 20% of cell spend or it is not testing Meta's finding. And the CR-264 Spanish result is a reason to run our own side-by-side read on SJR Commercial, where we already run both languages, rather than a reason to move a dollar this week.
