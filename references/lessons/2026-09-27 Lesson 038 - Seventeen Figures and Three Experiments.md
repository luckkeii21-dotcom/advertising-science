# Lesson 038 · Seventeen Figures and Three Experiments

Google has published a Demand Gen Drop every month for thirteen months running, September 2025 through September 2026, no month missing. Yesterday's research pass read all thirteen at source and counted every headline number printed in them.

Seventeen figures.

Three of them name an experiment.

Three of them name nothing at all.

This lesson is about the gap between the sentence and the footnote, and what happens to your judgement once you know the base rate.

## 1. The mechanism

Every coaching institute in Delhi has the same hoarding. **98% selection rate.** Huge type, one number, three words.

Somewhere on that hoarding, in type you have to stand close to read, is the part that says who got counted. Students who completed the full two-year programme. Students who cleared the institute's own internal cut-off before the real exam. Anybody who dropped out in year one is not in the denominator. Anybody the institute asked to leave is not in the denominator.

The 98% is true. It is true about a group the institute selected, after the fact, partly on the thing being measured.

The banner and the small print are two different documents written by two different people for two different reasons. The banner is written to make you walk in. The small print is written by whoever will have to defend the banner. That is why the small print is the evidence and the banner is decoration, and it is why the reading order most people use is backwards.

Google's Demand Gen series reuses four constructions. Each one changes what the sentence promises, and each one is invisible in the sentence.

**"At the same ROI."** January 2026 and September 2026. That phrase means volume rose while efficiency held, which is the arithmetic of adding inventory and spending more money. A reader hears cheaper.

**"Conversions or conversion value."** November 2025 and August 2026. A disjunction lets whichever of two metrics happened to move carry the sentence, and you never learn which one did.

**Adoption comparisons printed as results.** Five of the seventeen figures compare advertisers who adopted a feature against advertisers who did not. Advertisers who adopt features are self-selected. They are usually bigger, better staffed, already growing. None of those five has a control group.

**Footnote silence.** Three of the seventeen carry no footnote anywhere on the page: the LG Electronics figures, the Cropp figure, and the June 2026 claim that 72% of YouTube incremental conversions come from new customers.

Three footnotes out of seventeen name an experiment or an A/B test. A fourth reports an "increase in conversion lift", which implies a lift study without saying one was run. Everything else is an average over adopters.

So the prior is roughly one in six. When a platform prints a number, the base case is that no control group exists behind it.

## 2. The evidence

**[[Google Auction & Smart Bidding#GA-088|GA-088]], T1. The census.** All thirteen instalments read in full at source on 2026-09-26, every headline figure tabled against what its footnote actually says. The four constructions above, the three-in-seventeen counterfactual rate and the three silent figures all come out of that table. It sits at T1 because the claim is about what documents say, and the documents were read.

**[[Google Auction & Smart Bidding#GA-086|GA-086]], T1. One study, two numbers, seven months apart.** Read the two footnotes side by side:

- October 2025: "Google data, Global, May to June 2025, based on performance data **from** campaigns active since Q1 2024 with over 50 products in merchant feed."
- May 2026: "Google data, Global, May to June 2025, based on performance data **for** campaigns active since Q1 2024 with over 50 products in merchant feed."

Identical apart from one preposition. Same source, same geography, same two-month window, same survivorship filter of campaigns still running a year after launch with more than fifty products.

October's headline off that data: product feeds with tROAS goals deliver a **20%** conversion increase. May's headline off the same data: advertisers with large product selections see **33%**. Thirteen points apart. The stated population moved, the qualifier moved, the data stood still. Neither sentence mentions that the other exists.

Republication is not replication. Quoting both as two sources pointing the same way counts one study twice.

**[[Google Auction & Smart Bidding#GA-087|GA-087]], T1. The footnote names a different product.** In a post about Demand Gen: New Customer Only Mode improved new-customer ratio by 11.5% at 3% lower acquisition cost. The footnote: "internal studies conducted on **Search & Shopping campaigns** using best practices", Global, All Verticals A/B Test, March to May 2025. A reader banks 11.5% as Demand Gen evidence because Demand Gen is the only product on the page. The measurement happened somewhere else.

Two things are fair to Google here. The footnote describes an actual A/B test, which makes this the best-evidenced figure in the whole series. And Google printed the channel mismatch itself rather than burying it.

**[[Google Auction & Smart Bidding#GA-085|GA-085]], T1. Thirteen months of pointing the measurement outward.** Platform comparable conversion columns, conversion lift at lower spend and lower volume, target CPC, view-through conversion optimisation, Campaign Type Attribution and Uplift Experiments. Six of the thirteen drops ship one. Google's own words on the first: they "help you measure campaigns in a way that matches the default attribution used on other platforms."

That produces one cross-channel rule worth more than any figure above. Google's default columns and Meta's default columns are two different attribution models, so a side-by-side of the two default sets is not a comparison. When the question is which channel to fund, use the matched set, say which column set produced every number, and never mix the two inside one table.

**The limit, and it outranks all seventeen. [[Google Auction & Smart Bidding#GA-055|GA-055]] and [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-072|AT-072]], both T2.** A 21-day Demand Gen geo holdout on one e-commerce advertiser produced no statistically relevant new-customer lift, 85 orders against the 166 the test required, implied incremental CAC $140. Every Google figure in this lesson is first-party, measuring its own product. One holdout run by somebody else beats all of them on evidence grade, and it found nothing.

**[[Creative Science#CR-266|CR-266]], T1 for the statement only.** In November 2025 Google wrote that Pathmatics images and videos would arrive in the Google Ads asset picker so advertisers could "lift and shift top-performing creative assets from other platforms into Demand Gen." Stated as coming. Never confirmed shipped. Same reading rule, applied to a feature rather than a figure.

Worth noticing about all five of those: every one is T1, and not one of them says Demand Gen works. T1 attaches to what was observed. What was observed here is what a document says.

## 3. Our accounts

No Demand Gen runs anywhere on our book, so there is nothing to defend. Turn the four constructions around and read our own documents with them.

**The codex, counted this evening across the eleven topic files: 1,303 claims. 128 T1, 133 T2, 882 T3, 160 T4, none untiered.** T2 is the tier that means measured on our own accounts, so about 10% of everything we hold is ours. Google's controlled-footnote rate across thirteen months is three in seventeen, about 18%. Ours is lower than theirs. We do not get to enjoy their footnotes until that changes.

One small thing falls out of the same count. `bin/Sync-TeamRepo.ps1` scans every markdown file in the science folder for claim-style headings, and the Harvest Log holds exactly one because it quotes a claim header. Every codex total that script has ever published is high by one.

**All four numbers we quote as standards are adoption averages of our own work.** ChiroWorks $33.52 per opt-in, 6 July to 22 September, 143 opt-ins. StayWell $25.72, 15 June to 23 September, 144 opt-ins. SJR Commercial $2.78 blended, 21 to 27 August, 312 opt-ins. Phoenix Truxx $4.00 all-time. Each one is what we happened to achieve across what we happened to run. None of them is a target anybody agreed to. They get quoted as gates, benchmarks and pass/fail rows with no clause saying which of the two they are. That is construction three, produced in-house.

**Footnote silence, ours.** The $4.00 sits in six filed Phoenix Truxx reports as the judging benchmark and twice as a pass/fail scorecard row. No report says that its counter opens on or about 22 February 2026, which puts Meta's March click redefinition inside it. Lesson 037 has the full arithmetic. The point here is narrower: a figure quoted six times reads like six confirmations and is one reading.

**Announced is not shipped, ours.** ChiroWorks moved invisa-RED from a free consultation to a $49 introductory session on 6 September. At the last check on file, the ad and the live Instant Form both still said free consultation. That is CR-266's shape with our name on it.

**The construction we are most likely to write ourselves this Friday is "at the same ROI".** If StayWell's opt-ins rise 40% and cost per opt-in holds at $25.72, we spent more money. We did not buy better. Both numbers belong in the same sentence, and the first one on its own is a construction.

## 4. The decision rule

**A figure travels with its footnote or it does not travel. Read what produced the number before you read the number, then write that clause into the sentence that carries it. A figure with nothing behind it has no evidence, and it stays out of the deck.**

## 5. Quiz

Drop your answers in `_answers-inbox.md`. Lesson number plus answers is enough. Partial is fine.

**1.** A Google post reads: "Advertisers adopting this feature see a 30% increase in conversions or conversion value." Name the two constructions in that one sentence and say what each does to the claim.

**2.** Kartik sends you both product-feed figures, the 20% from October 2025 and the 33% from May 2026, and says two independent studies pointing the same way is enough to brief a feed build. Answer him in three sentences and say what the pair is actually worth.

**3.** You are writing the StayWell weekly. Opt-ins went from 41 to 58 week over week and cost per opt-in held at $25.70. Write the headline sentence you would file, and name the construction you just refused to use.

**4.** GA-087's footnote names Search and Shopping campaigns inside a post about Demand Gen, and this lesson still calls it the best-evidenced figure in the series. Explain how both of those are true at once.

**5.** A Google rep wants a Demand Gen budget on SJR Commercial and leads with the census's strongest number, the October 2025 A/B test at 11.5% better new-customer ratio. Name the one claim on our own file that outranks it, say what it found, and name what you ask the rep to run instead.

> [!note]- Answer key
>
> **1.** **"Adopting"** makes it an adoption comparison. The population is advertisers who chose the feature, who are self-selected, usually larger and better resourced, and there is no control group, so the sentence reports a correlation while reading like a result. **"Conversions or conversion value"** is a disjunction. Whichever of the two metrics moved 30% carries the sentence, and a reader optimising for conversion count cannot tell whether the 30% was value, which moves on average order size and not on the feature at all. Full marks if you added that the first defect is invisible without the footnote and the second is sitting in plain sight in the sentence.
>
> **2.** Something like: "They are one study, not two. Both footnotes read Google data, Global, May to June 2025, campaigns active since Q1 2024 with over 50 products, identical apart from one preposition, so the 33% is the 20% restated seven months later with the qualifier changed from tROAS goals to large product selections. It is one uncontrolled adopter average carrying a survivorship filter printed in its own footnote, worth one data point, and it sizes a test rather than a build." What the pair is worth: it is evidence that Google wants feeds adopted. It is not evidence that feeds caused the lift, because every campaign that adopted feeds and then got switched off is excluded by the filter's own wording.
>
> **3.** Something like: **"Opt-ins rose from 41 to 58, up 41%, at $25.70 each against $25.72 the week before. The extra 17 came from more spend at the same price, so the volume is real and the buying did not get cheaper."** The construction refused is **"at the same ROI"**. Filing "opt-ins up 41%" on its own, with the flat cost per opt-in a table lower or missing, lets the client read efficiency where the only thing that happened is more money at the same price. Full marks for naming the spend figure as the thing that makes the sentence honest.
>
> **4.** Two different questions. **Evidence grade** asks what generated the number, and this footnote names an A/B test run March to May 2025, which puts it above the five adoption averages and the three figures with no footnote at all, so it is the strongest thing in the census. **Provenance** asks whether the number is about the product on the page, and it is not, because the test ran on Search and Shopping while the post is about Demand Gen. A well-run measurement of the wrong product is still a well-run measurement. It is usable evidence about New Customer Only Mode on Search and Shopping, and it is not usable evidence about Demand Gen. Full marks if you said the two properties get graded separately, and that Google disclosing the mismatch itself counts in the footnote's favour.
>
> **5.** The claim is **GA-055, paired with AT-072, both T2**: a 21-day Demand Gen geo holdout produced no statistically relevant new-customer lift, 85 orders against the 166 the test required, and an implied incremental CAC of $140, while returning-customer orders rose 6.8% at $39 each. It outranks the 11.5% on two grounds. It is a holdout with a control rather than a first-party A/B test, and it measures Demand Gen rather than Search and Shopping. What you ask for: the **Uplift Experiment** and **Campaign Type Attribution** Google shipped in its own May 2026 drop, per GA-083, because those are counterfactual instruments on the actual product and they turn the conversation into a measurement. Full marks if you also said that any number the rep brings in the meantime gets read on the matched platform-comparable column set and named in the sentence, per GA-085, and that SJR has no Google account open at all right now, so the first question is what the test would run on.
