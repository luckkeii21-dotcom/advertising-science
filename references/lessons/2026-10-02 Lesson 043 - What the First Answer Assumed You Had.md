# Lesson 043 · What the First Answer Assumed You Had

🎬 **Watch first (3m 25s):** [[video/2026-10-02-lesson-043.mp4|Lesson 043 video]]

On 24 August, Jon Loomer said that when an upper-funnel goal is buying junk inventory, the fix is to abandon the goal rather than prune the placement. He named the exposures by hand: Audience Network on link clicks and landing-page views, ads on Facebook Reels on reach.

On 30 September, the same man prescribed those goals and pruned exactly those two placements.

Five weeks apart. One source. And he quotes the mechanism in the same words both times: with no conversion to optimise for, "Meta will exploit weaknesses to find the cheapest and likely lowest quality optimized actions."

There are two lazy readings available. One is that he contradicted himself, so stop listening to him. The other is that the new post supersedes the old one, so overwrite your notes. Both throw away the most useful thing on the table.

## 1. The mechanism

Advice has two halves and only one of them gets said out loud.

The half that gets said is **the move**. Abandon the goal. Prune the placement. Raise the budget 20%.

The half that stays silent is **what your situation has to contain for that move to exist**. Nobody writes this part down, because to the person giving the advice it is too obvious to mention.

August's move was abandon the goal. That silently assumes you have another goal to go to, and a conversion sitting at the end of it.

September's situation is an ad set whose whole job is to send traffic to a blog post. No purchase. No form. No call. There is nothing at the end of it to optimise for. So abandoning the goal is not a worse option than pruning. It is not an option at all.

When the first move is unavailable, the fix drops to the next one down. Guard rails. Keep the goal, prune the placement, cap the frequency, narrow the audience to people who already follow you.

Think about a mechanic who tells you never to drive on a space-saver spare at highway speed, just get the tyre fixed. Then another customer rings him at 11pm on a Sunday, 40 miles from home, blown tyre, nothing open for nine hours. He tells them to fit the spare and drive back at 40. His view on spares has not moved a millimetre. The first customer had a tyre shop. The second one did not.

The silent half is the half you have to reconstruct yourself. So when one source hands you two answers, the question is never *which one is right*. It is **what did the first one assume I had, and do I have it**.

Here the assumption collapses to a single word: conversion. If the campaign has one, August stands. If it has none, September is for you.

And notice what never moved in any of this. Not the mechanism. He restates it verbatim. The disagreement was never about how delivery behaves.

## 2. The evidence

Four claims carry this, and the tier split inside one of them is worth more than the rest.

**[[Meta Delivery & Andromeda#MD-166|MD-166]], T1.** Meta's own help article. A Facebook Page gets **2 organic link posts a month** on the free tier, and Meta sells the increase through Meta One. Advanced at $49.99 buys 8, Expert at $149.99 buys 20, Max at $499.99 is unlimited. The limit resets on the 1st, unused slots do not carry forward, and **links in ads are exempt by name**.

**That claim is T1 and half of what circulates from it is not, which is the point to take.** The Facebook numbers are Meta's own table. The per-tier *Instagram* splits that get quoted alongside them come from an operator, Meta's article covers Facebook Pages only, and this codex has now caught a third unverified version of them. Meta's own two surfaces also disagree on the price, $149.99 on the help article against $149.00 on the announcement. **A tier belongs to the sentence you are quoting, not to the document you found it in.** Read the article and you would call all of it T1. Check it and two-thirds of the Instagram half is somebody's paraphrase.

Meta's first sentence in that article is also the caveat: "Limits on posts and comments with links may not apply to all Pages." Meta does not say which. So nobody can state from the article whether a given client Page is capped. You have to open the Page.

**[[Scaling Models#SC-149|SC-149]], T3.** The claim that decides the whole argument, and its governing sentence is the one to memorise: "The performance goal matters a whole lot when assessing whether there's a problem to be solved." Under conversion optimisation, delivery self-corrects and manual placement pruning solves nothing, because Meta knows it cannot get conversions from junk. Under an upper-funnel goal the logic inverts, because a junk impression satisfies a link click perfectly well.

**[[Scaling Models#SC-173|SC-173]], T3, and it is thin on purpose.** One operator, one ten-minute episode, no spend, no account, no before-and-after. He flags it as an exception to his own standing advice **four times in ten minutes**, including "in most cases, I'd give you a side eye for talking about doing this in the first place." His own verdict on whether it works: "We'll see. But my hunch is that it would be." One permanent ad set replaces the organic publishing routine, up to 50 ads one per post, followers-only custom audience, a frequency cap, **$5 a day set to match the $149 subscription**. T3 is the correct tier and the four disclaimers are why.

**[[Meta Delivery & Andromeda#MD-150|MD-150]] and [[Meta Delivery & Andromeda#MD-151|MD-151]], T1 for the surfaces.** Meta is withdrawing ad-set placement exclusion, and the reason September's move exists at all is that the withdrawal is bottom-of-funnel only. On the goals this design uses, the off switch survives. Without that, the whole design would be unbuildable.

**[[Scaling Models#SC-130|SC-130]], T3.** The same structural shape doing a different job: small fixed budget, upper-funnel objective, large creative pool, one frequency cap, one fixed audience. SC-130 is a retargeting presence layer. SC-173 is an organic-distribution replacement. Recognising a known structure in a new costume is most of what reading research is.

## 3. Our accounts

Five things, in order of how much they matter to us.

**One: the cap does not touch a single thing we run.** Links in ads are exempt by name. MD-166 changes what a client Page can post for free. It changes nothing about what we buy, what it costs, or how it delivers.

**Two: across all four live accounts, 21 to 27 September, there was no upper-funnel goal anywhere.** Twenty-six ad sets spent money: SJR 4, Phoenix 8, ChiroWorks 7, StayWell 7.

Be precise about how much of that the export actually proves. **Fifteen of the 26 carry an explicit `actions:lead` or `actions:leadgen.other` result indicator**, which is direct evidence. Four ChiroWorks ad sets produced no result in the window, so Meta leaves the indicator blank and the export says nothing about their goal. StayWell's export does not include the column at all.

For those eleven the evidence is the name, and one of them is better than an inference. **`(NEW) All ads | Collinsville | Open targeting | Relaunch 21st Sep 2026` appears twice in ChiroWorks:** once at $225.50 reporting `actions:leadgen.other`, once at $38.28 reporting nothing. Same name, so it is a copy of a lead-optimised ad set that happened to produce no result that week. The other three ChiroWorks rows are named for a body part or a doctor, and all seven StayWell rows are named for a creative format, a body part, a condition or a doctor against open targeting. **Not one of the 26 names reach, traffic, video views or engagement.**

So: proven on 15, near-certain on 1, inferred from naming on 10, contradicted on none. Under SC-149 that means **the correct action on placements across our entire live book is nothing**, and the question SC-173 raises is about a goal we do not currently run on any client.

**Three: the money comparison is computable from our own numbers, and there is a detail in Meta's table nobody has stated.**

| Meta One tier | Link posts a month | Extra over free | Price | Price per extra slot |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Free | 2 | 0 | $0 | n/a |
| Essential | 2 | **0** | $14.99 | **buys nothing** |
| Advanced | 8 | 6 | $49.99 | **$8.33** |
| Expert | 20 | 18 | $149.99 | **$8.33** |
| Max | Unlimited | Unlimited | $499.99 | n/a |

Meta priced both paid tiers at **the same $8.33 per extra link post, to the cent**. And Essential, at $14.99 a month, buys zero extra link slots.

So the question is no longer the fuzzy one about subscriptions against ads. It is: **what does $8.33 buy on our own book?** Here is what the four accounts actually charged over 21 to 27 September, computed from the raw ad-level exports behind the filed client reports.

| Account | Spend | Impressions | Link clicks | CPM | Cost per link click | $150 buys | $8.33 buys |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SJR Commercial | $769.98 | 27,049 | 775 | $28.47 | $0.99 | 151 clicks | **8.4 clicks** |
| Phoenix Truxx | $1,391.30 | 49,105 | 835 | $28.33 | $1.67 | 90 clicks | **5.0 clicks** |
| ChiroWorks | $527.33 | 23,948 | 238 | $22.02 | $2.22 | 68 clicks | **3.8 clicks** |
| StayWell | $441.44 | 10,116 | 165 | $43.64 | $2.68 | 56 clicks | **3.1 clicks** |
| **All four** | **$3,130.05** | **110,218** | **2,013** | **$28.40** | **$1.55** | **96 clicks** | **5.4 clicks** |

**Each extra organic link slot has to beat 3 to 8 guaranteed clicks.** That is the comparison, and it is decidable rather than philosophical. The one sentence in the whole episode that holds up without any data behind it is the one that explains why: "Meta has devalued link sharing over the years by throttling reach for businesses. With ads, I can guarantee a certain amount of delivery."

Three guards on that table, and the first one strengthens the ads side rather than weakening it.

- Those are **conversion-optimised prices paid for cold open targeting in a local geo**. An ad set optimised for link clicks against a Page's own followers normally prices lower per click. So 3 to 8 is a floor, not a quote.
- A bought click from open targeting is a stranger. An organic link post reaches people who chose to follow. Loomer's design answers that by targeting followers only, which makes the size of that follower pool the deciding input. **We can size it for exactly one client out of five.** Phoenix Truxx's Facebook Page carries **35,600 followers**. For the other four the vault holds Instagram counts or nothing, and MD-166 is Facebook Pages only, so an Instagram number cannot stand in.
- An account CPM is an average over ad sets that are nothing like each other. Inside SJR in that one week, ad-set CPM ran from **$14.31** on Spanish open targeting to **$49.99** on the English Vans Program. A 3.5x spread in one account in seven days. "$150 buys 5,269 impressions" is true of the account and false of every ad set in it.

**Four: a correction to our own log, and it is ours.** Yesterday's research entry said we are the population for this test, because any capped client Page that posts links more than twice a month can answer it for $5 a day. We are the population for the **mechanism**. We are not the population for the **use case**. SC-173's design is one ad per blog post or podcast episode, up to 50 of them. None of our five clients publishes either. Two truck dealerships and three clinics do not have a content library to distribute, and nobody has checked whether any of their Pages posts a link even twice a month.

And the design does not survive its own budget at our prices. $150 a month at our blended $28.40 CPM is 5,282 impressions. Spread across up to 50 ads that is **106 impressions per ad per month**, or about 2 clicks per post. SC-130 survives at that scale because it is deliberately presence rather than performance and says so. SC-173 has a traffic goal, and traffic goals get judged on clicks.

**Phoenix is the one client we can run that arithmetic against properly, and it is the clearest number in the lesson.** Its Facebook Page has 35,600 followers. At Phoenix's own CPM of $28.33, showing one impression to each of them costs **$1,009**. The $150 the design names reaches about **15% of the pool, once, in a month**. So the frequency cap that holds the design together is doing nothing at that budget, because the budget cannot get round the audience even one time. The cap only starts to matter somewhere north of $1,000 a month, which is nearly seven times the subscription it was designed to replace.

## 4. The decision rule

**When one source gives you two answers, do not pick one. Find what the first answer assumed you had, then check whether you have it.**

If you still have it, the first answer stands and the second one is for somebody else. If you do not have it, the reversal was written for you.

The version of this for client work: before you carry an operator's structure into an account, price the thing it is competing with. Here the competition is $8.33 a slot, which turns a subscription argument into a cost-per-click argument we can already settle with our own exports.

> **1.** What changed is the availability of the first remedy, not the mechanism. August's "abandon the goal" assumes a bottom-funnel conversion to fall back to; a campaign driving traffic to a blog post has none, so pruning is the only move left. What did not change is the mechanism, which he states in the same words both times: with no conversion to optimise for, Meta finds the cheapest and lowest-quality optimised actions. Full credit for naming the availability of the remedy as the variable. Extra credit for adding that SC-149's governing condition, the performance goal, is also unchanged, which is why nothing is refuted.
>
> **2.** The per-tier Instagram splits, and the exact price. The Instagram figures come from an operator and Meta's article covers Facebook Pages only, publishing one number per tier with no Instagram split, so they are unverified against any Meta surface and have now been stated three different ways. The price appears as $149.99 on the help article and $149.00 on the Meta for Business announcement, which is the third time two Meta surfaces have disagreed on the same fact. Full credit for either plus the reason. Extra credit for also naming the comment loophole, where the common reading is right for a standalone comment and wrong for extra links inside the comments of a post that already carries one, which Meta exempts by name. Further credit for stating the general rule: a tier belongs to the sentence, not the document.
>
> **3.** Four steps. First, open the Page and confirm it is actually capped, because Meta's own first sentence says the limits may not apply to all Pages. Second, count how many link posts a month he genuinely wants to publish; under 3 and the free tier already covers him and the whole question is dead. Third, price the subscription per marginal slot: Advanced is 6 extra at $8.33 each, Expert is 18 extra at $8.33 each, and Essential at $14.99 buys zero extra, so Essential is never the answer. Fourth, price the alternative at his own cost per link click and ask whether $8.33 of ads beats one throttled organic post; on our book $8.33 buys 3 to 8 clicks, and his own account's number is the one that matters. The missing input is the size of his Facebook Page follower pool, because the design targets followers only and no claim here can tell you how many people that is or what they cost to reach. Full credit for the cap check first and the per-slot price. Extra credit for noticing $5 a day is $150 against $149.99, so he is choosing between two near-identical bills and the only question is which one delivers.
>
> **4.** Something close to: all seven funded ad sets in ChiroWorks over 21 to 27 September were lead-optimised, and under SC-149 placement pruning on conversion delivery solves nothing, so stripping Audience Network only matters once we deliberately add an upper-funnel goal. Full credit for splitting the request in two. Extra credit for naming the account-level Placement Controls setting as the surviving off switch and flagging that it is account-wide, so it cannot differ between two clients sharing an account.

Before the first $5: the Page has to be genuinely capped; the client has to publish links more often than twice a month, which no client of ours currently does; and somebody has to write down in advance which number settles it, because a traffic ad set judged on opt-ins fails automatically. Full credit for naming the absent use case. Extra credit for the budget arithmetic: at our blended $28.40 CPM, $150 a month over up to 50 ads is 106 impressions per ad, roughly 2 clicks per post, which is not a test of anything, so the design needs either far fewer ads or far more money before it can produce a readable answer.
