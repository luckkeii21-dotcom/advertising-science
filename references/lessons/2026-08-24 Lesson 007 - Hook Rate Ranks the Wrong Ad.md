# Lesson 007 · Hook Rate Ranks the Wrong Ad

🎬 **Lesson video (2m 38s, silent, watch anywhere):** [[video/2026-08-24-lesson-007.mp4]]

**If you only answer two questions, answer Q1 and Q4.** Six lessons have gone out and the inbox has come back empty six times. Two answers is a real submission.

This is the first lesson where our own accounts already contain the finding, in writing, before the codex banked it. That makes it the most important one so far.

## 1. The mechanism

Hook rate is 3-second video plays divided by impressions. Meta does not give it to you. You build it as a custom metric, which is exactly why it feels like insider knowledge when you have it on screen.

It measures one thing: how many people did not scroll past in the first three seconds.

Here is the trap. That number feels like a quality score for the ad, because a stopped thumb feels like the hard part. So the column gets used as a ranking. Best hook rate wins, worst hook rate gets cut, and the next batch gets built to beat the top hook.

Think about a shop with a brilliant window display. People stop. They come in. Footfall triples. Then you look at the till and takings are flat, because the window promised something the shop does not sell. The window is doing its job perfectly. Ranking your shops by footfall would put that one first.

Hook rate is footfall. It is a real measurement of a real thing, and it is upstream of every decision that actually costs money. A high hook rate with a bad cost per result is not a good ad. It is a good hook bolted to a weak body, and the metric cannot tell you which of those two you are looking at.

The correlation is real. Better hooks do, on average, come with better results. It is just loose enough that ranking by it picks the wrong ad often enough to matter.

## 2. The evidence

- **CR-176, T2, banked today. This is the strongest shown creative evidence in the codex, and it cuts against a column we use.** Ben Heath builds hook rate live and reads two rows off the same account. A **15.55% hook rate delivered £11.02 per booked call on 33 conversions.** A **27% hook rate cost over £30 per booked call on 64 conversions.** Both have real sample sizes. The spread inside that one account runs about 3.5x, from 7-9% at the bottom to just under 27% at the top, and the cheapest ad sat in the middle.
- **The definition matters and ours already matches his.** 3-second plays over impressions. Our Phoenix reports carry the method note in the file: "Hook = 3-sec views / impr, hold = ThruPlay / impr." Do not compare a hook rate against anyone who divides by reach or by video views.
- **The diagnostic that survives, and it is genuinely useful.** High hook rate plus bad cost per result reads as a good hook on a weak body. That is a production instruction, not a kill signal.
- **Separate that from the prescription, because the tiers differ.** The diagnostic is T2 with rows on screen. Heath's fix, splicing the best hook onto the best-converting body, is **T3 with nothing shown**. He says "we've done that so many times, so often that ad is a really high performer" and never shows a spliced ad's before and after. That makes him the second operator this month to claim a re-cut revival and show nothing, after Fraser Cottrell.
- **CR-177, T3, contested, banked today.** The fatigue metric operators actually use is frequency above 2.0, at ad level, over 14 days, on the new-audience segment only. Zero data shown anywhere. The source undercuts his own threshold in the same breath: reported frequency is a mean over a right-skewed distribution Meta stopped exposing six years ago. His prescription is the good part and it agrees with law 1: **fix frequency in the creative, never in the ad set.**
- **Folklore guard, still standing.** "80% of people never make it past the hook" and its 90% cousin have no retention curve behind them anywhere. The nearest origin is an Ogilvy print-headline observation about magazine readership, restated decades later as a video statistic. CR-176 is the first account data suggesting the hook is not where the money is decided.

## 3. Our accounts

We wrote this finding down ourselves five weeks before the codex banked it.

**Phoenix Truxx is the cleanest case in the vault.** The `work truck = business card` English video owns the best hook in the account and it is the most expensive lead source we run.

| Week | Ad | Hook | Spend | Leads | CPL |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jul 10-16 | work truck video | 32.0% | $664.62 | 63 | $10.55 |
| Jul 10-16 | Box Truck static | none | $24.88 | 12 | **$2.07** |
| Jul 17-23 | work truck video | **33.7%** | $664.30 | 37 | $17.95 |
| Jul 17-23 | Box Truck v2 static | none | $152.86 | 36 | **$4.25** |

Read the video row across the two weeks. **The hook went up, 32.0% to 33.7%, and the cost per lead went up 70%.** Attention improved and the account got worse. The Jul 23 report already called it: "the EN video still owns the best hook in the account at 33.7%, but hook does not equal cheap leads: it is an attention engine at $17.95, not a lead engine."

Now look at the column. **The statics beating it 4 to 5 times over have no hook rate at all**, because Meta only reports 3-second plays on video. The metric cannot even see the ads that are winning. A leaderboard sorted by hook rate would have shown a top-to-bottom ranking of our worst-value creative.

**Chiropraise is the extreme version.** The Real Patient Testimonial video, same ad, one month apart:

- Jun 21-27: hook **32.9%** (694 3-second plays, 134 ThruPlays), 8 leads at **$12.04**
- Jul 17-23: hook **37.3%** (628 / 1,684), 1 lead at **$71.70**

The hook rose 4.4 points. The cost per lead went up roughly sixfold. Nothing in the attention column moved in the direction the money moved.

**SJR is where you learn the honest version, because it cuts both ways.** In the week of Jun 9-16, the higher hook really was the cheaper ad: UGC Inspirational at 43.2% hook and $1.32 CPL, against Caesar at 29.8% hook and $3.20. That is the correlation being real, and you should not pretend it is not.

Then watch Caesar decay over three weeks: hook **29.8% → 24.9% → 26.2%**, CPL **$3.20 → $6.49 → $8.70**. The hook fell about 12%. The cost nearly tripled. Same ad, same account. If you were watching the hook column for a fatigue signal, you would have seen a wobble and called it fine while the lead cost ran away from you.

Note what Caesar's hold rate did in that same stretch, 41.3% down to 8.2%. The hold moved far harder than the hook did. That is a hint, not a law, and we have never tested it.

## 4. The decision rule

**Rank creative by cost per result at ad-set level. Use hook rate only as an anomaly detector: a high hook with a bad cost per result means fix the body, and a low hook with a good cost per result means leave it alone and go find out why it works.**

Never put a hook rate in a client report as evidence an ad is good. It is a diagnostic for us, not a result for them.

## 5. Quiz

Drop answers in `lessons/_answers-inbox.md`. Lesson number plus your answers. Partial is fine and still gets graded.

1. Write the formula for hook rate, and name the one reason two people's hook rates are often not comparable.
2. In the Heath account, which ad was cheaper per booked call, the 15.55% hook or the 27% hook, and roughly by how much?
3. CR-176 contains a T2 diagnostic and a T3 prescription. Name both, and say what you are allowed to do with each.
4. **Applied.** You open Phoenix Truxx and sort the ad leaderboard by hook rate, descending. Name two specific ways that ranking misleads you, using the Jul 17-23 numbers.
5. **Applied.** A new ChiroWorks video comes back with a 41% hook rate, the highest we have ever recorded on that account, and a $58 cost per lead against a $30 target. What is your read, what do you change, and what do you explicitly not change?

> [!note]- Answer key
> **1.** 3-second video plays divided by impressions. Meta does not provide it, so everyone builds their own, and the common break is a different denominator (reach or video views instead of impressions). Also accept: statics have no hook rate at all, so any list mixing formats is incomplete.
> **2.** The 15.55% hook was cheaper, at £11.02 against over £30. Roughly a third of the cost, on the ad with the worse hook. Accept "about 3x cheaper".
> **3.** T2 diagnostic: high hook plus bad cost per result means a good hook on a weak body, shown on screen with conversion counts, so you may act on it. T3 prescription: splice the winning hook onto the best-converting body, asserted with no before-and-after ever shown, so you may test it and must call it a bet. Full marks for naming it as the second undemonstrated re-cut revival claim this month.
> **4.** First, the top of that list is the $17.95 video and the bottom is empty, because the four statics running at $4.25 to $6.42 have no hook rate to sort by. The ranking hides the winners entirely. Second, the video's hook went UP week over week, 32.0% to 33.7%, while its CPL went up 70%, so the column moved in the opposite direction to the money and would have read as an improvement. Credit anyone who adds that the Spanish pair at 21.6% and 25.9% would rank last while costing about the same as the top-ranked ad.
> **5.** Read: a good hook bolted to a weak body, which is the CR-176 diagnostic exactly. Change the body, the offer clarity and the CTA, and check the landing or form step, since the drop-off is downstream of the first three seconds. Do not change the hook, and do not kill the ad on the CPL alone until you have checked ad-set level rather than the ad's own row (Lesson 003) and confirmed the ad set is not simply starved, which is what produced ChiroWorks' $86 CPL on FTN. Also acceptable and better: check the hold rate, since Caesar's hold moved far harder than its hook when it decayed.
