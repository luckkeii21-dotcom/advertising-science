# Lesson 059 - The Formula Only Needs One Number

**Date:** 2026-10-24
**Topic:** Learning & Signal
**Source:** harvest-driven. Meta for Business News published an ad-set count formula on 21 October 2026 and today's research run banked it as LS-089.
**Video:** none. Saturday is not a video day.

---

## 1. The mechanism

Meta published a line of arithmetic this week. It decides how many ad sets your budget can carry.

> Total weekly budget ÷ (cost per result × 50) = the most ad sets your budget can support

Then Meta added the part that makes it a finding rather than a calculator. Most accounts are running more ad sets than that.

Here is the piece Meta did not say out loud. Do the algebra and the formula collapses.

Weekly budget divided by cost per result is weekly results. That is the definition of cost per result, read in the other direction. So the whole expression is:

> **weekly results ÷ 50**

The budget cancels. The cost per result cancels. Two of the three inputs do nothing. What is left is this: count the conversions your account produced last week, divide by fifty, and that is the most ad sets you can run with every one of them clearing the learning phase.

The analogy that actually maps. You have a stack of fifty-kilo cement bags to move and a van that holds one bag per trip. It does not matter what the cement cost or what you paid for the van. The only number that decides how many trips you make is how many bags you have. Budget and cost per result are the price of the cement. The count is the cement.

This is why the direction of use matters, and the direction is the whole argument underneath the claim. Run it **forwards** from the conversions you already produce and it caps your ad set count. That is free, and it is true on every account. Run it **backwards** from fifty events per ad set to the budget you would need, and you have invented a spend requirement out of a status label.

## 2. The evidence

Four claims, and the tiers are doing real work here.

- **LS-089, T1 for the sentence.** The formula itself, published by Meta on 21 October 2026. T1 because the sentence is the platform's own. The arithmetic proves nothing by itself, because arithmetic never does. What it buys us is that Meta has now put its name on the derivation.
- **LS-001, T1 and contested.** The fifty-results-in-seven-days exit condition the formula sits on. It gained a first-party performance number this week: ad sets below roughly fifty conversions a week "ran about 28% more expensive per purchase". The footnote behind that 28% is the words "in our analysis". No population, no window, no sample, no control. **Carry the direction, never the digits.** That number does not go in a client deck and it does not go in a forecast.
- **LS-060, T3.** Permanent limited learning is the normal state on a high-ticket account. Matt Shiver works it out on air: at a $500 cost per call, fifty conversions a week is $25,000 a week, so he is always in limited learning and does not treat it as a fault.
- **LS-035, T3 and contested.** Charley T caps a CBO at three ad sets, conditional on the budget clearing learning for all three. Nick Theriot runs the opposite structure and reports it working.

Theriot is the number to hold onto, because he is the live counterexample and his own figures are on screen. $800 a day at a $35 cost per purchase. That is $5,600 a week and 160 purchases, so the formula allows 3.2 ad sets. He runs ten to twenty live. Over the window he shows, that account moved from a $98 cost per purchase to $45.

So the formula is a constraint on signal per ad set. It is not a prediction that breaking it makes the account worse. Those are two different claims and only the first one is published.

## 3. Our accounts

Run it forwards on our own book, off filed weekly reports. The division is weekly opt-ins over fifty.

| Account | Filed week | Spend | Opt-ins | Cost per opt-in | Ad sets the formula allows |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SJR Commercial | Oct 16-22 | $779.67 | 95 | $8.21 | **1.90** |
| Phoenix Truxx | Oct 9-15 | $1,336.39 | 95 | $14.07 | **1.90** |
| StayWell | Oct 16-22 | $537.61 | 18 | $29.87 | **0.36** |
| ChiroWorks | Sep 30-Oct 6, last live week | $463.28 | 17 | $27.25 | **0.34** |

Four accounts and not one of them is allowed two ad sets. The two truck accounts land on 1.90 each, which is a coincidence of the arithmetic and not a pattern. StayWell and ChiroWorks are allowed roughly a third of one ad set, which means no structure exists that clears the threshold on those accounts at that spend.

This is exactly what LS-060 predicted. Our whole book is high-ticket lead gen, and the threshold was always out of reach.

What I cannot give you today is the right-hand side of the comparison. **The live ad set count per account is not verified in this window.** SJR runs a van lane and a dump lane. Phoenix runs the statics CBO plus the Core NJ+NY ring campaign launched 9 October. StayWell runs Binder and Schneider. Those are campaign names off the filed reports, not a counted list of live ad sets, and the scoreboard has carried "count the live ad sets" as an open item since lesson 036. One export per account closes it and turns this table from a published sentence into an audit instrument.

Read honestly, the table already tells you the answer will be "more than allowed" everywhere. It also tells you that consolidating down to a single ad set would still leave StayWell and ChiroWorks under the threshold. Consolidation cannot buy them the label. Nothing can at that spend.

## 4. The decision rule

**Count last week's conversions, divide by fifty, and never run more ad sets than that number. When the number comes out below one, stop managing toward the learning phase at all and justify every split on what you learn from it instead.**

A second rule, from LS-090, because it governs what you do once the count tells you you are wrong: **a freeze protects a brief you chose, never a brief you inherited.** If three things need rebriefing, change all three at once and take one learning phase instead of three.

**5.** StayWell's formula result is 0.36 ad sets and it currently runs at least two campaigns. > [!note]- Answer key
> **1.** Weekly budget and cost per result cancel, because weekly budget divided by cost per result *is* weekly results. The formula reduces to weekly results ÷ 50. It is asking for one number: how many conversions the account produced last week.
>
> **2.** Forwards it starts from conversions you already have and returns a ceiling on ad set count, which is a free structural constraint and true on any account. Backwards it starts from the 50-event threshold and returns a budget you must go and find, which converts a status label into a spend demand. The backwards version is **LS-060**, the spend requirement in disguise. Blue Sense Digital names that back-propagation as one of four common mistakes below $50k a month, which means the contest at LS-001 is now practitioner against Meta's own published arithmetic.
>
> **3.** Its entire footnote is the words "in our analysis". No population, no window, no sample, no spend band, no control. On top of that, sub-50 against over-50 is also small accounts against large accounts, which is a selection defect, so the comparison is not clean even in principle. What we keep is the **direction**: being below the threshold is more expensive, not cheaper. We never quote the 28%.
>
> **4.** $2,670 ÷ ($14.07 × 50) = $2,670 ÷ $703.50 = **3.80 ad sets**. The check: cost per opt-in will not hold at $14.07 while the budget doubles. The formula's own input is the thing the budget change breaks, so the honest answer is to re-run the division after the account settles at the new spend rather than to promise the structure in advance. Credit for also naming the live ad set count as unverified.
>
> **5.** It does **not** achieve the stated goal. StayWell's 18 weekly opt-ins give 0.36, so even one consolidated ad set sits at roughly a third of the threshold and stays in limited learning. Consolidation cannot buy the label on that account at that spend, and per LS-060 the label is not a fault there anyway. Legitimate reasons to consolidate anyway: fewer ad sets means less auction overlap between two campaigns aimed at the same Michigan pool, and it concentrates the read so one lane's performance is legible instead of split. Those are real reasons. "Exiting limited learning" is not one.
