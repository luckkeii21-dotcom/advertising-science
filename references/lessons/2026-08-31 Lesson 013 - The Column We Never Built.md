# Lesson 013 · The Column We Never Built

> Lesson 003 said an ad's own row is not the verdict. Lesson 007 said hook rate ranks the wrong ad. Lesson 011 said a starved row never bought a chance. This one is about the row you trust most, cost per opt-in, and the column that has to sit next to it before that number means anything.

**If you only answer two questions, answer Q2 and Q5.** Q2 is live on StayWell this week. Q5 is the guard that stops this lesson turning into a reason to stop cutting ads.

## 1. The mechanism

A shop hires two people to bring customers in off the street. One hands out a voucher for a free coffee and brings in 40 people an hour. The other explains what the shop actually sells and brings in 9. Rank them on cost per person through the door and the voucher wins four to one.

Nobody at that shop counts what those people bought. So nobody ever learns that 38 of the 40 came for the coffee.

That is what a cost per opt-in column does on its own. It measures how cheaply an ad produces the event you told the platform to buy. It says nothing about what arrives at the other end.

Charley T's fix is to plot every ad on two axes instead of one: cost per sale, and profit per sale, both against the account average. Four quadrants come out of it.

- **Scalers.** Cheaper than average, more profitable than average. Fund these.
- **Keep or fix.** Expensive, and more profitable than average. Worth repairing, because you can nearly always drive a cost down and you can almost never raise the value of what an ad brings in.
- **Fake wins.** Cheaper than average, less profitable than average. This is the quadrant that matters.
- **Liabilities.** Expensive and unprofitable. These go first.

The fake wins are the point. They are exactly the ads a percentage scorecard promotes. His arithmetic: a 3x return at a $20 cost per sale implies a $60 order and $40 of profit. A 2x return at a $100 cost per sale implies a $200 order and $100 of profit. The second ad has the worse ratio, the worse cost, and two and a half times the profit. His line: "when you're optimizing for ROAS, you're optimizing for the ad that is closest to converting, not the ad that makes you the most money."

Read the definition before you use it. He defines profit per transaction as order value minus cost per acquisition. **It nets out ad cost only. It does not deduct cost of goods.** That is contribution after media. Treat it as accounting profit and you overstate by the whole cost line.

## 2. The evidence

**MM-190, T3, active, banked 2026-08-30.** The four-quadrant ranking, stated identically in three episodes across five weeks. Vendor tool, no exported account data, and the confidence percentages the tool prints are unverifiable. The arithmetic behind it was recomputed this pass and holds.

**SC-152, T3, active, banked 2026-08-30.** Same operator, and it does two things. It gives the first entry trigger in the codex for when cleanup beats launching: **more than 10 ads in one campaign, or more than three campaigns in one account.** Below that, an account is not disordered enough for pruning to be the best move available. And it kills his own earlier rule on camera. In January he says to keep removing the worst-profit ad with no stopping condition. In February he prunes a live 46-ad account down to 8, which is 38 ads cut, and **cost per sale falls from $314 to $263 while profit per sale falls from $206 to $131.** Cost improved 16%. Profit fell 36%. He walks the curve at 10%, 25% and 30% and finds the account breaking between 25 and 30. The continuous version is retracted by his own demonstration.

**Law 12, and this is where it gets interesting, because it looks like a flat contradiction.** In a shown 89-ad campaign the winners and losers consumed almost the same budget, $48,594 against $48,800, and the losers ran a $230 cost per acquisition at **negative $0.98** profit per transaction (MD-046, T2). That campaign was cut from 89 ads to 4, nothing new was launched for six weeks, and spend went up 10x in 90 days (SC-050, T2).

Cutting 85 of 89 is a **95.5%** cut. Charley T says the account breaks between 25% and 30%. Both are T2 or shown. Both are real.

They reconcile, and the reconciliation is the whole lesson. **Depth is not the variable. What you cut is the variable.** MD-046's losers were measured at negative contribution, individually, with the second column present. Charley T's 38 were ranked on one axis and cut down the list, and the ads he threw away were carrying order value. He did not cut too deep. He cut without knowing what he was cutting, and 25% is simply where that starts to cost more than it saves.

**SC-008 is still contested and this collides with it head on.** Charley T's kill order starts at the highest spenders. Four operators plus a Meta employee say never turn off a profitable top spender, because it anchors delivery. The 2026-08-23 correction narrowed the fight: he is cutting the high-spend, **worst-profit** ad, capped at 20% of account spend per day. Nobody in the argument defends killing a profitable top spender. What survives is genuinely open, and it is whether an unprofitable-looking top spender is actually unprofitable or is a top-of-funnel engine whose value lands on other ads. Zero controlled data on either side.

## 3. Our accounts

Here is the finding from this pass, and it is about us.

**Across five accounts and every report we have ever filed, exactly one table puts a cost per opt-in next to a booking count for the same entity.** It is ChiroWorks, 10 to 24 July, and it is campaign level. There is no ad-level version of it anywhere, on any client, ever.

So the second axis MM-190 is built on does not exist on our book. We have been ranking on one axis for every account, every week, because it is the only axis that arrives by itself.

**The one time we did build it, it reversed.**

| Campaign | Spend | Opt-ins | Cost per opt-in | Booked | Cost per booking |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| invisa-RED free consultation | $358 | 17 | **$21.07** | 2 | $179 |
| Both offers consolidated | $115 | 2 | $57.37 | 1 | **$115** |
| Functional Tonal Neurology | $345 | 4 | $86.30 | 0 | none |

The cheapest campaign per opt-in was the most expensive per booking. Read column four and you fund invisa-RED. Read column six and the ranking flips.

**Now the guard, and it is in our own audit file, written by us at the time.** That reversal rests on **2 opt-ins and 1 booking**. Functional Tonal Neurology was worse on both measures, so it is not part of the reversal at all. The audit's own words: "Do not let a 2-lead sample redirect the budget." A reversal that would vanish if one person had answered a phone differently is a reason to build the column, not a reason to move money.

**What the same audit found downstream is the more useful number.** 24 of 25 opt-ins matched to Meta IDs at 96%. Nine of 24 were already in the CRM, aged 169 to 1,979 days. All three bookings came from those nine. Zero of the fifteen genuinely new opt-ins booked. Two of 22 reached a live human, median call length **5 seconds**. All three bookings arrived through text automation and none through the calling system. The line in that file: "the algorithm is optimising for the cheapest form, and the cheapest form is not the cheapest patient."

**StayWell, 21 to 27 August, live this week.** One campaign, 12 opt-ins for $252.68 at $21.06. Inside the campaign the spread is 4.6x: AD3 Truth-Bomb at **$5.19** is the cheapest, BestPerformer Remake at **$23.89** is the priciest and takes the largest spend. The report's own note: the two biggest-spend ads are the two priciest per opt-in. Downstream, 3 reached the appointment stage, 1 appointment booked, cancelled, show rate 0%.

**The week before is the closest thing to a fake win we can actually show.** 8 to 16 August: the ad that produced the one booking we can name was Old UGC Winning – Copy at **$20.82** per opt-in. The Testimonial Collage in the same campaign ran **$0.83**. The ad that booked someone cost **25 times** the cheapest ad beside it. One booking is one booking, so this is a sighting rather than a result. Two flags on that account before anyone quotes the opt-in counts: the lead pixel has double-fired historically, so 12 opt-ins may be about 6 people, and the CRM enquiry count is the reliable callable number.

**The same shape one level down.** On the 5 July demographic split, 25-34 men came in at **$1.91** each, the cheapest cell on the page, and booked nobody. 55-64 men came in at **$21.05**, eleven times the price, and produced the booking.

**SJR and Phoenix cannot build the column at all, and that is the finding.** SJR ran 312 opt-ins at $2.78 blended in the 27 August week. Downstream: 4 appointments, 0 marked shown, and **0 of 4,844 all-time have reached a scheduled call**. Phoenix ran 209 at $6.34, and every opportunity sits at New Lead for the eleventh straight week. The only real second column Phoenix has is Ruddy's own count of trucks delivered from marketing: 5 in March, 4 in April, 4 in May, 3 in June, **2 in July**, 18 total. His sentence is the whole argument for this lesson: "$10,000 a month to sell two trucks, I'm not making 10,000."

**And the pruning trigger barely fires on our book.** SC-152 wants more than 10 ads in one campaign. StayWell's single campaign has run 12. Every other account is under: Phoenix ships 8 statics in its own campaign with 2 in the Spanish video set, ChiroWorks Chiro Care holds 2 ads, SJR runs 9 creatives across two campaigns. **We do not have a pruning problem. We have a measurement problem, and pruning harder without the second column is the exact move that took an account from $206 of profit per sale to $131.**

## 4. The decision rule

**Never rank ads on cost per opt-in alone. Put the downstream column beside it first, and if the downstream column cannot be built on that account, say so out loud and fix the CRM stage before touching budget.**

> [!note]- Answer key
> 1. **What it includes and excludes.** Order value minus cost per acquisition. It nets out media cost only and does not deduct cost of goods, fulfilment or delivery. It should be called contribution after media, or contribution after ad spend. **The size of the error:** you overstate profit by the entire cost of goods line, so on a 40-point-margin product a stated $100 of profit is $40. Full credit needs the words "cost of goods is still in there". **The asymmetry, in his own terms:** "You can always optimize an environment to bring down the CPA. There's almost nothing you can do outside of the ad to improve the AOV of that ad." An expensive ad bringing in valuable customers has a fixable problem, because cost responds to bids, audiences, landing pages and offers. A cheap ad bringing in low-value customers has an unfixable one, because the value of who it attracts is a property of the ad itself. Bonus for noting the trap: fake wins are the quadrant a percentage scorecard actively promotes, so the better your ROAS column looks the more of them you may be funding.
> 2. **The honest answer is do not cull yet, and the reason is arithmetic rather than caution.** Twelve ads is one over the trigger, and the trigger is T3 from a vendor tool with no exported data. Twelve opt-ins across twelve ads is one opt-in per ad. At $21 each, an ad performing exactly at the account average shows zero at $50 of spend about 15% of the time, so the four zeros are inside noise and lesson 011 already covers them. **The two data problems.** The lead pixel has double-fired historically, so the 12 may be about 6 real people, which halves every per-ad denominator on the page. And the CRM enquiry count, 9, is the reliable callable number, so the cost per opt-in in the report is measured against a count we do not trust. **What to build first:** the ad name written onto the CRM record at opt-in, so that next month's table has a booking count per ad. Until that exists the cull is a cull of a column we know is wrong. Full credit for refusing the cull and naming the pixel issue. Bonus for noting that BestPerformer Remake being both the biggest spender and the priciest per opt-in is precisely the SC-008 fight, so it is the one ad you should touch last, and for noting that show rate 0% makes every per-ad argument this week moot because nothing has arrived at the other end of any of them.
> 3. **Neither is wrong, and the variable is what you cut rather than how much.** In the 89-ad case each losing ad had been individually measured at negative contribution, $48,800 of real spend at negative $0.98 per transaction, so cutting them removed a quantified loss and the depth was simply however many ads met that test. In the 46-ad case the ads were ranked on one axis and cut down the list, and the discarded ads were carrying order value, so the cut removed value that was never on the ranking. 25% to 30% is where ranking blind starts costing more than it saves. **The condition for a deep cut:** every ad you cut has been individually measured, on the downstream column, at negative or near-zero contribution. With that condition met, 95% is fine. Without it, 25% is already too far. Bonus for naming the two brakes that apply either way, never more than 20% of account spend in one day because pausing is itself a budget change, and law 3's rule that a starved ad has no measurement behind it and is not a candidate.
> 4. **What you can honestly claim: almost nothing about either ad.** Both cost figures are cost per form fill. The second column does not exist at ad level on Phoenix and has not existed for eleven weeks. **What the proposal is ranking on:** the price of a form submission, which is the exact single axis this lesson is about. The video may be the fake win or the static may be, and the data cannot say which. Note also that the video holds the largest single spend at $485, so this is the SC-008 argument again with no profit column to settle it. **What to ask Ruddy for:** which of the trucks delivered came from our opt-ins, by name and by month, matched back to the CRM record. Even five matched names across March to July would give the first ad-level downstream evidence on the account. Second best is any stage marked past New Lead, because the funnel is currently unmeasurable at the first step. Bonus for spotting that 2 trucks in July against his own $10,000 figure is the only profit column Phoenix has, and it is a monthly account-level number, so it can condemn the whole program and can never rank two ads. Bonus for noting the earlier report's "6-8x under the $50-80 target" is loose: at $9.82 the true range is 5.1x to 8.1x.
> 5. **What to do in the meantime, in order.** Keep reporting cost per opt-in, because it is real and it is the price of the thing we actually buy. Stop using it as a ranking. Write the ad name onto the CRM record at opt-in on every account, which is a one-time setup and the only thing standing between us and the second column. Judge each ad against its own history rather than against the ad beside it, which is lesson 012 and costs nothing. And where the downstream column genuinely cannot be built, say that in the report instead of implying the cheap ad won. **The failure mode of over-applying this:** refusing to cut anything, on the grounds that no cut can be justified without a column we do not have. That is worse than the original error. Liabilities are still identifiable without profit data, an ad with real funded spend and zero opt-ins over a long window is cuttable today, and law 12 covers it. **Why ChiroWorks is not evidence that cheap ads are bad:** it is one campaign-level table with 2 opt-ins and 1 booking on one side of the reversal, our own audit says do not redirect budget on it, and the third campaign was worse on both measures, which is the opposite of a reversal. The correct reading is that a single axis cannot see the answer, which is a claim about our instruments rather than about cheap ads. Bonus for naming the real cause the same audit found, that 2 of 22 reached a live human at a median call length of 5 seconds, so the largest measured loss in that account sat downstream of every ad in it.
