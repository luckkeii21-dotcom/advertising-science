# Lesson 031 · The Headline Number Is a Sum

Eleven days ago this codex banked a fact: one static ad had spent **$260,000**. The operator said "this particular static ad right here", gave a build date, gave a figure. It went into two claims as a data point about how much spend a single creative can carry.

Yesterday the same operator walked the same asset and added one sentence. "We do have it in three different cities."

It is three ads. **Roughly $78,300 each.** Nothing was a lie and the codex was wrong by 3x.

## 1. The mechanism

A headline number hides its parts in one of two ways, and both leave it technically true.

**Composition.** The number adds things together and the addition is invisible. Three ads become one ad. Four lead sources become one lead count. Four optimisation events become one Results column. You are handed a total and you read it as a unit.

**Construction.** The number is a product of inputs, and at least one input was assumed rather than measured. Reach times frequency times price looks like arithmetic. If two of the three were guessed, it is a guess with a decimal point on it.

Think about a phone bill. Four thousand rupees a month is a real number off a real statement. Whether it is high depends entirely on whether it is one person or four, and the bill does not say. The number is not wrong. It just cannot answer the question you asked it.

That is the whole lesson. **A number you cannot decompose cannot be compared to anything.** The $260,000 figure was being used to estimate how much spend one creative can absorb, which is a per-ad question, and the figure was a three-ad total. Wrong denominator, wrong answer, no dishonesty anywhere in the chain.

The reason this bites in advertising specifically is that almost every number we are handed is already an aggregate. An account total, a campaign total, a column that sums rows, a case study that sums cities. The default state of a marketing number is summed. Decomposed is the exception.

## 2. The evidence

- **[[Marketing Math & Unit Economics#MM-220|MM-220]], T3, banked today.** Meta itemised how a third-party estimate of Polish scam-ad revenue was built: reach times frequency times CPM. EU-wide reach for 27 countries applied to one country. Frequency assumed at 3, taken from a US vendor's page built on that vendor's own 2,800 customers. A CPM the authors state they could not estimate empirically. 108 ads observed on one iOS device the authors had deliberately conditioned. A full-year figure extrapolated from three single days that differed from each other by up to 80%. **Read the five checks, not the verdict.** Meta is a defendant in litigation brought by the party whose law firm wrote the report, and the report itself was not read here, so the method critique is adopted and the ruling is not.
- **[[Creative Science#CR-117|CR-117]], T3, corrected today.** Mean lifetime spend per ad is $700 to $1,500. The right tail is thousands of times the mean and rests on three operator claims. One of the three shrank by 3x yesterday. The other two, Charley T's $2 million ad and Blue Sense's quarter-million-a-month VSL, have never been re-told and carry exactly the same exposure.
- **[[Creative Science#CR-048|CR-048]], T3, corrected today.** The count of operators reporting six figures of lifetime spend on ONE static drops from three to two. The format argument survives, because $78,300 on a single static is still two orders of magnitude above the mean.
- **[[Marketing Math & Unit Economics#MM-067|MM-067]], T3.** A $2M/year brand's TikTok account reported 1,100 conversions against 158 seven-day-click purchases, because some campaigns optimised for add to cart and some for view content. The conversion count was a sum of three different events. It produced an apparent LTGP:CAC of 7 to 8. Computed on purchases it was **0.48**. The incumbent agency read the 7 and scaled spend into it.
- **[[Learning & Signal#LS-081|LS-081]], T2, measured on our own exports.** Meta's Results column prints whatever each ad set optimises for. One of our accounts printed four different events in it in one week.
- **[[Meta Delivery & Andromeda#MD-153|MD-153]], T1.** The sibling rule for a different class of number: before repeating a platform case study, find the word. *Incremental* or *lift* means somebody claims a holdout. *Increase* or *more* means a before-and-after.

MM-220 and MM-067 are the same defect on opposite sides of the screen. An outsider guessing at Meta's numbers and a platform summing its own events both hand you a product or a total that reads as a measurement.

## 3. Our accounts

**SJR Commercial, 9 to 16 June 2026.** The `Truck buyer leads | NJ` campaign, 2 ad sets, $910.26. Its own Results column reads **$10.5844 per result**. The business actually bought those people at **$6.8959**. The column is high by a little over half, because 34.85% of that campaign's opt-ins came through the website and a form ad set's column cannot count a door it did not optimise for. Across the whole account that week, $4,897.15 bought **783 opt-ins**: 560 through Meta forms and 223 through the website, so **28.48%** sit outside the column entirely.

That is composition and construction on one screen. The column sums units that are not the same unit, and it drops a door.

**SJR Commercial, weekly report filed 13 July 2026.** 161 leads entered the pipeline in the window. The report printed the breakdown on the next line: SWA Zapier 80, Facebook 63, Renters Remorse 14, landing page 4. **Only 63 of the 161 came from the ads, 39%.** Our own report got this right. The headline sentence and the decomposition shipped together, which is what made the Facebook feed stopping after 9 July visible at all. Same discipline, same week, applied in one place and missing in the other.

**SJR Commercial, the `Hal w hook` pair.** Same ad name under two optimisation events in the same week: $137.66 per opt-in against $3.497. A factor of 39. It is a natural pairing and not a test, because the destination and the budget moved with the event. Three variables in a two-row table.

**Lesson 030's ranking table.** Cost per opt-in ran backwards across the three quality tiers, and the Below tier was a single ad. A tier average built on one row is that row wearing a bigger name.

## 4. The decision rule

**Before you repeat a number, name its divisor and name which of its inputs was measured. If you cannot do both in one sentence, the number is a headline and not a finding.**

Two questions, and they take about ten seconds each.

- **Divisor.** How many things is this divided across? Three cities, four lead sources, four optimisation events, one ad?
- **Measurement.** Of the inputs this was built from, which were counted and which were assumed?

The tell to listen for is an operator saying "this one ad" with no count beside it. The tell to look for in a report is a total with no breakdown on the next line.

## 5. Quiz

Five questions. Answers go in `_answers-inbox.md`. Partial answers get graded.

**Q1.** Name the two ways a headline number hides its parts, and give the one question that catches each.

**Q2.** A competitor's case study says one creative has done £1.2m in spend. Write the three questions you send back before anything from it enters a brief, and say which single one, if unanswered, stops you using the figure at all.

**Q3.** SJR's `Truck buyer leads | NJ` campaign reads $10.5844 per result and the business bought those opt-ins at $6.8959. Explain the gap in two sentences without using the word "attribution", then say which of the two numbers belongs in a client report and why.

**Q4.** You are writing the SJR weekly for the 6 to 12 July window. The CRM shows 161 leads created: SWA Zapier 80, Facebook 63, Renters Remorse 14, landing 4. Write the one-sentence headline you would actually put at the top of a client report, and say what you would refuse to put in the cost-per-opt-in cell and why.

**Q5.** SJR, 9 to 16 June: $4,897.15 spent, 783 opt-ins, 560 through Meta forms and 223 through the website. Compute the account-level cost per opt-in. Then say whether you would print it, and name the one assumption that would break it if it turned out to be false.

> [!note]- Answer key
>
> **A1.** **Composition**, the number sums parts and the addition is invisible. Question: how many things is this divided across? **Construction**, the number is a product of inputs and some were assumed. Question: which of the inputs was actually measured? Full marks need both phrased as questions you would ask out loud, because that is the form they get used in.
>
> **A2.** One, is that one ad or several ads added together, and if several, how many? Two, over what window, and does the window start at the ad's build date or is it a trailing period? Three, is that spend on one account or summed across clients, geos or duplicates? **The first one stops you cold if it is unanswered**, because the entire use of the figure is per-ad capacity and an undisclosed divisor makes it unusable. The $260,000 static answered questions two and three in its first telling and only answered question one eleven days later. Extra credit for saying you treat it as a sum until the operator says otherwise.
>
> **A3.** The campaign optimised for the Meta form, so its own column counts only form submissions, while a real share of the people that campaign produced filled in the form on the dealership website instead. The column's numerator is the full spend and its denominator is only one of the two doors, so the price comes out inflated. **$6.8959 belongs in the report**, because it is the price the business actually paid per person who opted in, and it is the number that survives a check against the CRM. Do not lose the point by writing "attribution".
>
> **A4.** Something close to: "161 people entered the pipeline this week, 63 of them from our Meta ads, and the Facebook-sourced flow stopped completely after 9 July." **Refuse to put anything in the cost-per-opt-in cell**, because the ad account was unreadable that week, so there is no spend figure, and dividing by 161 would price our 63 against three other sources' volume. An empty cell with a reason beside it is honest. Full marks require both the 63 and the refusal, and the strongest answers say the 161 is a sum of four intake routes before they say anything else.
>
> **A5.** $4,897.15 divided by 783 is **$6.2543 per opt-in**. Yes, print it, with the two doors named beside it. **The assumption that would break it: that all 223 website opt-ins were produced by the ads.** The website has organic and direct traffic the account did not pay for, and if a share of the 223 arrived on its own, the denominator is too big and the true cost per ad-driven opt-in is higher. The fix is a source or UTM split on the website form, not a different formula. Extra credit for noticing this is the mistake the lesson opens with, pointed at us: we corrected one summed denominator and quietly built a new one.
