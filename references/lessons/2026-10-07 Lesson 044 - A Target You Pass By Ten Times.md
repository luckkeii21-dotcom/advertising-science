# Lesson 044 · A Target You Pass By Ten Times

🎬 **Watch first (3m 59s):** [[video/2026-10-07-lesson-044.mp4|Lesson 044 video]]

Three filed Phoenix Truxx reports in July 2026 carry the same row.

| Report window | Plan target | Achieved | Verdict printed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jun 29 to Jul 5 | $50 to $80 per opt-in | $7.42 | PASS by 7 to 11x |
| Jul 3 to Jul 9 | $50 to $80 per opt-in | $9.11 | PASS by 5.5 to 8.8x |
| Jul 10 to Jul 12 | $50 to $80 per opt-in | $8.27 | PASS by 6 to 10x |

In the same month the account delivered **2 trucks**. The client's own bar is **5 a month**. July cost him **$9,792 all in**, and on 5 August David Ruddy said it to our faces: "To spend 10,000 a month to sell two trucks. I'm not making 10,000. It's not worth it."

So the instrument printed PASS, three weeks running, by nearly an order of magnitude, on an account the client was about to cut.

The target was not wrong by a rounding error. It was wrong by ten times, in the direction where it can never fire.

## 1. The mechanism

Any number sitting next to a cost per opt-in is one of three different things, and they are not interchangeable.

**What happened.** An average over whatever you ran. ChiroWorks at $33.52. It is a measurement. It has no opinion.

**What the business needs.** The price at which buying a customer still makes money. It comes from the back end: gross profit per sale, and how many opt-ins it takes to get one. It is the only one of the three that can judge anything.

**What the last slice cost.** The increment. You added spend, you got extra opt-ins, and those extra ones have their own price. It is usually worse than the average, and the average hides it.

The failure in the Phoenix table is using the second word for a number that was never derived like the second thing. Someone wrote $50 to $80 into a plan. Nothing on file shows where it came from. Then it got printed as a gate in filed client reports.

Here is the shape of it. A school sets the pass mark at the class average. Nobody can ever say the class did badly, because half of them clear it by construction. Then the class improves, the pass mark rises with it, and the same paper that passed in March fails in June. The mark is measuring the class against itself. To mean anything it has to come from outside the room.

Phoenix's version is worse than a pass mark at the average. It is a pass mark at a tenth of the average. A standard set that far below the real price cannot be missed, so clearing it carries no information at all. Three reports said PASS and none of them told anybody anything.

**The second half, and this is the one that costs money.** Kill rules get denominated in the target. Our Mattia strategy writes one out: kill an ad at **3x target CPL with zero qualified leads**. Attach that rule shape to Phoenix's $50 to $80 and it fires at $150 to $240 on an account running $9.06. An ad would have to get **17 to 26 times more expensive than everything around it** before the rule does anything. The rule is written down, it looks like discipline, and it is switched off.

## 2. The evidence

**[[Marketing Math & Unit Economics#MM-178|MM-178]], T4.** Judge a period against the plan, never against last period. The observation underneath it is about the tools: Meta and Google hand you two comparison buttons, previous period and previous year, so those become the baselines everybody reasons from. A week that improves on a bad week reads as progress while the month misses its number. It is T4 because the operator's wider argument, that the blended target should float across the year, does not reconcile in his own figures. The comparison-baseline half stands on its own.

**[[Marketing Math & Unit Economics#MM-168|MM-168]], T3.** A metric on target while the business is flat is the signature of a number that hides a problem. His case is an agency hitting its return targets for six years on a business that grew 5% in total, and hitting the targets is exactly what kept it buried. Asserted with no P&L shown, so T3. Phoenix is the in-house instance and it is stronger than his, because we hold the dates and the dollar figures.

**[[Marketing Math & Unit Economics#MM-222|MM-222]], T3.** The cleanest worked example of average versus marginal on file. Baseline $337 per new customer. Add 25% more spend, $80,000 of it. The average degrades to $392, which clears a $400 tolerance, and most reporting stops there. That $80,000 bought 76 customers, so the increment cost about **$1,052 each**, two and a half times the tolerance. The average absorbed it because the base was big enough to dilute it.

**[[Marketing Math & Unit Economics#MM-199|MM-199]], T3.** Where a real target comes from. A buyer mid-walkthrough of his own best account names the limit of his own job: the thing standing between $5,000 a day and $10,000 a day is not the campaign, the creative or the bid, it is that the business cannot yet accept a $100 acquisition cost instead of $50. Raising that number is a back-end decision. The ceiling is set outside the ad account.

**[[Marketing Math & Unit Economics#MM-223|MM-223]], T3** and **[[Marketing Math & Unit Economics#MM-183|MM-183]], T3** are the guard rails. MM-223 gives two health numbers that are not ratios at all, so they cannot rise while the business shrinks. MM-183 says the reading window has to match the volume, which is why none of this gets read daily at our clients' spend.

Tier teaching for today: **the weakest claim in the stack is carrying the lesson.** MM-178 is T4 and it is the spine, because the three T3s all describe consequences of the thing MM-178 names. A low tier means test it harder before betting on it. It does not mean ignore it, and here the in-house evidence is firmer than the claim that pointed at it.

## 3. Our accounts

**Four accounts gate on their own achieved average.** ChiroWorks, 6 July to 22 September, $4,794.02 across 143 opt-ins, **$33.52 each**. StayWell, 15 June to 23 September, $3,704.08 across 144, **$25.72**. SJR Commercial, 21 to 27 August, $866.14 across 312, **$2.78 blended**. Phoenix Truxx, **$4.00 all-time**. Every one of those is a measurement of whatever we happened to run, and every one gets quoted as a gate or a pass row.

**A correction to what we have been carrying since 25 September.** The open item says no account we run has a written target. That is wrong. **Four of them do, and all four are wrong in the same direction.**

| Account | Written target | Real price | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phoenix Truxx | $50 to $80 per opt-in | $9.06, Jul 10 to 16 | 6 to 9x too loose |
| StayWell | $30 to $50 | $5.02, 239 opt-ins, 1 May to 6 Jun | 6 to 8x too loose |
| Mattia Spinal Care | $20 to $40 blended | $9 to $20 under the incumbent | 2 to 4x too loose |
| SJR dump division | $4, stretch sub-$3 | not yet launched | borrowed, see below |

SJR's is the most instructive. The transfer strategy writes "Target CPL ≤ $4 (stretch sub-$3)" for the dump-truck division. **Four dollars is Phoenix Truxx's achieved all-time figure, and $3.19 is Phoenix's early all-time blend.** It is another account's average with the word target in front of it. Nothing about SJR's dump economics went into it.

**The Mattia doc is the one that got it right, and it diagnosed the whole defect three months ago.** Written 2 July: "CPL targets miscalibrated ~5x ($30-50 target vs $5-13 actual), which poisons every kill rule downstream. Decision rules must be denominated in cost-per-showed and cost-per-case." It then sets a real one, **cost per case under $1,000**, and instructs that the cheap front-end metric is demoted in every report. That is MM-199 executed. The ceiling came from the back end.

**The one target on our whole book that was derived from the business, and the client wrote it rather than us.** Ruddy, 5 August: 5 or more trucks delivered a month, or the account does not pay for itself. Delivered by month: March 5, April 4, May 4, June 3, **July 2**, 18 total from marketing. We called his arithmetic soft in the meeting note and never checked it. It was right. July all in was $9,792 against 2 sales.

**And we still cannot convert his target into a media-buying rule.** To turn 5 trucks a month into an allowable cost per opt-in you need gross profit per truck and the opt-in-to-sale rate. The 19-sale VinSolutions file has **no vehicle, no selling price and no gross column**. We matched 13 of the 19 to our own opt-in records by name, and the median is 12 days from opt-in to sale, range 3 to 37. So the one honest target on the book stops one column short of being usable, and that missing column is a request, not a calculation.

**The marginal read, run on our book for the first time.** Phoenix stepped spend in July and the two windows are clean enough to subtract.

| | Spend | Opt-ins | Blended |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jul 3 to 9 | $1,075.68 | 118 | $9.11 |
| Jul 10 to 16 | $1,340.88 | 148 | $9.06 |
| **The increment** | **$265.20** | **30** | **$8.84** |

The increment cost **$8.84**, slightly under the blended $9.06, and the average barely moved. MM-222's warning is that an average hides a bad marginal. Here it hid a good one, which is the result worth reporting precisely because it is the one nobody was looking for.

**One honest caveat and it is large.** That increment is not a budget step, it is a budget step plus the statics engine coming back on. The cheap English statics ad set ran $5.39 against the legacy video at $10.55. So $8.84 prices a creative change and a spend change together, which is MM-178's own named confounder. Call it a reading, not an experiment.

## 4. The decision rule

**A number can only judge an account if it was written before the period and derived from the back end. Everything else is a reading, and a reading gets labelled as one.**

Three things follow from it this week.

Label the four achieved figures as achieved, in the file that holds each one. One clause each.

Re-derive the four written targets from the back end or retire them. A target beaten by six times does not need loosening, it needs deleting, because while it sits there it reads as a PASS and it switches off every kill rule denominated in it.

Ask Phoenix for gross profit per truck. It is the one input that turns Ruddy's 5 trucks into a price we can buy against, and it is one column on a file they already have.

## 5. Quiz

Drop your answers in `lessons/_answers-inbox.md`.

**1.** Name the three things a number beside a cost per opt-in can be, and say which of the three is allowed to decide whether to kill an ad.

**2.** ChiroWorks runs at $33.52 per opt-in. A buyer proposes a kill rule of 3x the account's achieved figure, so $100.57. State the specific defect in that rule, and say what happens to it the month ChiroWorks improves to $28.

**3.** StayWell's filed report shows 239 opt-ins at $5.02 against a written target of $30 to $50, and calls it about one eighth of target. Phoenix's shows $9.06 against $50 to $80. Both are printed as passes. Explain why clearing a target by that margin tells the client nothing, and name the claim.

**4.** A client has run at $40 per opt-in on $100 a day for five months, flat. They ask you to forecast what $300 a day would produce. Say what you can honestly give them and what you cannot, name the two claims that decide it, and say what you would ask for.

**5.** Phoenix's July increment priced at $8.84 against a blended $9.06, which looks like proof that more spend was cheap. Give the strongest reason that figure should not be quoted to the client as a marginal cost, and say what you would have had to run to earn the right to quote it.

> [!note]- Answer key
> **1.** What happened (the achieved average, a measurement). What the business needs (the target, derived from gross profit per sale and the opt-in-to-sale rate). What the last slice cost (the marginal figure). **Only the target can decide a kill.** The achieved average judges the account against itself, which means it cannot fail and it moves whenever the account moves. The marginal figure tells you whether the next dollar is worth spending, which is a budget question rather than a kill question. Full credit also names that a kill decision wants the target expressed per booked appointment or per sale wherever the account can carry it, per [[Marketing Math & Unit Economics#MM-199|MM-199]].
>
> **2.** The defect is that the gate is anchored to the number it is supposed to judge, so it is not an independent standard. If ChiroWorks improves to $28, the gate drops to $84, and an ad that was acceptable at $90 last month becomes a kill with nothing about that ad having changed. It fails in the other direction too: a bad month raises the gate and protects the ads that caused it. Best answers point out that $33.52 is itself a 79-day average across two different offers, invisa-RED at $32.96 and the chiropractic consultation at $34.64, so 3x of it is 3x of a blend. The fix is a written target per offer.
>
> **3.** A standard set six to nine times above the real price cannot be missed, so clearing it is not evidence of anything. **[[Marketing Math & Unit Economics#MM-168|MM-168]]** is the claim: a metric on target while the business is flat is the signature of a number that hides a problem. Phoenix is the proof on our own book. Three consecutive filed reports printed PASS on cost while the account delivered 2 trucks against a bar of 5, and the client said $9,792 for two trucks was not worth it. Credit for noting the honest version is already in the 27 August report, which says cost "has never been the constraint" and therefore stops looking there.
>
> **4.** You can give them the achieved average and the spread of weekly readings behind it. You cannot give them a spend-response forecast. **[[Marketing Math & Unit Economics#MM-183|MM-183]]** is the first claim: with no spend variance in the history, every data point sits at one spend level, so any model fitted to it is decoration. **[[Marketing Math & Unit Economics#MM-222|MM-222]]** is the second: the $40 average is the price of the first $100 a day and says nothing about the price of the next $200, which is a different and almost certainly worse number. What to ask for: a deliberate step, run long enough to read. Hold creative and offer fixed, move to $200 a day for a fortnight, and subtract the two windows to price the increment. Say out loud that the first read will be noisy. Best answers add that at our clients' volumes the window is weekly or monthly, never daily.
>
> **5.** The increment is confounded. The spend step and the return of the statics engine landed in the same week, and the cheap English statics ad set ran $5.39 against the legacy video at $10.55, so $8.84 prices a creative change as well as a budget change. That is MM-178's own named confounder: a variance-to-plan read has to carry the possibility of a creative or structural change, or it becomes a spending verdict on something else entirely. To earn the quote you need the step with composition held: same creatives live, same ad sets, same offer, budget the only thing that moved, across two comparable windows. Credit for noting that 32 of the 148 opt-ins in the second window are web or pixel events rather than Meta form submissions, so the two windows are not counting quite the same thing, and that the Phoenix web form was not wired into the CRM at the time.
