# Lesson 078 · The Ceiling Was Set Sixteen Percent Above Where We Already Are

🎬 Video: [[video/2026-11-11-lesson-078.mp4]]

Its two findings are about our own instrument and about one source's rotation behaviour, neither of which is advertising science. No harvest to teach, so the rotation decided.

The claim is [[Marketing Math & Unit Economics#MM-172|MM-172]], banked on 26 August and never taught. Seventy-seven days.

Timing is not an accident today. Tomorrow you raise the Phoenix dump lane from $40 a day to $60, the first of six steps to $130 by 24 November, and the gate you wrote for each step is a cost per lead under about $35.

## 1. The mechanism

Cost per opt-in is not a number your account has. It is a number your account has **at a budget**. Change the budget and you are quoting a price for a thing you are no longer buying.

The reason is the shape of the demand pool. In any market some people are already looking. They are cheap, because the ad does almost no work: it arrives, it names the thing, they were ready. Behind them stand people who would buy if pushed, and behind those, people who are not in the market at all. The platform spends your money on the easiest available person first, every day, because that is what optimising for a conversion means. So the first dollar you spend buys the cheapest person in the pool and the ten-thousandth buys whoever is left.

The analogy maps exactly. You are picking apples off one tree. The low fruit comes off in minutes. Then you need the ladder. Then you need the ladder in a bad spot. Nothing about the tree got worse, nothing about your technique got worse, and your apples per hour fell anyway. Double the hours and you do not double the apples. The tree is the market. Your budget is the hours.

Two consequences, and the second one is the one that costs money.

**First: a cost per opt-in that holds while you scale is information, not luck.** It means the pool had slack, or the creative reached a part of it the old creative never did.

**Second, and this is where plans break: the budget approval and the number used to justify it refer to different budgets.** You read $30 at today's spend, you approve triple the spend, and the plan assumes the $30 travels. It does not travel. That is not pessimism. It is the only direction this curve has ever been observed to run.

## 2. The evidence

[[Marketing Math & Unit Economics#MM-172|MM-172]] is **T2**, and it is the best-evidenced scaling-decay case anywhere in the codex, because the numbers are a publicly traded company's own disclosed filings and cohort tables rather than an operator's memory. A supplement brand, read off its own documents:

- Q1 2025: 27,000 new customers at a **$104** acquisition cost, the cheapest they ever bought one.
- Q1 2026: spend stepped to $18.3M, acquisition cost **$305**. Tripled.
- Q2 2026: spend doubled again to $35M.
- Six-month gross profit against acquisition cost on those cohorts: **2.0x falling to 1.03x**. At 2.0x a dollar of marketing returned two dollars of gross profit by month six. At 1.03x it returns the dollar.
- Q2 2026: they spent **78% of revenue** on Meta and Google, and posted **negative $19M** adjusted profit at a **negative 41%** margin.

Then the structural finding, which is the part to carry into every conversation about a competitor who is scaling fast. **The scale is funded, not earned.** A third party pays **70% of the acquisition cost** on new cohorts. The cash still has to be repaid later. So their cash flow genuinely improved while their unit economics genuinely collapsed, and both facts live in the same quarter.

The guard is written into the claim and it stays attached: these are the company's own numbers, presented by a company with an interest in presenting them well, and the analyst reading them says so himself. The claim also records four number hazards in that source, pairs of figures that contradict each other in the same breath, none of which were banked. Nothing in this lesson uses them.

A second **T2** instance, smaller and closer to our size. [[Marketing Math & Unit Economics#MM-146|MM-146]]: a roughly $2M-a-year brand added a channel as purely additive budget, total media went $40,000 to $60,000 a month, and net sales rose **6%**. Fifty per cent more money for six per cent more sales. Long-term gross profit against acquisition cost computed live at **0.48**, which is forty-eight cents back per dollar in. Order count rose while order value fell, so anyone reading volume alone would have called it a win.

The model form is **T3**. [[Marketing Math & Unit Economics#MM-120|MM-120]] states it in one line: scaling margin is lifetime value over **final** acquisition cost, and because acquisition cost is a function of the spend level, a healthy number today does not survive the budget you plan to reach. The operator raises modelled spend 40% and watches final cost per acquisition climb to $142. Worked on screen, no exported account data, so it is a model and not a measurement. It still tells you which number to put in the plan.

The thing that stops you panicking is also **T3**. [[Marketing Math & Unit Economics#MM-021|MM-021]]: if you raise a budget by **less than one result costs**, the added money cannot yet buy a whole conversion, so the average cost per result climbs as pure arithmetic until conversions catch up. Expected math to sit through, not a performance problem and not a reason to revert.

The piece that explains why this bites us harder than it bites them is **T4**, so it decides nothing. [[Marketing Math & Unit Economics#MM-182|MM-182]]: a fixed geography saturates orders of magnitude faster than a national one. Pure assertion, no curve, no account, illustrated with Pepsi and Coca-Cola. It is banked because the codex has no other entry that states it, and because it transfers to our book harder than to the book of the man who said it. Treat it as the hypothesis you reach for after the measurement, never as the reason for a decision.

Notice the ladder, because it is the method rather than the content. **T2 says the decay is real and gives it a size. T3 says where to put the number in a plan. T4 says why a local account might get there sooner.** A plan built on the T2 and the T3 is defensible. A plan built on the T4 is a story.

## 3. Our accounts

**Phoenix Truxx is the clean case, and the surprise is that spend never rose.** Real people first-touched by our ads, month by month:

| Month | Spend | Opt-ins | Cost per opt-in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jul 2026 | $5,791.81 | 467 | $12.40 |
| Aug 2026 | $6,198.59 | 509 | $12.18 |
| Sep 2026 | $5,777.65 | 417 | $13.86 |
| Oct 2026 | $5,748.85 | 313 | $18.37 |

Four months inside a $450 spend band, and the price went up **51%** from August to October. MM-172's mechanism is a spend-level mechanism, and this account did not change its spend level. So something else ate the pool, and the account tells us what: **34 live ads with 6 of them taking 80% of October, about 4 new ads shipped all month, and 5% of the last seven days' spend on creative under 30 days old against a 25% target.** September says the same thing from another angle: image ads held 230 opt-ins across two months while cost per lead rose every week, $9.40 to $14.94, as frequency rose.

That is the honest version of the mechanism at our size. **The pool gets exhausted by repetition at flat spend, not only by budget.** MM-182 is the hypothesis for why it happens this fast: a nine-state county-targeted footprint weighted to New Jersey, about 9.5M people, about 2.23M Hispanic or Latino, and the real addressable number is the credit-blocked Spanish-speaking tradesman subset of that. Not tens of millions. T4, so it explains and does not decide.

**Now tomorrow's decision, with the arithmetic done.** The dump lane spent about $333 in the week of 30 October to 5 November for 11 opt-ins at **$30.28**, roughly $48 a day across a week that straddled the 3 November cut to $40. The written ramp is $40 to $60 on 12 November, then $80, $100, $115, $130 by 24 November, with the main campaign walking $160 down to $70 in step. The gate on every step is a dump-lane cost per lead **under about $35**.

Two things fall straight out of the claims.

**The gate has 16% of headroom.** $35 against $30.28 is $4.72. MM-172's case tripled. MM-146's case bought 6% more revenue with 50% more money. If this lane behaves anything like either one, the gate trips somewhere around the $80 or $100 step and the plan ends early, in week two, with box at or near zero and nothing left to fall back on. That is not an argument against the ramp. It is an argument for deciding **now** what happens when the gate trips, because deciding it at $100 a day with an unhappy client is a worse conversation.

**The first step will look like a failure and will not be one.** $40 to $60 is a $20 step, and one opt-in in this lane costs $30.28. Twenty dollars cannot buy a whole opt-in. MM-021 says the average rises on arithmetic alone until the conversions catch up, so the lane's cost per opt-in gets worse for the first day or two and that reading means nothing. **Do not revert on it, and do not report it.**

**SJR Commercial is the counter-case, and it refutes nothing.** The week of 30 October to 5 November: $769.72, 124 opt-ins, **$6.21**, vans 88 at $5.59 and dump 36 at $7.72. Second-cheapest week on record, where every week before 23 October ran $7.49 to $8.21. The account got cheaper. Read why before calling it a golden goose: two fresh units carried the vans, Asia Hybrid at 44 and $4.97 and UGC Inspirational at 35 and $4.96, and Instagram Reels overtook Facebook Reels, 40 against 36, with Instagram growing 28 to 53 opt-ins. **New creative and a new placement bought headroom. The pool did not get bigger.** That is the decay being paid off, not repealed, and the bill comes back.

**StayWell is the sample-size case, and it is the one that stops you over-reading your own account.** Four consecutive weeks at roughly flat spend around $535 to $562: $14.35 for 39 opt-ins, then $19.12 for 28, then $29.87 for 18, then $24.46 for 23. The price doubled and came back 18%. Then the detail that settles it: the 23 to 29 October recovery came from **both education videos recovering without a refresh**. Nothing was changed and the number improved. At 18 to 39 opt-ins a week, a single opt-in moves the average by 3 to 6%, and three fewer people in a week moves it a great deal more than that. Phoenix's four months at 313 to 509 a month is a trend. StayWell's four weeks is weather.

## 4. The decision rule

**Model the cost per opt-in at the budget you are moving to, never the one you are moving from, and write down in advance what you do when the gate trips.**

Three tests, in this order, before any budget step is approved:

1. **What price does this plan need at the target budget, and how much headroom is that above today?** If the headroom is thinner than the decay already measured in this account, the plan fails on arithmetic before it launches.
2. **Is this step smaller than one result costs?** If yes, the first days get worse on arithmetic. Expect it, do not revert, do not report it.
3. **Is the thing I am reading a trend or a week?** Direction holding across three comparable periods at flat spend, on a sample where one result moves the average under about 5%. Below that, you are reading weather.
