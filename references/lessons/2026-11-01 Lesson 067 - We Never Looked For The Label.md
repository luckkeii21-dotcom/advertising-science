# Lesson 067 · We Never Looked For The Label

Topic: Creative Science, taken from the rotation at the pointer. Both of this morning's research passes came back empty, so there was no harvest to teach. The pointer's own topic and the oldest claim in the teaching queue are the same thing today, which has not happened before: [[Creative Science#CR-288|CR-288]], banked 23 October, nine days waiting. No video, Sunday.

## 1. The mechanism

Every time an ad gets more expensive, somebody in this business says the ad wore out. We say it too. We have said it in a client report four times in the last eight weeks.

The evidence we offer is always the same shape. A cost per lead that climbed week over week. A frequency number that went up. A click rate that drifted down. Each of those is a fingerprint, not a confession. They are all consistent with the ad wearing out, and they are all equally consistent with four other things: a smaller pool, a pricier auction, a broken form, a holiday weekend.

CR-288 points at something different. Meta applies a **creative fatigue label to the ad itself**. Jon Loomer, 21 October:

> "Meta will tell you if frequency is a problem. Your ads will get a label of creative fatigue. And that's a sign that, yes, people have seen your ad or ads too many times, and you need to create something different. But don't assume that will happen. And achieving creative fatigue is likely way more difficult than you think."

The analogy is a smoke alarm. You can stand in the hallway and argue about whether the smell is smoke or somebody's toast. Or you can look up and see whether the alarm is going off. Arguing about the smell is free and infinite. Looking up takes two seconds and ends the argument.

The way to use this is as a **gate, not advice**. Before writing the word fatigue, look for the label. If the label is not there, the drop has a different cause and you go and find it.

And read Loomer's second sentence, because it is the half that costs money: *achieving creative fatigue is likely way more difficult than you think.* The default assumption in this industry runs the wrong way.

## 2. The evidence

[[Creative Science#CR-288|CR-288]] is **T3**. One operator, one sentence, on camera. He describes what the label means, shows it to nobody, and names no location in Ads Manager. There is no case anywhere in his account of checking the label against an outcome.

So why does one sentence from one person earn an ID at all? Because of what sits around it.

[[Creative Science#CR-027|CR-027]] is **T3, contested**, and it is the longest-running fight in this codex. Does audience exhaustion happen at normal spend? Ben Heath says yes and the fix is volume. Charley T says no and the cause is the operator launching new ads that eat the incumbent's lunch. Andrew Faris says both are describing better delivery burning the pool faster. Loomer himself adds a fourth position, warm-pool exhaustion. **Four camps, months of argument, and not one of them has produced a first-party readout.**

[[Creative Science#CR-116|CR-116]] is **T3, contested** too, and it is the threshold everyone quotes: purchase probability collapses after two impressions of the same creative, so read ad-level frequency above 2 as a red flag. It went contested on 23 October because Loomer reported a $450,000 broad-US campaign at total frequency 4.5, carrying two ads at about 3.0 each, with no fatigue and results improving month over month. Read that refutation narrowly, because his figures carry no window and [[Meta Delivery & Andromeda#MD-008|MD-008]] says a frequency number means nothing until you name the window. His lifetime 3.0 does not numerically beat a 14-day 2.0.

That is the whole point. Two contested T3 claims, four camps, and the one check that would settle any of it is a label in the interface that **nobody on this team has ever gone looking for.**

One guard before you lean on it. [[Meta Delivery & Andromeda#MD-105|MD-105]] is **T2** and it records Meta's own diagnostic surface recommending a setting that was already switched on. A Meta label is a signal, not a verdict. Verify where it reads before you quote it to a client.

## 3. Our accounts

Here is our own record, and it is worse than I expected.

**StayWell, 29 June to 5 July.** Same $244 of spend, half the leads: 12 at $20.35 against 26 at $9.40 the week before. Link clicks were **119 in both weeks**. Click-to-form conversion halved, 10.9% to 5.0%, on an ad roster unchanged for 19 days. And **frequency fell to 2.10**. We filed that as fatigue hitting conversion before it hit the frequency trigger. Nobody checked the label.

**ChiroWorks, week to 8 October.** CTR held at 1.01% against 1.02%, CPM flat at $20.82, and form completion fell to 6.5% from 10.9%. The first draft blamed a pricier audience pool. Kartik killed that and named creative fatigue on Ad_05 instead. Both explanations were inferred from the same three numbers. Nobody checked the label.

**Phoenix Truxx, September.** The image lane held 230 leads in both August and September while cost per lead walked $9.40, $12.82, $13.34, $14.94, weekly frequency went 2.1 to 2.4, and clicks per impression fell about 2.1% to 1.7%. That is the most careful fatigue case we have ever built, and it is still four inferred signals. Nobody checked the label.

**SJR Commercial, Phase 5 dump bench.** The Caesar Spanish dump video in about week 10, taking 63% of dump spend, cost per lead $3.20 to $6.49 to $8.70 to $9.18 at 2.33 frequency. Nobody checked the label.

Four accounts, four fatigue diagnoses, zero label checks.

Two more things our own book says. Our refresh SOP fires at **2.7 all-time frequency**, which is a number with no first-party source anywhere in this codex. And on MetaTechAI the arithmetic kills the trigger outright: about 52,500 impressions of 14-day spend against a 235,635,000-adult catchment puts 14-day ad-level frequency near 1.0 for the entire run, so it will never cross 2.0 and a frequency trigger in that account would never fire once.

Worth holding against all of it: [[Creative Science#CR-261|CR-261]] is **T1**, Meta's own measurement, and it says an ad set carrying an image, a video and a vertical video with audio together delivered 7.3% lower cost per acquisition. Creative variety earns its keep whether or not fatigue is real. The fatigue argument is about **why you refresh and when**, never about whether variety is worth having.

## 4. The decision rule

**Look for Meta's creative fatigue label before you write the word fatigue. No label means the cause is somewhere else, and you go and find it before the report goes out.**

The first job this creates is not a report. It is a two-minute errand: open one client account, find out whether this label exists and where it reads, and write down what you saw. Until somebody does that, CR-288 is a thing we read rather than a thing we use.

> [!note]- Answer key
>
> **A1.** One sentence close to: Meta applies a creative fatigue label to an ad when frequency is genuinely a problem, so the absence of that label is evidence against a fatigue diagnosis. Tier **T3**. Evidence behind it: one operator stating it on camera, 21 October 2026. He does not show the label, names no location in Ads Manager, and gives no case where its presence or absence was tested against an outcome. Why it earns an ID: every other fatigue claim in the codex is argued from inferred evidence, a frequency number or a spend curve or a cost rise, and the CR-027 debate has run for months across four camps without anybody producing a first-party readout. This points at something that either appears in the interface or does not. It is the cheapest check available and the only one that needs no theory. Credit for noting his prescription when the label IS present matches the rest of the corpus: create more ads.
>
> **A2.** Because his figures carry no window. He says total frequency 4.5 on a campaign running "over a long period of time", and about 3.0 on two ads that took $150,000 each across that same span, so those are lifetime numbers. CR-116's threshold is a 30-to-60-day read and CR-177's is 14 days. The claim that tells you why is **[[Meta Delivery & Andromeda#MD-008|MD-008]]**: a frequency number means nothing until the window is named. A lifetime 3.0 across many months may well be consistent with a 14-day 2.0. What does survive is qualitative, a deliberately concentrated $450,000 campaign reporting no fatigue, plus his explicit denial that a universal threshold exists, which is why the status moved on the generality and not on the arithmetic. Full marks for noting the irony that his own argument opens by insisting the window is everything and then omits it.
>
> **A3.** The label check goes **first**, because it is two seconds and it ends or redirects the whole investigation. After that, in rough cost order: the form and the landing page, because click-to-form halving on identical clicks points downstream of the ad; then anything that changed in the funnel or the offer in those 19 days; then the pool and the auction, read on CPM and reach; then seasonality, since this window is the US 4 July weekend; then creative, last. The number that argues hardest against fatigue is **link clicks of 119 in both weeks**. The ad kept earning the click at exactly the same rate. A worn-out ad loses the click first. Credit also for frequency falling to 2.10 rather than rising, which is the wrong direction for the diagnosis, and extra credit for naming the holiday weekend without claiming it.
>
> **A4.** Send back: the fatigue line cannot ship yet, because the one check that would support it has not been run. The missing check is whether Meta applied the creative fatigue label to the ads in question. The two accounts where that same evidence shape was filed are **Phoenix Truxx September** (image lane, 230 leads both months, cost per lead $9.40 to $14.94, weekly frequency 2.1 to 2.4, clicks per impression 2.1% to 1.7%) and **SJR Commercial** (the Caesar dump video, $3.20 to $9.18 at 2.33 frequency). What the report may honestly say in the meantime: the ad is old, it has been seen this many times, its cost per lead rose by this much across these weeks, and it costs more to show each month. All of that is observed. The causal sentence is not. Credit for naming the filed rule that already requires this, prove the fatigue before blaming it, and for noting that "our ads wore out" reads well to a client precisely because it points at a fix instead of a buying mistake, which is exactly why it needs the extra gate.
>
> **A5.** A reasonable errand: open one account with a long-running ad that we have already called fatigued, Phoenix Truxx or SJR Commercial, go to the ad level in Ads Manager, and look for a creative fatigue label or flag on the ad row and in whatever diagnostic or recommendation panel the account carries. Write down either where it reads and what it says, with a screenshot, or that it was not present anywhere you looked and the exact places you looked. Then: if the label is found, CR-288 moves up on sourcing because the mechanism is confirmed first-party in our own interface, and the location goes into the codex so anybody can repeat the check. If it is not found, CR-288 gets a recorded guard that the label could not be located in our accounts, and the gate it proposes cannot be operated here, which also means it stops being quotable either way. Why a found label still does not settle it: **[[Meta Delivery & Andromeda#MD-105|MD-105]]**, T2, where a Meta diagnostic surface recommended a setting that was already enabled. A platform label is a signal and not a verdict, so a present label raises the diagnosis and does not prove it.
