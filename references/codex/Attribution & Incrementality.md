# Attribution & Incrementality

## Claims

## Incremental Attribution: The Switch Decision

### AT-001 · Meta incremental attribution flipped from losing to standard attribution (0.8x, 2024-Jul 2025) to beating it (1.26x, Jul 2025-Jul 2026) in Haus geo-holdout tests
Tier: T2 · Status: active
Haus measured IA-vs-standard head-to-head cells with geo-holdout studies, ignoring in-platform metrics entirely. The geometric-mean ratio of incremental ROAS (IA cell over standard cell) was 0.8x in the 2024-July 2025 sample and 1.26x in the July 2025-July 2026 sample. The 2025 sample was about a dozen tests and Haus caveated "not there yet"; the 2026 refresh reversed the verdict. Test mix skewed US: 90% US, 7% UK, 3% Canada.
Sources: Andrew Faris, DTC Brands See 38% Better Meta Ads Performance When They Switch To Incremental Attribution - Olivia, 2026-08-10
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### AT-002 · DTC-only brands see up to 38% better incremental performance from switching to incremental attribution; omnichannel brands see much smaller gains
Tier: T2 · Status: active
In the Haus data, DTC-only brands (no Amazon or retail KPI) showed the biggest IA-over-standard gains, up to 38%, while brands with an omnichannel secondary KPI gained far less. Proposed mechanism is signal completeness: when Meta sees every purchase (all on the dotcom), the lift-trained model optimizes better than when it is blind to Amazon/retail conversions. Faris notes the mechanism is not fully settled since incomplete signal also afflicts standard attribution.
Sources: Andrew Faris, DTC Brands See 38% Better Meta Ads Performance When They Switch To Incremental Attribution - Olivia, 2026-08-10
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### AT-003 · For DTC-only brands the downside of switching to incremental attribution is near zero: worst IA test results were roughly at par with standard
Tier: T2 · Status: active
In the Haus test distribution, the bottom of the IA results for DTC-only brands was basically even with standard attribution while the top end ran roughly 38-40% better, so risk is asymmetric in IA's favor. Faris's rollout rule: under roughly $5k/day spend, just switch (no 50-50 test, you can always switch back); under roughly $10k/day, move your highest-volume-optimized spend straight over to IA and watch. Olivia's counterpoint: the sample is still small, so a larger account should test rather than blanket-switch.
Test-design constraint from a second operator, and it is the reason a partial rollout wastes the effort: go all in or not at all, because what you are measuring is correlation to backend revenue lift, and in-platform attributed figures cannot judge a change to the attribution model itself. Reading a switch to incremental attribution off the incremental attribution column is circular. So the read-out is store-level new-customer revenue over the switch window, not Ads Manager, which also means the window has to be long enough for a store-level number to move. That constraint sits comfortably with Faris's under-$5k/day "just switch" rule and against any 50-50 split test. Asserted as a principle, no test shown.
Sources: Andrew Faris, DTC Brands See 38% Better Meta Ads Performance When They Switch To Incremental Attribution - Olivia, 2026-08-10; Blue Sense Digital, How To Use New Incremental Attribution in Meta Ads, 2025-05-07
Last touched: 2026-08-19

### AT-004 · Faris moved 100% of clients to manual bids with incremental attribution and zero accounts got worse
Tier: T3 · Status: active
After retesting IA in mid-2026, AJF Growth runs all clients on manual bids with incremental attribution; the bulk of spend is cost cap plus IA. Some clients saw no big change but none regressed. The standout effect was total spend volume increasing at the same targets (more ads getting spent) rather than dramatically different ad selection. He has not observed the "good at first, decays over time" pattern some report on X, and speculates that pattern is exposed creative-volume weakness or misread seasonality.
Date the position, because it moved inside three months and the earlier version is the opposite advice. On 20 May 2026 he was not there. His working hypothesis then was that an account already running manual bids, broad targeting, click optimization and past-customer exclusion probably did not need incremental attribution yet, and he had a conversion lift study in flight to find out ([[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-035|AT-035]]). By 10 August 2026 every client was on it. Neither telling shows the result that changed his mind, and the May telling also carries his observation that IA's incrementality adjustment factor ran larger than other tools' while its actual optimization performed worse. Read the August position as a practitioner's conclusion reached over a summer, not as a test read out, and never cite "100% of clients, zero regressions" without the date attached.

### AT-005 · Incremental attribution works worse for value optimization (tROAS) than for volume optimization, likely because far less value-optimized conversion-lift data exists to train on
Tier: T3 · Status: active
When AJF moved highest-value (tROAS) spend to IA the impact was much smaller and sometimes slightly negative, versus clear gains on volume-optimized spend. Mechanism: historically much less total value-optimized spend and fewer value-optimized lift studies feed the IA model, so it is slower to get good there. Same logic predicts weaker international results; the Haus dataset was 90% US so international IA is essentially untested.
Sources: Andrew Faris, DTC Brands See 38% Better Meta Ads Performance When They Switch To Incremental Attribution - Olivia, 2026-08-10
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### AT-006 · Incremental attribution (announced Aug 2024) uses models to predict whether the ad caused the conversion, and now works for both reporting and delivery optimization
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Unlike standard attribution, incremental attribution does not rely on click/view/engage windows; it models causation. Over roughly the year before mid-2026 it evolved from reporting-only into an optimization option, so it can change which conversions delivery optimizes toward. When optimizing under standard attribution Meta can load up on view-through conversions if that raises reported results; incremental optimization removes that incentive.
Rollout and location, from an agency watching it arrive: the control sits at ad set level under Hidden settings, then Attribution. It began appearing around April 2025 and was live on 10-20% of that agency's account book by 7 May 2025, so an account without the setting in mid-2025 was not misconfigured, it was un-rolled-out. Meta's own marketing figure alongside the launch was roughly 27% better new-customer revenue when an ad set uses incremental attribution. That number is a vendor claim recited from memory with nothing shown, and it should never be quoted to a client as measurement.
Sources: Jon Loomer, Most Advertisers Should Only Use Incremental Attribution, 2026-07-29; Blue Sense Digital, How To Use New Incremental Attribution in Meta Ads, 2025-05-07
Last touched: 2026-08-19

### AT-007 · Incremental attribution results converge on click-based attribution: they roughly match the click-through portion of standard results, and decomposed IA sums to about 7-day click plus 1-day engaged view
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Two independent sources land on the same convergence. Loomer finds incremental-attributed conversion counts line up more or less with click-through conversions under standard attribution; accounts propped up by view/engage-through see a large surface drop that is reporting, not performance, and he believes most advertisers who abandoned IA did so because of that perceived drop. Taylor Holiday, from CTC's in-flight CLS studies, observes that decomposing IA's credited conversions and summing 7-day click, 1-day engaged view, and 1-day view converges on roughly what 7-day click plus 1-day engaged view reported, effectively the old 7-day click standard, though the comparison is inexact because Meta added a 5-second no-click engaged-view definition. Implication: IA may be re-deriving the attribution the industry pressed Meta into, now grounded in lift data.

### AT-035 · Contested: what Meta's incremental attribution actually is. Three incompatible mechanisms are on file from one speaker, and they disagree on whether the number is a lift measured on your own account
Tier: T3 · Status: contested
The operating question is single: can you tell a client that the incremental attribution column is a lift measured on their account. Position A says no, Position B says yes, Position C says the question is moot because the number is a click-window average. No Meta documentation is quoted anywhere in any of the three, and no test is shown.
Position A, a competitor-derived factor (Blue Sense Digital, May and June 2026, the considered version). Meta takes conversion-lift experiments run by other advertisers in your niche, reads how incremental those campaign types were, and applies a factor into your account. [[Meta Delivery & Andromeda#MD-034|MD-034]] banks the model-trained-on-lift-studies description. The consequence that matters when reading the number: an incremental attribution figure is evidence about your industry, and it is not a measured lift for your account. Never quote it to a client as a verified lift figure, and never present it as the output of a holdout you ran. The underlying arithmetic he gives for what the factor represents: a 2% purchase rate in the exposed cell against 1% in the holdout means a reported 5 ROAS is really 2.5.
Position B, a permanent 10% holdout (same speaker, June 2025). Meta's native incremental attribution runs a permanent holdout of 10% of your target demographic, so an account using it is "effectively always running holdouts" and getting a real-time incrementality read. If that is right, the number IS a measured lift on your own account and Position A's client-reporting rule is too conservative. Stated from practitioner understanding with no documentation.
Position C, an arithmetic midpoint (same speaker, May 2025). Across the two accounts he pulled up on screen, the incremental figure sat close enough to the average of 1-day click and 7-day click that he suspects it is little more than that average. Full numbers and the consequence in [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-075|AT-075]]. If Position C holds, the setting is a proxy, not an instrument, and neither A nor B describes it.
What survives all three, and it is the only thing to act on: the setting rests entirely on attribution, so it inherits every limitation of an in-platform conversion lift study and cannot be a clean causal read. His own conclusion is to check it against business-level metrics such as new-customer revenue rather than trusting the column. Read it via Columns, Compare attribution setting, Incremental attribution, and use it the way [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-038|AT-038]] uses it, as one rung on a ladder you reconcile against acquisition MER. Do not build a client-facing claim on the mechanism until Meta documents it.
**Searched and not found, 2026-08-20. Record the negative so nobody re-searches this corpus.** Twenty-five transcripts were read in full on this pass. None of them explains the mechanism. No fourth account of how incremental attribution works was offered, and none of the three positions above was resolved or retracted. The mechanism remains undocumented.
What the pass did add, all from Andrew Faris on 20 May 2026, and none of it is a mechanism. First, a negative signal read off an absence: Meta went silent on incremental attribution at its own 2026 Performance Marketing Summit, a year after announcing it there. "Similar to how they didn't say much about AI-generated ads, they also didn't say much about incremental attribution as a tool. I think that was striking, too." He reads the silence against Meta's habit of promoting hard whatever tests well, and infers that internal results are unimpressive. That is an inference from an absence and it is worth exactly what an inference from an absence is worth. Note that Meta was loud at the same event about conversion lift studies and about third-party audit ([[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-052|AT-052]]), which is what makes the quiet on IA legible at all. Second, his own initial test: "Our initial test suggests that the tool is not actually great yet, even though the incrementality adjustment factor on those is probably higher than other tools. It hasn't worked quite as well yet in terms of the actual optimization." A larger incrementality adjustment factor paired with worse actual optimization is a specific and checkable shape. No numbers, no baseline, no design described, so T3. Third, a test in flight: AJF Growth is running a conversion lift study comparing incremental attribution against traditional 7-day click plus 1-day view on prospecting, plus separate lift studies on click-optimized retention spend to find out whether it cannibalises other revenue. Results were pending at that date. **Chase this in a later episode.** It is the only designed test of the IA switch anyone in this corpus has running, and his own position moved sharply between May and August 2026 ([[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-004|AT-004]]).

### AT-075 · In both accounts pulled up on screen, Meta's incremental attribution landed near the arithmetic midpoint of 1-day click and 7-day click, which if it generalizes makes the setting a proxy rather than an independent causal instrument
Tier: T3 · Status: active
The numbers are in [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-074|AT-074]] and they were shown. Account A: 7-day click 4.77, incremental 3.25, with 1-day click running roughly half of 7-day click's attributed revenue. Account B: 7-day click 3.0, incremental 2.07, 1-day click 1.86. In both, incremental sits between the two click windows. The operator's own conclusion, offered as a suspicion rather than a finding: "you could maybe even argue that incremental attribution is just 7-day click plus one day click divided by two. They're just kind of choosing the middle and saying that's what's incremental."
Two consequences if it holds, and they run in opposite directions, which is why this is worth banking rather than dismissing. Against the setting: it would not be an independent causal read at all, it would be a blend of two windows you can already see for free, and every claim resting on it being lift-derived weakens. In favour of the operator: a click-window proxy becomes defensible, so an account without access to incremental attribution can approximate it, and the convergence [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-007|AT-007]] reports between incremental attribution and click-based attribution has an arithmetic explanation rather than a causal one.
Hold the tension honestly and do not pick a winner. This sits directly against the permanent-10%-holdout mechanism the same speaker gives six weeks later, and against the competitor-lift-factor mechanism he gives a year later; all three are recorded in [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-035|AT-035]], which is now contested for exactly this reason. Evidence quality: the two ROAS ladders behind the inference are T2, and the generalization from two accounts to a formula is the speaker's speculation, which is why this claim is T3. The check is cheap and any account with the setting can run it: pull 1-day click, 7-day click and incremental side by side over the same window and see whether incremental lands on the average. Do that before repeating either the midpoint story or the holdout story to a client.
Sources: Blue Sense Digital, How To Use New Incremental Attribution in Meta Ads, 2025-05-07
Last touched: 2026-08-19

## How Much Platform-Reported Numbers Lie

### AT-008 · Meta 7-day-click optimized campaigns underreport true contribution by 10-20%, so true incremental lands near 28-day click, roughly 7-day click times 110-115%, and that multiplier predates the March 2026 click redefinition
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Meta-analyses of Meta lift tests compiled by Haus and Common Thread Collective show 7-day-click campaigns underreport their true incremental contribution by roughly 10-20%, which means 28-day click in-platform reporting lands about right. Faris states the same result as a multiplier: 28-day click revenue in Meta was on average the true incremental return of the ads, roughly 7-day click times 110 to 115%, and the multiplier holds most cleanly when the ad set was optimized on 7-day click. This is his core reason for never using Northbeam/Triple Whale for any client: Meta's own platform reporting tied to click (and now IA) is reliable enough, and that conclusion is itself a regression across all published tests rather than one account's result. Tier downgraded from T2 to T3 on 2026-08-19: both sources are the same speaker citing Haus and CTC meta-analyses from memory, and neither showed the studies.
Two operating points travel with the multiplier. First, a cost cap or bid cap has to be set against your own incrementality factor rather than against raw 7-day-click revenue, because delayed conversions have not fully accrued on a three-day-old ad when buyers keep purchasing for the next 25 days. Second, the expiry date. Faris flags himself that the whole benchmark is pre-March-2026, before Meta changed what counts as a click. [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-036|AT-036]] records that redefinition: 7-day click now requires an outbound click, and likes, comments, shares and Page clicks moved into a new 1-day engaged view bucket. A 110-115% multiplier derived on the old, wider 7-day-click column cannot be applied to the narrower post-March-2026 column without re-deriving it, and applying it unchanged will understate true incremental by whatever share the engagement clicks used to carry. Faris's own updated position is that 7-day click plus 1-day engaged view is roughly right on incrementality, which is the same window pairing [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-036|AT-036]] reaches from the reporting side. Client-report consequence: never quote the 110-115% figure, or a 28-day-click benchmark, against post-March-2026 data as if it were current.
Second expiry, added 2026-08-19, and it is the bigger one. This claim fixes the SIGN of the 7-day-click error: it says the column under-reports, always, so the correction is always upward. [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-074|AT-074]] shows two accounts from one operator where the sign runs both ways, including a shown account reading 4.77 on 7-day click against 3.25 on Meta's own incremental column, which is over-attribution. Read the 110-115% multiplier as a population average across the tests Haus and CTC pooled, and never as a property of the account in front of you. Determine the sign for your own account by reconciling against acquisition MER ([[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-038|AT-038]]) before applying any multiplier in either direction. Tier stays T3: nothing today showed the underlying studies.
Dating note added 2026-08-20. On 20 May 2026 the same speaker restates the under-reporting direction in stronger terms and attaches two scopes to it, click-optimized purchases only and view conversions excluded, both of which this claim currently lacks. That is either a narrowing of this claim or a contradiction of AT-074. The scoping is unconfirmed. The full statement, its date and the unresolved reconciliation are recorded at [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-074|AT-074]]; nothing was shown there either.

### AT-009 · Platform ROAS can sit flat at 20 while the business declines: stripping view-through dropped one large retailer from 20 to 7, and incremental attribution took it to ~1
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Default platform attribution over-credits so heavily that the reported ROAS number stays constant while true acquisition efficiency collapses, hiding a CAC blow-up for months. Example given for a well-known large Australian retailer: reported 20x to 7x without view-through conversions, then roughly 1x on incremental attribution. Rule: a 20 ROAS in Facebook "is not real". Professor Charley T's practitioner-side version of the same point: ROAS is trivially gameable to 100x by restricting delivery to email openers, which will not scale and will not add revenue, so a high ROAS is largely a measure of audience warmth. Actionable form: when an account reports an unusually high ROAS, check what share of delivery went to warm and owned audiences before treating it as a win. [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-038|AT-038]] turns the same audit ladder into a positive test by reconciling each attribution setting against acquisition MER.

### AT-010 · Meta's default attribution credits any same-day ad interaction followed by a conversion, so platform-reported results overstate causal impact
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Meta takes credit if a user interacted with an ad and later converted, even when an email or word-of-mouth actually drove the sale, as long as the ad was served that same day. The right question is incremental: what results did you get that you would not have gotten without the ads. See AT-021 for when incrementality measurement is worth buying.
The email-blast case names the most common instance of this and the fix for it, from a guest on Professor Charley T's channel with Charley agreeing. Send a blast to a list that is also sitting inside your Meta delivery pool and Meta claims credit for the sales it produces: "if you're sending out emails and you don't have that segmented and excluded from your audience, well, Meta is now taking credit for those email blasts where you generate a ton of sales, making it look like you have a good return in ad spend, but in reality, you're still not acquiring those new customers and growing." Two things make it worth banking separately. The distortion is timed, so it arrives in bursts on send days rather than as a steady inflation, which is what makes it readable in a daily chart. And the fix is the same one that fixes half the account problems in this file: segment the email list and exclude it from the delivery audience. Asserted, no numbers shown.

### AT-011 · View-through attribution over-credits ads that were merely scrolled past, misroutes budget, and its conversions are largely non-incremental; turn it off in nearly all campaigns
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Multiple independent sources converge on this. Shiver's mechanism: a prospect already in your DMs scrolls past your static ad for a second, books via the DM link the same day, and the ad takes credit; because the ad with the most attributed conversions gets more budget, view-through inflation misroutes spend to ads that did not cause conversions. His setting: keep 7-day click as primary, 1-day engaged-view is acceptable, disable 1-day view in roughly 99% of campaigns once you get 1-3+ conversions/day, unless pixel fires are so scarce nothing hits the results column. Loomer's independent incrementality argument: an impression served within 1 day of a conversion earns credit even if the person never consciously saw the ad, isolated remarketing audiences make the inflation worse, and a high view/engage-through share is a strong argument the ad did not cause those conversions. Blue Sense Digital adds the specific channel of distortion and a weaker fallback position: view-through over-attributes on existing-customer revenue, so ad sets and creatives that happen to touch the existing base read artificially well and pull budget toward themselves, and if you will not remove 1-day view from the campaign setting, at minimum exclude it from ad-set budget decisions. He runs it as one data-integrity package with 7-day-click reporting at every spend tier and existing customers excluded from cold campaigns. Magnitude of the inflation in [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-037|AT-037]].
The structural mechanism, added 2026-08-19, and it is the strongest argument on this claim. Meta cannot drop a browser cookie from inside the app. A view-through conversion therefore requires a join between an in-app impression and a browser-side event, and that join only exists if a pixel event already fired on that device. So by construction every 1-day-view conversion is a prior site visitor. The claim is not that view-through conversions skew toward warm people. It is that a cold person cannot produce one. He flags his own account of it as a simplification, and no Meta documentation is shown, so it is a reasoned mechanism rather than a documented one. The same operator adds a second, independent inflation channel he observed live: Feed and Reels now render up to four ads simultaneously in one grid view, so several advertisers can each claim a view-through on the same conversion. No attribution data behind that inference.
His setting rule from the same episode: remove 1-day view from the attribution setting entirely for 8 and 9-figure brands, and specifically for any brand with high existing-customer revenue or heavy spend on another channel, which he puts at roughly 95% of the accounts he works with. His worst case, a live account with numbers stated and nothing shown: a brand spending $70,000 a month on Google for three to four years reported 75x ROAS on a broad Meta campaign running $2,000-3,000 a month, essentially all of it 1-day view claiming existing customers and Google-sourced traffic that was already about to convert. Read that as the trigger condition rather than as a benchmark: a small Meta budget sitting beside a large other-channel budget is where view-through inflation goes vertical. The narrow exception where 1-day view is deliberately kept is in [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-076|AT-076]].
His default conclusion from the two accounts in [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-074|AT-074]] is that simply switching the reporting window to 7-day click already removes most of the over-attribution that incremental attribution was built to solve, and that 7-day click is the right model on 90% of businesses. Dissent on file, recorded because it is the only one in this batch: Sam Piliero's agency prescribes standard attribution, 7-day click plus 1-day engagement plus 1-day view, count all conversions, as its build default. He gives no argument for it, no test, and never addresses incrementality, so it is a configuration habit rather than a counter-claim, and it is not treated here as contesting the mechanism.
**View-through does not only inflate the total, it can REVERSE ad set rankings.** This is the sharpest operational form of the claim and it comes from Blue Sense Digital on 26 February 2025: "you look at a specific adset seems to be doing well compared to another one but once you take out the view through conversions it's actually the opposite story and so you're misallocating budget because you don't have clarity over the actual click-through conversions." An inflation you can mentally discount is survivable, because the ordering still holds. A reversal is not, because the decision itself inverts and the losing ad set gets the budget. His instruction in that episode is that 97% of accounts should report on 7-day click only with view-through removed, and his mechanism is the same one this claim already carries: a view with no click or interaction followed by a purchase 24 hours later was more likely driven by an email or a Google ad.
**Dating caveat on that instruction, and it is load-bearing. Half of it is superseded.** February 2025 predates the March 2026 redefinition at [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-036|AT-036]], so "just use 7-day click" now points at a narrower column than the one he meant, one that no longer contains likes, comments, shares or Page clicks. The view-through principle is live and unaffected. The specific column instruction is stale: the current equivalent of what he was prescribing is 7-day click paired with 1-day engaged view, not 7-day click bare. Never apply a pre-March-2026 column instruction to a post-March-2026 account without translating it.
Two operating refinements from Shiver on 30 June 2026, both rules rather than mechanism. Read the compare-attribution split first ([[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-082|AT-082]]), then switch the offending windows off at ad set level, which is where the setting lives. His configuration varies by account maturity: on established accounts he runs click-through only, and on a newer account he keeps engaged view on and turns view-through off, because a new account needs the conversion count more than it needs the precision. It varies by campaign type too: 7-day click for a schedule or purchase campaign, 1-day for a pure opt-in lead campaign. He also reports the prevalence of the failure, which is the reason the rule is worth stating at all: "I've seen so many people have one-day view conversions as their primary conversions. And again, that kind of skews the results and that's not actually what we want."

### AT-012 · Audit attribution mix with the "compare attribution settings" columns: view-through at ~10% or less of conversions is tolerable; if it dominates, duplicate the ad set without view attribution
Tier: T2 · Status: active
Shown client example: 427 message conversions, 390 from 7-day click, 37 (~10%) from view-through, which he leaves running. If instead a large share (his hypothetical: 302 of 427) were view-through, the ads are taking credit for conversions that were not theirs and the ad set should be rebuilt with view-through off. Second shown case, from Blue Sense Digital's Ads Manager screenshots on an audited ecommerce account: switching the reporting column to 7-day click alone dropped ROAS from 2.14 to 1.68 (a 21% cut) and attributed revenue from about $79k to about $62k in one month, roughly $17k of over-attribution, converging toward the ROI the P&L showed. He states roughly 95% of audited accounts never touch the column. His mechanism for why view-through concentrates on returning customers: a view-through can only fire for someone who already hit the site and triggered the pixel, so those conversions cluster on people already deep in the purchase journey. [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-011|AT-011]] now carries the harder version of that mechanism, which is that Meta cannot set a browser cookie from the app, so the join is impossible without a prior pixel fire and the clustering is a certainty rather than a tendency. Tension to hold rather than resolve: [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-037|AT-037]] puts the typical view-through share at 30-50% of reported ROAS across audits, so the ~10% tolerance here describes a healthy exception rather than the norm. Treat 10% as the level you want to find, not the level you should expect.

### AT-073 · Google's conversion window is an attribution lever the advertiser sets: a 90/30/30 window over-credits bottom of funnel, a 7/3/1 window under-credits top of funnel and concentrates credit on brand, and the window should be set from measured time to purchase

The account and its 90/30/30 setting are real and described; no performance delta from changing them is shown anywhere, and the AOV thresholds are a rule of thumb.

### AT-022 · Measurement hierarchy from noise to truth: anecdote < platform attribution < MMM < quasi-experiments (diff-in-diff, regression discontinuity) < geo holdouts, with LTGP:CAC as ground truth
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Platform ROAS is "basic correlation"; MMM is multi-regression correlation adjusting for third-party variables; quasi-experimental designs beat MMM; a geo holdout is the true experiment. LTGP:CAC is the ultimate business truth but cannot allocate credit across 6 platforms, which is what the experimental layers are for. Decisions should be made as close to base reality as the situation allows. The pyramid trades reliability against speed, and the operating consequence is the part to keep: finance-grade metrics (acquisition MER, CAC, profit contribution) move slowly and must be read over longer windows, which is exactly why fast platform metrics keep a job as directional feedback inside a single platform. Incrementality he puts at roughly 20% of operators, the $10M+/month tier with US or multi-market presence. Warning attached: a DIY geo test built in a spreadsheet by doubling spend in one state will almost always conclude "not incremental", because the design is wrong and the test never reaches statistical relevance while overspending or underspending. That warning is contested by the same speaker's own earlier position, which recommends exactly that design as a first gate; both sides are recorded in [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-069|AT-069]]. Named implementations for the quasi-experimental tier are in [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-053|AT-053]], and the geo-lift design in [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-050|AT-050]].
A fuller five-tier statement of the same pyramid, from a third video, names the bottom rung the codex was missing: expert opinion, then simple correlation with no third-variable adjustment, then MMM and multi-touch attribution, then quasi-experiments, then geo lift holdouts. Two boundaries travel with it. Simple before-and-after correlation is genuinely defensible at very low revenue, because there are few confounders when a business goes from $100/day to $1,000/day on one channel, and it stops being defensible at eight and nine figures where many simultaneous efforts obscure the signal. And the top of the growth curve breaks measurement in the opposite way: on an account returning 40x, everything the buyer launched worked, which means the account generated no learnings and no evidence that media was driving the business at all. Fast compounding growth and near-zero growth both defeat correlation, for opposite reasons. The regression discontinuity design that sits in the quasi-experiment tier gets a worked shape here too: set a threshold, treat everyone above it, and compare the cohort clustered just above against the cohort just below, for example giving a free gift to every customer whose prior-year spend exceeded $2,000 and then comparing next-year spend for the $1,980 group against the $2,020 group. That is a quasi-random control an operator can build out of CRM data with no geo split at all. Borrowed academic framework and worked illustrations, no data attached.

### AT-023 · Contested: whether to log and reuse a static incrementality discount factor per campaign type
Tier: T3 · Status: contested
Position A, against static factors (Haus, Aug 2026). Haus abandoned static incrementality factors (a fixed multiplier on platform reporting) because business seasonality and changing incrementality make a single factor from an experiment run months or a year ago unreliable, and humans cannot sensibly weight a 3-month-old test against a same-season year-old test. Their replacement is a machine-learning model that debiases pixel/platform data daily using a cross-customer index of thousands of experiments, weighting a brand's own tests more heavily as it runs more of them. Position B, for static factors (Blue Sense Digital, Feb 2025). Log a discount factor per campaign type from each test and treat the log as the account's bible. His worked hypothetical: a PMax reporting 5 ROAS that delivers only 3x incremental gets a 40% discount factor applied to every future PMax read, so an 8x PMax is understood as returning far less on the next $5k. Brand exclusions are the fix most operators assume closes the gap, and he is saying it does not, which is the operationally important half. The 30-40% itself has no incrementality test behind it, it is a working rule from managing the accounts. Mechanism from the same channel: adding $1,000 to a PMax reporting 5x does not produce $5,000 of extra revenue, and the failure is misattribution rather than a scaling ceiling ([[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-062|AT-062]]).
Position A gains a mechanism from a June 2025 episode: an MMM returns a per-channel ROI estimate every single day, while a geo lift returns one number fixed to a past window that may no longer hold. The likely reconciliation is scale, and it should be attached rather than resolved: static factors are the only option available below the thresholds in [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-021|AT-021]], where a brand can afford perhaps one test a year, and they are known-blunt above it, where enough tests exist to regress. ### AT-071 · A geo lift is sized backwards from the lift it needs to detect: the model states the required percentage lift for significance first, and that number sets the budget
Tier: T2 · Status: active
This is the step that separates a real geo test from the spreadsheet version, and it happens before any money moves. The model computes the lift required to clear significance given the baseline, and the budget is then derived from that requirement. His illustrative figure: needing a 20% lift across four states over four weeks implies roughly $70,000 of ad spend to have a chance of producing it. If you cannot fund the implied budget, the test cannot succeed and should not be run, which is a far more useful gate than a revenue threshold.
The full method executed with real money, in a 21-day Australian holdout. Correlate monthly order volume state against state to find which move together, isolate p-values, cross-correlate to identify the states that best predict all the others, and pick the test cell from that rather than by intuition. Build a baseline from 12 months of daily orders. Compute point estimates at 95% and 90% confidence, giving a lower and upper bound, then convert each bound into the number of incremental orders required to clear it. In this test the baseline was about 2,500 orders over the window and the 95% threshold was a lift of 254 orders. Back the budget out from an assumed CAC and the orders needed to clear the lower bound. Test cell was Victoria plus Queensland, control was the rest of Australia, confidence set at 90%, and new-customer and returning-customer orders were measured separately.

### AT-081 · Incrementality test timing decides whether the test can answer anything: never run one in a high-seasonality month, the window for reverse holdouts opens when growth stalls, and multi-market operation is a measurement asset
Tier: T4 · Status: active
Three design rules from one operator, none tested, all cheap to apply and all easy to get wrong.
Seasonality. Do not run an incrementality test in a month like November. Seasonal noise swamps the effect you are trying to detect, and you end up falling back on platform-attributed numbers, which cannot answer the question the test was for. Run in a flat month, October or September in his example. Direct consequence for us: never schedule a holdout across a promotional period or a Black Friday window, and never accept a client's request to test "while things are busy".
When to run a reverse holdout. The opportunity opens when growth stalls or profitability weakens. During a growth run you made five changes and grew 100%, and you cannot tell which change did what. Once growth stops, you can strip the changes back one at a time and find out which of them was doing nothing. The insight is that a stalled period is a measurement opportunity rather than only a problem, which is a useful thing to say in a client conversation about a flat month.
Market structure as capacity. A brand selling across enough US states can run several holdout cells simultaneously and test multiple things at once. A brand operating in Australia, the US, New Zealand, the UK and the EU can stage any rollout one market at a time and isolate its effect. That makes geographic footprint a measurement asset, not just a revenue one, and it is why the same operator can test on his US clients and not his Australian ones ([[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-051|AT-051]]). Design arguments only, no test cited for any of the three.
Sources: Blue Sense Digital, Should You Should Run TikTok Ads In 2025?, 2025-08-18

### AT-031 · Judge a new ad only on ROAS or cost per conversion after a few days; one high-cost day is noise, but a cost that stays high and climbs over a full week is a real signal
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Meta needs days of real activity to figure out who responds; judging after hours or a single day "tells you almost nothing reliably". The separation rule: 1 day of high cost is a normal fluctuation, a week-plus of high and climbing cost is actionable. All other dashboard metrics should be ignored by beginners because they do not indicate profitability; this is what prevents switching off ads too early. Professor Charley T states the same thing as a hard floor: seven days is the smallest data window that has any value for an optimization decision, and day-to-day data is never actionable. He gives a concrete stability test with it, accepting roughly 34% day-to-day fluctuation as normal while expecting the weekly number to move only a few percent. Heath's variant in the same session ties cadence to conversion volume rather than to a fixed window: an account at thousands of conversions a day can be read in three hours, an account at six conversions a week may need a month to judge a new ad, and a statistical-significance calculator is the tiebreaker.
Faris makes the same point as the first bucket of his account-diagnosis checklist, which is to ask whether the problem exists at all. Probabilistic prediction produces genuine day-to-day variation, and the lower the total conversion count the more randomness shows up, so a low-spend account is more likely to have a statistical noise problem than a delivery problem. His live example: a client at roughly $100 average order value on modest spend with ad sets split across products, so individual ad sets land on a couple of purchases a day and some days show zero. That is baseline variation. He notes it resolves over the long view, particularly on manual bids. Client-report consequence: at low conversion counts, a zero-conversion day or a single bad ad set is not a finding, and reporting it as one manufactures a problem the client will then ask you to fix.

### AT-034 · Proposed boundary on incremental attribution: skip it for beginners and for advertisers without an existing customer base, because the payoff is filtering conversions warm buyers would have made anyway
Tier: T3 · Status: active

His stated exclusions are beginner advertisers and advertisers without much of a customer base, on the grounds that there is little non-incremental conversion to filter and the reporting drop is discouraging without the upside. He also states the mechanism behind the pessimism: incremental attribution is fairly accurate inside the window but the window is 7-day click, 1-day view and 1-day engaged view by default, so considered purchases that close later are not captured at all, and results genuinely understate contribution rather than merely looking worse. Warning attached: expect worse-looking ROAS and cost per purchase, and expect the emotional reaction to that to be the real reason advertisers abandon the setting.
This boundary is a T3 assertion and it pushes against the T2 evidence already on file. [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-003|AT-003]] finds the downside near zero for DTC-only brands (worst results roughly at par with standard), and [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-004|AT-004]] reports Faris moving 100% of clients to incremental attribution with zero accounts getting worse. Take the tiered evidence over the caution unless the account is genuinely new. The pessimism mechanism itself is consistent with [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-007|AT-007]].
A second, independently reasoned boundary from Blue Sense Digital lands close to Heath's and is sharper about the mechanism: do not switch a low conversion count, high AOV account to incremental attribution, because most of that account's conversions are not incremental and the ad set can end up with almost no signal left to learn from. The failure mode is signal starvation at the optimizer, not a discouraging column. Note the honesty: the speaker labels every recommendation in that episode "purely theoretical based", and it was recorded days after the setting reached his account book, so it is a prediction rather than a result. It is worth holding because it names a concrete testable condition, low conversion count plus high AOV, where the T2 evidence in [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-003|AT-003]] and [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-004|AT-004]] genuinely has no coverage.

### AT-066 · "Meta is broken" is almost never the right diagnosis, and the only fault Faris accepts as real is a short overspend bug visible as a sudden spend spike at a strange CPM
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Faris has personally managed well over 100 Meta accounts, spending from tens of thousands per day up to over $100,000 per day at peak, and says he essentially never sees a genuine platform fault. The one exception he grants is overspend bugs, which are real, last a couple of hours, and show up clearly in the data as a sudden spend spike at an unusual CPM, most visibly on manual bids. That signature is the whole test: if the spend curve and CPM do not show it, the platform-fault explanation has no evidence behind it. On a recent audit where even Meta staff suggested Meta had broken something, he put user error at 95% plus.
The second half is a deliberate bias in diagnosis rather than a claim about the platform. If the explanation is platform failure there is no next action beyond support tickets, rep emails and rebuilding the account, all of which extend the bleed, so his agency has effectively outlawed saying it to clients. A client who blames Meta cannot fix anything. A client who assumes the fault is theirs can. He also offers it as a vetting signal for brand owners, since an agency that regularly blames the platform is displaying a lack of platform knowledge. Client-report consequence, and this one is load-bearing: never write "Meta broke" or "a platform issue" into a report without the spend-spike and CPM evidence attached, because it is both the least likely explanation and the only one that comes with no plan for next period. Note the honest limit, which is that "I never see it" from one agency is not evidence that platform faults are rare across all accounts, and the diagnostic bias is argued from usefulness rather than from accuracy. ### AT-067 · A post newly promoted into a scaling campaign reads artificially cheap on cost per follow, because it carries organic follows and follows earned in the earlier capped campaign
Tier: T3 · Status: active
A measurement trap the speaker flags inside his own follower-growth method. When a post is moved into the scaling campaign it brings with it every follow it generated organically and every follow it generated during the first, budget-capped campaign, while the cost side counts only the new spend. Its cost per follow therefore reads better than the incremental cost of the money you just added, and the newest post will usually look like the best one. His control is to track spend and follows for every post continuously and judge on the average across all posts rather than on any single post's headline number.
The generalizable form matters more than the follower use case: any metric that divides a cumulative lifetime numerator by a fresh spend denominator will flatter whatever you just started. The same shape appears whenever an ad with prior organic reach is put behind budget, and it is the mirror of the warm-pool inflation in [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-043|AT-043]]. Operator rule: before promoting an organic post, record its follow and engagement counts at that moment and measure only the delta after spend begins. Practitioner observation, no data quantifying the distortion.

### AT-068 · Discard the last couple of days of any trailing 30-day window before drawing a conclusion, because conversions have not finished accruing and platform revenue is neither accurate nor complete
Tier: T3 · Status: active
The tail of a last-30-days view is always distorted and should never carry a scaling decision. He pairs the rule with two reminders that are the reason for it: Meta is not the only source of revenue, and platform attribution is neither fully accurate nor inclusive of all the money the business makes. The practical instruction is to cut the final days off the window and read the stable portion.
The mechanism is delayed conversion, which is banked elsewhere in stronger form. [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-008|AT-008]] notes that buyers keep purchasing for weeks and that conversions have not fully accrued on a three-day-old ad, and [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-016|AT-016]] lists delayed and cross-device conversion as structural leaks. Client-report consequence, and this is the one that changes how we write: a period-end report is by construction reading the most incomplete days of the period as if they were final, so a week-over-week or month-over-month comparison always flatters the earlier window and penalises the current one. Either cut the tail from both windows so the comparison is like for like, or say in the report that the final days are still filling in and mark what that makes uncertain. He gives no quantification of how large the distortion is or exactly how many days to cut, so treat "a couple of days" as a floor and check the account's own conversion-lag curve where the data allows it.

### AT-080 · Three on-site leading indicators read a new channel's traffic quality before purchase data lands: average session duration, add-to-cart rate, and pop-up form fill rate, each split by traffic source
Tier: T3 · Status: active
The gap this fills. When a new channel switches on, purchase data is too thin and too lagged to judge it for weeks, and the platform's own conversion column is the thing under suspicion. These three read within days, they come from the site rather than the platform, and they must be segmented by source or the new channel's traffic gets averaged into the existing baseline and disappears.
Average session duration first. His thresholds: sub-three-second bounces indicate bot or low-intent traffic, ten to fifteen seconds indicates real intent. Add-to-cart rate second, which catches traffic that browses but never engages commercially. Pop-up form fill rate third, and this one carries a specific trap worth holding on its own: fill rate can hold flat or improve while conversion off the back of it collapses, leaving discount-claiming non-buyers sitting dormant on the email list. So a healthy fill rate is not evidence of quality unless the downstream conversion is read with it.
The case that produced them, on the same account as [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-077|AT-077]] and [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-078|AT-078]]: adding the channel raised sessions 63%, roughly 11,000 extra sessions against 9,800 platform clicks, while sessions with cart additions fell in absolute terms, sessions reaching checkout fell further, sessions completed fell from 969 to 721, and site conversion rate dropped 54%. Traffic up, every quality measure down. That pattern is the reason the indicators exist. The mechanism is demonstrated in the shown account; the three-second and ten-to-fifteen-second thresholds are recalled and asserted, not measured on screen, which is why this sits at T3. Operator form: before launching any new channel, snapshot these three metrics by source, then read them weekly against the pre-launch baseline instead of arguing about the platform's conversion column.

### AT-084 · Two checks before believing anyone's revenue-chart case study: which months the window covers, and whether the line is topline revenue or profit
Tier: T4 · Status: active
Professor Charley T reacting to another creator's case study, a Shopify revenue chart climbing to $758k presented as proof a tactic worked. Two objections, both structural, both cheap to apply to anything: "If you're one of us, you know it's [censored] BLACK FRIDAY. WHAT ARE YOU TALKING ABOUT? AND THAT'S NOT EVEN PROFIT. THAT'S TOPLINE SALES. YOU COULD HAVE LOST MORE MONEY. YOU COULD HAVE SPENT $10 million to see that bump."
First check, the window. The chart covers October and November, so the entire rise is confounded with Black Friday, the largest seasonal effect in ecommerce. A chart whose lift sits inside a known promotional period carries no information about the tactic being sold. This is the reading-side twin of [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-081|AT-081]], which says never to RUN an incrementality test in a high-seasonality month, and it is the same logic pointed at somebody else's evidence.
Second check, the line. A topline revenue chart with no spend line and no margin cannot show profit, so a business could have bought that curve at a loss and the chart would look identical. [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-058|AT-058]] is the general form of the objection.
Both checks apply to our own client reporting, not only to other people's case studies. Never put a revenue chart in a report over a window containing a promotion without saying so in the same breath, and never let a topline curve stand in for a profit claim. Reasoning about someone else's chart, nothing measured, which is why it is T4. Its value is as a two-item checklist, not as a finding.
