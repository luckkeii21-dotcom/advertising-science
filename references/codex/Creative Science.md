# Creative Science

Creative as the primary performance lever: diversity, fatigue, volume economics, testing structures, hooks.

Part of the [[00-Codex|Advertising Science Codex]]. Claims follow the tier system (T1 docs, T2 shown test, T3 practitioner, T4 theory).

## Why creative now decides delivery

### CR-001 · Meta built Andromeda explicitly to absorb a rapidly growing ad-creative volume driven by Advantage+ automation and GenAI creative tools
Tier: T1 · Status: active
Meta's engineering post states Advantage+ automation increases the number of eligible ads, and GenAI creative generation is expected to grow creative volume significantly: more than a million advertisers used its generative AI tools to create more than 15 million ads in a month. The retrieval engine was scaled so this larger, more diverse candidate pool can be personalized per person. This is the system-level reason creative diversity gives an account more surface area in retrieval.
Sources: Engineering at Meta, Meta Andromeda post, 2024-12-02

## Avatar and persona positioning

### CR-005 · Niching the same product's creative to one untapped avatar sharply cuts acquisition cost and unlocks scale
Tier: T2 · Status: active
Multiple independent sources. Nick Theriot showed a handbag account where generalized women-targeted creative at just under $1,000/day produced a $73 cost per purchase; the same product niched to women who crochet/knit at the same spend produced $35, roughly half. Fraser Cottrell describes the same mechanism (a heavy-duty soap repositioned from builders to dirt bikers) as giving Meta clean signals into an uncontested delivery pool. Nick's caveat: the blue-ocean avatar must still have a large TAM or the win does not scale.
Sources: Nick Theriot, The Most Braindead Way To Find Winning Products in 2026, 2026-08-17; Fraser Cottrell, How to Make Meta Ads So Good People Can't Stop Watching, 2026-08-16; Nick Theriot, I Spend $100k/Day On Facebook Ads, 2026-08-03
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### CR-006 · Repositioning a product onto a higher-value avatar or a new desire can multiply profit several-fold without changing the product
Tier: T2 · Status: active
Breath-spray client repositioned from college kids to cigar smokers (more disposable income, product reads as cheap to them): monthly sales went from ~$270k to $472k and monthly profit from ~$28k to ~$90k, with the avatar shift moving profit from ~$30k to over $100k/month within 30-60 days; dashboards were shown. A separate client whose anxiety-positioned product plateaued at $100k/month scaled to $1M/month after repositioning as a fun girls' night activity, a simultaneous avatar and desire change (numbers told, not shown). Blue Sense adds the downstream argument from a Gruns teardown: one SKU (daily greens gummies) is run as at least six distinct products (gut health for bloating, GLP-1 support, fiber for digestion, hair health, cognition and focus, multivitamin for business professionals), and each persona unlocks an offer that would be irrelevant to 90% of buyers under generic positioning (a GLP-1 partner discount, a free extra 30 days justified by a 120-day hair-health study). Repositioning is therefore the precondition for offer differentiation, not only a creative lever, and its contribution-margin impact is zero or positive. Competitor teardown, asserted, no performance data shown.
Sources: Nick Theriot, The Most Braindead Way To Find Winning Products in 2026, 2026-08-17; It Took Me 10 Years to Learn This Secret Facebook Ads Scaling Strategy, 2026-07-24; I spend $100k a day on Facebook Ads, 2026-08-14; Blue Sense Digital, How 8 and 9 Figure Brands Build Offers Without Discounting, 2026-06-08
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### CR-007 · Niche one level deep only; TAM size caps what any creative can scale
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Winning avatars are one broad attribute (cigar smokers, women who crochet), never stacked qualifiers. His illustration: ~1.2 million US doctors vs ~400 neurosurgeons; a hook calling out neurosurgeons has at most 400 qualified viewers and can never scale regardless of quality. Validate the avatar has real online mass before committing: multiple TikTok videos on the niche with 25K+ views, ideally 100K+. Blue Sense adds the operating consequence: persona specificity sets a maximum daily spend ceiling for the ad, so a hook calling out "e-commerce CFOs in Australia owning over $100 million" addresses maybe 1,000-2,000 people and $10,000/day behind it just hits the same thousand repeatedly. His prescription is not broader callouts, because broad callouts demand creative good enough to resonate with everyone. Stay hyper-specific, find the persona's daily spend ceiling, hold spend there and rotate creative into it; personas that churn (marathon trainees) refill. He puts genuinely broad top-of-funnel unaware ads out of reach below roughly $50M revenue.
Sources: Nick Theriot, The Most Braindead Way To Find Winning Products in 2026, 2026-08-17; How To Create Facebook Ad Hooks That Scale In 2026, 2026-07-27; Blue Sense Digital, How to Scale an eCommerce Brand Profitably in 2026: The Full System, 2026-06-15
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### CR-008 · Self-target with scenario dissection at the psychology level, not demographic callouts in the hook
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Pre-Andromeda hooks like "hey, you a woman 30 to 60" are too broad now. The working method dissects a familiar situation ("It's 11am, you're starving, you grab a $5 protein bar, 300+ calories, you eat half and it goes stale in your bag") so only matching sub-personas recognize themselves and keep watching. That both filters viewers and feeds Meta precise delivery signals. Blue Sense states the same rule harder across two videos: "Persona call outs are super overrated and it's because no one wants to hear themselves called out. People want to hear the problem that they're actually dealing with." He reports first-person that ads opening "agency owners who need X, listen up" get skipped, while a specific problem agitation holds him. Second, delivery-side reason: Meta auto-transcribes every ad, so a literal persona callout routes delivery to that persona even when the rest of the creative has nothing to do with them, wasting the impression. Two caveats he does not resolve. In the same transcript a VSL breakdown praises a persona-callout opener ("You have IBS, leaky gut, or bloating?"), so the rule is not absolute. And it sits against [[Creative Science#CR-070|CR-070]], which puts the avatar callout as job one. The reconcilable version: a callout does route delivery but underperforms as a hook, so put the callout inside the problem framing rather than in a literal "if you're a mom aged 30-35" opener.
Sources: Fraser Cottrell, How to Make Meta Ads So Good People Can't Stop Watching, 2026-08-16; Blue Sense Digital, How to Scale an eCommerce Brand Profitably in 2026: The Full System, 2026-06-15; Blue Sense Digital, Meta Ads Creative Strategy in 2026: The Full System, 2026-05-11
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### CR-010 · In a sophisticated market with no unique mechanism, avatar trial-and-error takes about 3 months before one unlocks scale
Tier: T3 · Status: active

Contrast: the cigar-spray client's avatar disconnect was obvious on audit and fixed within 30-60 days. The clearer the creative-avatar mismatch, the faster the fix.

### CR-011 · Effective TAM equals the number of category entry points a product supports
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Beyond raw buyer headcount, TAM widens with the number of distinct value propositions a product can carry (the Mars Man hormone supplement spans brain health, heart health, training, strength). A product with many entry points scales faster because message variety, the core creative lever, is structurally available; a niche product like a $200 baseball-glove wallet caps out regardless of execution.
Sources: Andrew Faris, You're Working Hard On The Wrong Problem, 2026-07-27
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### CR-136 · Mass desire cannot be created, only transferred, so a campaign whose job is to educate a market into wanting the outcome is misbuilt
Tier: T4 · Status: active
Cited from Eugene Schwartz and used as a gate before any campaign: "If you're trying to educate your market, just give up. Instead, you should channel the existing desire into your product." If the ad's job is to convince people they should want the outcome at all, the problem is the offer, not the copy. Operationally this kills category-education campaigns for small advertisers and pushes them toward an existing high-intensity desire the product can be attached to. It sits upstream of the awareness-stage rules in [[Creative Science#CR-021|CR-021]] and [[Creative Science#CR-022|CR-022]]: awareness stage decides how you talk, desire transfer decides whether you should be in the market at all. It also pairs with [[Creative Science#CR-083|CR-083]], where the same desire varies in intensity by season. Asserted as a principle from a 1966 book, no test attached.
Sources: Mark Builds Brands, 8 years of marketing advice in 70 minutes, 2026-03-21
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### CR-148 · Two ads pointing at the same page recruit different buyers: the same jacket returned $178 AOV from a discount-led static and $320 from a styling ad
Tier: T3 · Status: active
On a women's clothing account, two ads sending traffic to the identical page produced an AOV of $178 and $320. Mechanism: the creative selects the buyer, so a price-led ad recruits price-conscious single-item buyers and a styling or problem-solving ad recruits multi-item buyers. He verified the composition with custom conversion events showing one ad sold only the jacket while the other sold the jacket plus two or three items. Numbers asserted with the mechanism named, not shown on screen.
Operator move: report AOV and basket composition per ad, not only CPA and ROAS, and treat AOV divergence between ads as an audience-quality signal rather than noise. This is transaction-level evidence for MD-001 and [[Creative Science#CR-005|CR-005]], that the creative is the targeting, and it is a reason a lower-ROAS ad can be the better one to fund.
Sources: Professor Charley T, The BEST Facebook Ads Strategy for 2026 Post Andromeda, 2025-12-27
Last touched: 2026-08-18

## Hooks

### CR-012 · How much of a hook is the visual versus the copy?
Tier: T3 · Status: contested
Nick Theriot: for photo ads the hook is the image and the text hook together; for video it is what people see AND hear in the first 3 seconds. In his $130-watch example the text hook is only about 25% of the hook and the visuals (luxury box, lighting, slow-mo close-ups) are 75%, so judging a hook on copy alone misses most of the stopping power. Mark Builds Brands inverts the weighting: "roughly the copy is going to be about 80%. The next 15% is going to be the scroll stopper, and the final 5% will be the audio." He calls audio the smallest needle mover and treats the human-versus-AI voiceover choice as near-irrelevant. Neither side showed comparative data; both are practitioner assertions. The split may be category-dependent, since Theriot's example is a visually-demonstrable ecom product and Mark's accounts lean lead-gen. Mark's visual-selection test is usable regardless of the weighting: a scroll stopper qualifies only if a naive viewer says out loud "what did I just watch" after seeing it.
Sources: Nick Theriot, How To Create Facebook Ad Hooks That Scale In 2026, 2026-07-27; Mark Builds Brands, 8 years of marketing advice in 70 minutes, 2026-03-21
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### CR-013 · A scalable hook needs four properties: calls out the ideal customer, intrigues curiosity, addresses a large TAM, and differs from every competitor
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Stop the scroll of the ideal customer, create curiosity to keep watching, target a desire held by a large total addressable market, and differentiate visually or verbally from competitors. Hooks that copy the niche's standard pattern do not work even when well executed. One example ad meeting all four spent almost $200,000.
Sources: Nick Theriot, How To Create Facebook Ad Hooks That Scale In 2026, 2026-07-27
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### CR-014 · The highest-leverage 2026 hook pattern is desired outcome WITHOUT the mainstream solution's hated symptom
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Across $4.3M spent in 30 days, the lead hook archetype calls out what the audience wants but cannot have due to a symptom of the current mainstream solution: "If you're over 40, here's how to lose weight without needles" (vs Ozempic's needle fear). Formula: [outcome] + without + [the specific thing people hate about the dominant solution], peeling off the segment blocked by that objection.
Sources: Nick Theriot, I Spent $4.3M on Facebook Ads Last Month, 2026-08-10
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### CR-015 · An 18-archetype hook library is scaling in 2026: problem/solution, failed-solutions list, David-vs-Goliath, desired state, news-style, testimonial, before/after, mechanism-question and more
Tier: T3 · Status: active
The scaling set includes: problem then solution ("Cigar breath, never again with this"); list of failed solutions then yours; David vs Goliath ("four cigars later and she doesn't smell a thing"); show desired state; offer and offer-ending FOMO; problem as question; problem as news story; testimonial; before-and-after; "not hard if you have the right system"; current solution's flaw; competitor superiority for stage-4 sophistication; ditched-mainstream-solution; speed to result ("30 seconds and she can't smell a thing"); new mechanism plus superiority; mechanism-question ("what would happen to your acne if the cells fueling it were shut down?"). Compiled from ads that scaled during a $4.3M month.
Sources: Nick Theriot, I Spent $4.3M on Facebook Ads Last Month, 2026-08-10
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### CR-016 · Making the viewer FEEL an emotion through a story beats naming the emotion; deliberate rage-bait hooks produced some of his best ads ever
Tier: T3 · Status: active
The failure mode is selling to an emotion directly ("do you feel sad that dogs are in shelters?"); the working version shows a story that induces the feeling, then introduces the product as the resolution. He uses an emotion-wheel prompt to generate hooks that intentionally upset the ideal customer ("Most cigar guys think their wife is overreacting. She's not."), then gives the solution.

### CR-017 · Polarizing belief-based hooks self-select the audience, then a trusted proxy executes the belief change
Tier: T3 · Status: active
A sunscreen street-interview ad opens "sunscreen is worse than the sun", keeping anti-sunscreen viewers and scrolling everyone else away, cleaning the audience Meta optimizes into. Because the viewer identifies with the interviewee, his on-camera flip to accepting the natural-ingredient product transfers the belief change. Under-education kills the same structure: a dandruff-shampoo ad failed because the antifungal mechanism got 2 sentences instead of the ~15 extra seconds it needed.

### CR-018 · A low hook rate does not mean a losing ad; judge winners on CPA and CTR, not thumbstop
Tier: T2 · Status: active
His Aloha problem-solution ad had only a 21% hook rate but a 2.2% click-through rate and a strong CPA, because the fewer people who stayed were precisely the interested ones. An ad can deliberately filter hard at the top and still acquire customers cheaply; hook rate is not the winner/loser metric.

### CR-019 · Reframe price against what the customer already spends, not against competing solutions
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Anchor the price to an existing approved purchase: "for less than the cost of one premium cigar, never hear you smell like an ashtray again." The reframe converts an unfamiliar price comparison into a familiar, already-approved spend.

### CR-020 · A specific exaggerated claim beats a generic category claim
Tier: T3 · Status: active
When every competitor says "lose fat", saying "lose 18 lb in 4 weeks" makes yours the only concrete ad and the consumer's choice becomes trivial. Escalation applies inside creative styles too: when everyone shows a streamer reacting, the next winner smashes the computer with a bat, because the baseline reaction is stale.

### CR-021 · For problem-aware markets, lead with the new mechanism, not the problem, and introduce the mechanism before naming the product

Tier: T4 · Status: active
From Breakthrough Advertising: at lower sophistication call out the desire ("how to lose 47 lb in 30 days or your money back"); in a more sophisticated marketplace lead with the product/solution ("new wonder drug for losing weight") then bridge to desire. Book-derived framework, no test data.

### CR-123 · Grade every hook on five criteria (clarity, relevance, novelty, specificity, credibility) and accept a couple scored well rather than all five
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Definitions as given. Clarity: a stranger understands the subject inside 3 seconds, not a deliberate confusion play. Relevance: problem agitation, explicitly not a persona callout (see [[Creative Science#CR-008|CR-008]]). Novelty: "purple ocean" white space, a repositioning that is adjacent-but-novel rather than a true blue ocean with no proven demand, tested by asking whether the hook has been done a million times. Specificity: numbers, names, quantified outcomes, which is where authority comes from. Credibility: usually carried by the visual, a domain-relevant figure appearing on screen rather than a generic lab coat, not by the copy. A good hook does not need 10/10 on all five. Stated across two videos by the same operator; no scoring data shown, so this is a rubric, not a measured model. This is the same five-criteria set the workspace hook scorecard runs, and it is a distinct framework from the four properties in [[Creative Science#CR-013|CR-013]].
Sources: Blue Sense Digital, How to Scale an eCommerce Brand Profitably in 2026: The Full System, 2026-06-15; Blue Sense Digital, Meta Ads Creative Strategy in 2026: The Full System, 2026-05-11
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### CR-124 · Hooks carry the widest performance delta and each new hook is a separate creative entity, so build many hooks against few bodies and refresh fatigued winners with hook swaps
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Two linked mechanics from the same operator. Production allocation: about 80% of viewers never watch past the hook, and the spread available from swapping hooks is wider than the spread from swapping bodies, so at equal production time 2 bodies x 15 hooks beats 5 bodies x 2 hooks. His caveat is explicit: if the body scripting is weak, one body may accidentally be the winner and the leverage read is wrong. Three hook layers are independently testable, visual, audio, and overlaid copy including primary text. Shoot six hook takes at recording time; it costs a few extra minutes. Delivery mechanic: a new hook gets its own creative ID, so Meta will not bundle it with the source asset the way it bundles near-duplicates, and it reaches a novel audience despite the remaining 87 seconds being identical. His reported result: ads that fatigued after $50-100k of spend absorbed another $100k at the same efficiency after 50 new hooks were rotated on, and the 50 hooks took about an hour to shoot. Numbers asserted, no dashboard shown. Second use: rescue an ad that failed on hook rate while its body scripting was sound. The bundling side of this is [[Creative Science#CR-028|CR-028]] and MD-003.
Sources: Blue Sense Digital, Meta Ads Creative Strategy in 2026: The Full System, 2026-05-11; Blue Sense Digital, How to Scale an eCommerce Brand Profitably in 2026: The Full System, 2026-06-15
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### CR-145 · Naming the live platform trend inside the hook lowers CPM and cost per lead, and the angle is perishable
Tier: T3 · Status: active
He runs the trend of the moment as one angle slot inside a standing batch, same 60-second script structure across five pain points or angles, one of which is the current trend (his examples: "yapping videos" and Claude/AI). His ads with "Claude" in the hook are outperforming the rest of the account on CPM and cost per lead. Claimed mechanism is the same as organic trend-riding: familiarity buys cheaper attention, and it shows up in the CPM column first. No dashboard shown, single account. Caveat surfaced in the same conversation and not by him: he and his co-host both describe audience saturation and active "not interested" tagging on AI content, so the window is short. Treat the trend slot as a rotating angle, not a permanent one.
Sources: Dr. Matt Shiver, Organic vs Paid Ads: What's Actually Working In 2026, 2026-06-25
Last touched: 2026-08-18

## Creative volume and fatigue

### CR-024 · How many net-new creatives does a spend level actually require?
Tier: T3 · Status: contested
Fraser Cottrell's brackets: $10-20k/month needs only 3-4 net-new creatives monthly (ads fatigue slower at low spend; overloading starves every ad). $20-50k: 5-6/month, adding TOF educational formats. $50-70k: the pivotal bracket where a winner lasts only 4-6 weeks, requiring 10-15 net-new concepts monthly plus variations. $70-100k: creative is the main scaling lever (bidding and budget mechanics matter far less), ~20 concepts monthly, offer testing starts. $100-250k: ~30/month floor, and poor quality now triggers a delivery penalty spiral (bad ads get served to fewer people, CPA rises). $250-500k+: hard floor of high-40s to 50+ net-new monthly with fully systematized creative ops.
Blue Sense Digital's rule runs roughly 5-10x higher at every tier: one new ad per $1,000 of monthly spend in AUD, so $30k/month means 30 new ads, $100k means 100, and $250-500k means 250-500. His derivation back-propagates from revenue: required creatives = new-customer revenue target divided by (mean lifetime spend per ad x average 7-day-click ROAS). Worked example: 500 ads over a year against $500k spend gives $1,000 mean spend per ad; at 3x ROAS the expected value per ad is $3,000; a $300k monthly new-customer revenue target therefore needs 100 creatives per month. He names two known gaps in his own model. It ignores time-to-realise, since an ad may take four months to spend its $1,000. And it averages winners and losers together, so a real forecast has to model both separately. Volume should flex month to month with the revenue forecast rather than sitting at a fixed number.
An earlier formulation from the same operator constrains volume from the opposite side: creative tests per month = (Meta budget x testing allocation %) / (3 x AOV), where 3x AOV is the minimum spend a single creative needs before a verdict. That caps how many creatives a given budget can honestly evaluate, and it cannot be reconciled with the 1-ad-per-$1,000 rule at low AOV without raising the testing allocation.
Neither side showed a controlled comparison. The positions do not reconcile on the number: Fraser's $250-500k floor of 50+ is five to ten times below Blue Sense's 250-500 for the same spend. What both agree on is that volume must be derived from spend rather than fixed by habit.
Sources: Fraser Cottrell, Creative Volume | How Many Ads You Actually Need at Each Spend Level, 2026-08-02; Blue Sense Digital, Meta Ads Creative Strategy in 2026: The Full System, 2026-05-11; Blue Sense Digital, How to Scale an eCommerce Brand Profitably in 2026: The Full System, 2026-06-15; Blue Sense Digital, How To Structure Your Meta Ads for Profit (Free Live Webinar), 2025-02-19
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### CR-025 · Run creative as a fixed-cadence production pipeline, not ad-hoc creation
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Multiple independent sources. Ben Heath: at high spend, lock ~3 proven content types and produce ~12 new creatives/week to prevent everything fatiguing at once (ROAS at a quarter of two weeks prior). Nick Theriot: 5 new ad ideas per week x 3 creatives = 15 creatives per client per week, on a continuous 2-week loop where the next ~15 concepts are being built while the current batch tests. The pipeline, not any individual ad, is the fatigue defense.
Sources: Ben Heath, $100 vs $100,000 Facebook Ads Strategy, 2026-08-12; Nick Theriot, My Full Process For Scaling Facebook Ad Clients In 2026, 2026-07-20; NEW Customers or ALL Customers with Meta Ads In 2026?, 2026-08-05
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### CR-026 · Raw net-new volume vs multiplying proven winners
Tier: T3 · Status: contested
Sam Piliero (80+ brands, $30M+/month): raw creative volume does not matter; his agency stopped chasing volume and instead multiplies quality, using AI to produce ~20 iterations per run from the top five highest-spending image ads plus competitor inspiration, which yields hit rate AND volume simultaneously. Fraser Cottrell and Ben Heath hold the opposing emphasis: high spend requires large net-new concept counts on a fixed cadence (Fraser: ~30/month at $100-250k, 50+ at $250-500k; Ben: ~12/week at $100k/month), because at scale everything fatigues fast regardless of quality. The positions partly reconcile (iterations are still launches), but the prescriptions differ on whether net-new concept count is the KPI.
Blue Sense Digital offers a third position that reconciles them through a quality floor. His leverage hierarchy, stated identically in two videos, is volume first, then concept quality, hook strength, editing quality, UGC talent, and storytelling last. The argument: hand ten senior creative strategists five objectively good ads and ask which one won, and none can beat guessing, they will only rationalize. Since prediction above the bar is impossible, define the bar (his shorthand is a 7/10 ad), then maximise volume above it. He rejects the naive version explicitly: he observed two accounts launching 2,000+ ads a month while barely reaching $4M/month spend and staying unprofitable, because that volume cleared no concept, hook or format standard. Operator action under this position is to invest in defining and teaching what the bar looks like, then remove throughput constraints. Asserted, no controlled test.
Sources: Sam Piliero, Do THIS and the Meta Andromeda Algorithm Will LOVE You!, 2026-08-14; Fraser Cottrell, Creative Volume, 2026-08-02; Ben Heath, $100 vs $100,000 Facebook Ads Strategy, 2026-08-12; Blue Sense Digital, How to Scale an eCommerce Brand Profitably in 2026: The Full System, 2026-06-15; Blue Sense Digital, Meta Ads Creative Strategy in 2026: The Full System, 2026-05-11
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### CR-027 · Does audience exhaustion actually happen at normal spend?
Tier: T3 · Status: contested

Every high-budget account runs into fatigue and the fix is a constant refresh pipeline, not better individual ads.
Professor Charley T denies the population-exhaustion version outright across two videos. First: "I used to spend over a million dollar a day for single accounts. His conclusion is that what operators call audience fatigue is misallocated effort, too much spend at the bottom of the funnel and not enough at the top, and that interfering slows the system's discovery of new people. Neither side showed data. ### CR-029 · Injecting iterations of a proven winner around the fatigue point flattens the decay curve and extends profitable life

### CR-117 · Every ad has a fixed lifetime spend capacity, mean roughly $700-$1,500, and pushing daily spend harder shortens its life instead of raising the total

### CR-118 · Only 5-10% of ads ever hold spend, so a concept needs 10-20 ads before it can be called dead

### CR-032 · Width/depth testing: prove a new message with 2-3 formats, then multiply the winner across every format
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Width = one winning core message spread across creative styles (UGC video becomes b-roll+music, an animation variation, long-form primary text with static). A brand-new message gets only 2-3 creative types; if it works it graduates to full-width variation, if not it is scrapped. Depth = repeatedly advertising the same core message progressively better over months. Messaging methodology from Breakthrough Advertising.

### CR-033 · Allocate 80% of creative tests to iterating the winning message, 20% to genuinely new desires, avatars, awareness and sophistication stages
Tier: T3 · Status: active
80% of testing time, tested creatives, and production investment doubles down on the current winning core message via iterations and variations. The remaining 20% explores totally new desires, avatars, awareness stages, and sophistication stages. Stated identically as his standing exploration ratio.
Blue Sense Digital reaches the same axis with a three-bucket split stated in two videos: 50-60% straight replication of proven winners, 20-30% iteration on underperformers, 20-30% genuinely net-new concepts. The top bucket is the difference. Replication means re-running, reposting and remaking the same thing rather than iterating it, and he ranks it above iteration because each rerun performs about the same and reaches new people. He self-reports violating his own rule in his organic content at 80-90% net-new, and observes that the replication bucket reliably carries 80% of his views while the net-new bucket mostly does not perform. Its value is feeding new candidates into the replication bucket, which is why he calls a 0% net-new allocation the one genuinely bad choice: the winners all fatigue eventually. Asserted, no test.

### CR-036 · Test in a fixed order with all other variables locked: offer first, then 5 pain points, then formats/angles
Tier: T3 · Status: active

### CR-113 · Meta's in-ad Creative Testing tool splits the ad set's audience into segments so new ads get forced delivery, and whether performance advertisers should use it is disputed
Tier: T2 · Status: contested

He reads the default as a statement of design intent, on the grounds that most Meta spend is not conversion spend, and adds that any forced concurrent test cannibalises an existing winner. ### CR-114 · The five-variable ad builder: avatar, awareness stage, pain point, angle, format; lock four and vary one per ad set
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Every ad decomposes into five variables and a test isolates exactly one. Forward construction (avatar, then awareness stage, then pain point, then angle, then format) is how a brief is normally written; reverse construction starts from a format spotted in the ad library and works backward to the angle and pain point it carries. Sequence for most accounts: lock avatar, awareness, angle and format, and vary pain point first because pain points are the easiest variable to test. Then lock the winning pain point and run five ads varying only format (Twitter-style static, before/after, green screen, VSL, case study, talking head). Then lock format and vary angle. "Angle" here is a specific, narrow thing: the wrapper the offer is presented in, not the offer or the pain point. His examples of angles are new ("there's a new way to get clients online"), fast ("here's a faster way"), mistakes ("three mistakes people make when..."), and even-if-you-have-tried-before. Allocation rule: 80% of testing iterates known winners by dissecting which of the five variables made them win, 20% is genuinely new territory. Matches the same 80/20 split reached independently in [[Creative Science#CR-033|CR-033]].
Sources: Dr. Matt Shiver, The BEST Way to Scale Facebook Ads in 2026, 2026-08-18
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### CR-122 · Amount spent is the most reliable proxy for creative quality, above ROAS/CPA, above CPC/CTR, and above hook and hold rate

### CR-125 · Test angle first, offer second, persona third, format last; testing by medium instead of by concept is the standard inversion
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Reasoning per rung. Angle is unbounded and is where bad creative actually fails, because bad ads are a scripting and argument problem. Offer may matter most in absolute terms but you run out of tests fast with two or three products, and the only lever left becomes deep discounting. Persona is capped because you already know who buys from you. Format only exists as a fourth variable post-Andromeda, because delivery routes formats to people who engage with that format, so swapping format on the same persona, angle and offer reaches mostly the same people. Named failure mode: teams start at format ("let's test UGC"), which does not work because "UGC as a concept doesn't work", the script inside it does.
The same operator states the inverse failure directly: concluding "statics don't work for our brand" is a skill gap in translating the concept into a static, not a property of the format. His structure follows from it: split ad sets by concept, then run iterations across different media inside the concept, so video, static and GIF compete within a concept rather than being siloed into medium-based tests. ### CR-103 · Should you copy competitors' ads directly?
Tier: T3 · Status: contested
Nick Theriot: never. Copying a competitor caps you below the originator (his illustrative heuristic: roughly one order of magnitude below) and makes your hooks match the niche's saturated pattern. The sanctioned move is monitoring winning ads across different niches (via a trend tracker) and importing the mechanic where it is new, supplemented with customer surveys and YouTube/Reddit problem searches for verbatim customer phrasings.
Mark Builds Brands sits on the same side with a mechanism: swipe the philosophy, not the copy. "When I'm swiping ads, I'm not taking the copy. What I'm doing is I'm actually swiping the philosophy." His argument is about learning rate: 1:1 swiping produces a short-lived result and teaches the operator nothing, so the operator stays permanently dependent on finding the next thing to swipe, while framework extraction compounds into a reusable bank. He also names the AI application, which is to feed extracted philosophies and frameworks into a model to generate large numbers of on-principle variations, rather than prompting from a competitor's literal copy.
Professor Charley T and Ben Heath argue the opposite, in a joint session, with two filters. Heath's longevity filter: an ad running a week tells you nothing, an ad running 6+ months (he stretches to 9-18) has survived and is almost certainly profitable, and this holds "for just about every advertiser." Charley's selection filter: "What you should be looking at to copy is somebody at the level you're trying to get to. Don't go after somebody 10 times bigger than you." Hexclad, Vuori and Hormozi-tier brands have solved the business model, most of their ads fail anyway, and their assets (celebrity, influencer partnerships) are not replicable. He advocates literal shot-for-shot remakes of statics and videos from same-level operators.
Neither side showed performance data from a copied ad. The narrow point of disagreement is copying a same-level operator's execution, and the two positions do partly reconcile: everyone agrees copying a 10x-larger brand is wasted, and everyone agrees the saturated-pattern risk is real. Nobody tested it.

### CR-106 · Three outlier videos permanently reset a channel's view baseline
Tier: T3 · Status: active
The stepping pattern: at a 1K average, one 10K video lifts the next videos to 2-4K; after a 20K video the average sits 5-7K; a third outlier and the baseline is 10K+. Each outlier raises the floor rather than being an isolated spike. The catch: the three outliers must be on the right topics or the baseline resets toward the wrong audience.

### CR-108 · YouTube Shorts viewers and long-form viewers are separate populations
Tier: T3 · Status: active
A ~1M-subscriber client gets ~100,000 views on shorts and ~150 views on long-form. Shorts behave like a different app (podcast clips, reactions, comedy; talking-head content that works on Instagram flops on Shorts), and shorts views rarely convert into long-form viewers, so a shorts-heavy channel can look like an asset while its nurture engine is dead. Exception: pure-volume brand plays like Hormozi. Related valuation heuristic: one long-form view is worth roughly 10 short-form views, and displayed views undercount reach since CTR runs 2-10% of impressions.

### CR-109 · Verbatim-scripted YouTube videos flop even with perfect research and packaging; long-form punishes prompter-reading
Tier: T3 · Status: active

### CR-110 · YouTube in 2026 is a mid-funnel nurture platform, not top-of-funnel; guaranteed discovery now requires paid ads
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Content supply outgrew demand, collapsing organic discoverability for niche offers (40 creators with 100K-sub channels all chasing the same Airbnb automation leads). The working system: paid Meta/TikTok ads for discovery, then prospects self-navigate to the channel where long-form converts them to fans then buyers; a smaller channel can even add trust for high-ticket. Paid ads also drive unattributed conversions through owned content: well into six figures of attributed revenue landed on sub-500-view videos from buyers who saw an ad, searched the creator, and converted off-track.

### CR-111 · Define an ideal viewer profile: YouTube skews ~70% male by default while Instagram/TikTok roughly invert

### CR-112 · Chasing views only pays for low-ticket subscription offers that extend the content; for client acquisition, a 500-view video producing 2 booked calls is the win condition
Tier: T3 · Status: active

For client-getting channels, a 500-view video producing 2 booked calls and 1 sale is the correct win condition.
