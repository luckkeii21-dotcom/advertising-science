# Google PMax & Shopping

Performance Max internals, feed mechanics, brand cannibalization, channel decomposition.

Part of the [[00-Codex|Advertising Science Codex]]. Claims follow the tier system (T1 docs, T2 shown test, T3 practitioner, T4 theory).

## Claims

## PMax vs Search Priority

### GP-001 · An exact match keyword in a Search campaign that matches the query beats Performance Max; search themes rank only equal to phrase/broad keywords.
Tier: T1 · Status: active
Google's stated priority rule: when a search query matches an exact match keyword in your Search campaign, the Search campaign is prioritized over Performance Max. Search themes in PMax get the same prioritization as phrase and broad match keywords in Search campaigns, so exact-match coverage is the advertiser's lever for keeping queries in the controllable campaign. Brand exclusions in PMax prevent branded-query overlap.
Sources: Google Ads Help: About Performance Max / Use search themes, https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/14767319
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### GP-002 · Search themes are optional, additive, capped at 50 per asset group, and do not override exclusions or negative keywords.
Tier: T1 · Status: active
Search themes add the words and phrases customers use, on top of the queries and placements PMax already predicts from your assets, feeds, and landing pages. Up to 50 unique themes per asset group. Google states exclusions and negative keywords still apply, so themes expand reach without disabling the account's negative controls.
Sources: Google Ads Help: Use search themes with your Performance Max campaign, https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/14767319
Last touched: 2026-08-18

## How PMax Buys

### GP-003 · Performance Max serves one campaign across YouTube, Display, Search, Discover, Gmail, and Maps, bidding with Smart Bidding plus attribution technology in real time.
Tier: T1 · Status: active
A single PMax campaign buys across all six Google surfaces against the advertiser's stated goal (sales, leads, store visits), using Smart Bidding combined with attribution technology to set bids across that inventory in real time, maximizing conversions or conversion value. Asset groups are themed creative collections from which the system assembles ads, and Google may combine advertiser-provided assets with AI-generated ones.
Sources: Google Ads Help: About Performance Max campaigns, https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/10724817
Last touched: 2026-08-18

### GP-004 · PMax audience signals are suggestions that guide and accelerate Google AI; they are not targeting constraints.
Tier: T1 · Status: active
Google frames audience signals (remarketing lists, Customer Match, custom segments) as inputs that enhance the AI's optimization toward your goals, and best-practice guidance says they guide the AI and accelerate ramp-up. The campaign can and will serve beyond the supplied audiences; the signal shapes where learning starts, not where delivery ends.

Second practitioner source on the first half only, added 2026-08-19. A PMax audience signal does not restrict delivery the way a Meta interest does. In Meta an interest restricts to that interest; in PMax the signal is a direction the campaign should go in, and the system uses it functionally as a lookalike seed. That is direct support for the suggestion-not-constraint half of this claim, from a second episode and a different framing. The source is SILENT on the 30-day bypass. Four Blue Sense Google episodes were read in full on 2026-08-19, two of them on PMax structure specifically, and none of them restates the claim that Google bypasses the signal after roughly 30 days of conversion data. That half stays on a single T3 assertion and must not be read as strengthened here. Practitioner claim about platform behaviour from running PMax at scale, no documentation quoted and no test shown.

Practitioner extension added 2026-08-18 (T3, ASSERTED). The guidance window is roughly 30 days. Once about 30 days of conversion data has accumulated inside the campaign, Google bypasses the signal, and removing the audience signal produces no measurable performance change. On a mature account spending $30-100k/month on PMax, tweaking audience signals or search themes is "a one percenter at best", so an agency audit whose headline recommendation is signal tuning is proposing a non-intervention. Signals and search themes earn their keep in the cold-start window only: new account, new campaign, new asset group, new category, where they stop Google wasting budget on random exploration. When search themes are used, populate them from converting terms in the account's own historical search-term report rather than inventing them. Evidence base stated as PMax on 250+ accounts plus roughly 400 consulted e-commerce accounts from 7 to 10 figures. No test output shown. Consistent with the ranking in GP-010, where signals and search themes rank last of five variables.
Sources: Google Ads Help: About Performance Max campaigns, https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/10724817; Blue Sense Digital, How to Scale an eCommerce Brand Profitably in 2026: The Full System, 2026-06-15; Blue Sense Digital, The Only Google Ads Strategy You Need for 2026, 2026-06-22; Blue Sense Digital, How To Structure Performance Max Asset Groups, 2025-02-25

## PMax attribution and incrementality

### GP-005 · PMax over-attributes: 50% or more of PMax conversions are repeat or warm customers it did not acquire, and standard shopping running beside it is structurally under-credited.
Tier: T3 · Status: active
The mechanism is last-click credit theft inside one account. Standard shopping does the cold acquisition, PMax retargets and takes the final click before conversion, so the account reads as PMax scaling while shopping carries the acquisition. Presented as the consensus among large Google agencies "behind closed doors" three years after PMax replaced smart shopping. Evidence base stated as running PMax on 250+ accounts plus consulting on roughly 400 more e-commerce accounts from 7 to 10 figures. ASSERTED. No screenshots or exports described. Operator consequences: never judge the PMax-versus-shopping budget split on in-platform ROAS, and never kill standard shopping because PMax shows a better return next to it. The recommended reframe is to treat it as a split question, with roughly 20-40% PMax and the balance in standard shopping for most mid-to-upper-market ecom. See GP-006 for the test that produced this claim and GP-008 for the strategy built on it.

A harder and higher number for the warm share, added 2026-08-19. In the majority of audits this operator runs, the audit is literally just pulling up the client's Performance Max and Shopping campaigns that the client believes are cold prospecting and bringing in new customers, and showing that 70 to 80% of the conversions in them are warm or existing customers. That figure is above the 50%+ this claim banks, and it comes from the same channel. Read the headline as a range running from 50% up to 70-80% rather than a settled 50%, and note that the higher figure is the modal audit finding rather than a worst case. Evidence quality is unchanged: a recalled majority-of-audits frequency across an unstated account sample, no screenshot, no export, no dates, so ASSERTED. Two operator consequences. The diagnostic that opens most audits is splitting new against returning customers inside the campaign the client calls prospecting, which needs a new-customer conversion event fired as its own action. And the quantified discount to apply to the reported number, 30-40% even with brand exclusions running, is at GP-007.

Independent corroboration added 2026-08-19, at the portfolio-allocation level. Two agencies reversed their PMax allocation in the same direction. Blue Sense ran roughly 90% of spend in PMax in mid-to-late 2024 and is now at roughly 40% of total spend, with the difference moved back into standard shopping and standard search. Austin from Grow My Ads reports the same flip between last year and this year across his own book. Neither killed PMax; both repositioned it as a middle-of-funnel remarketing campaign inside the feeder structure at GP-008, which Austin credits to John Moran. Both name the same two reasons: regaining advertiser control, and stopping the system spending non-incrementally on existing customers. Note that 40% lands at the top of the 20-40% band above, so two agencies arrived independently at the range this claim already recommends. Evidence gap: this is what two agencies now believe and do, not evidence that the reversal improved results. No before or after revenue, ROAS or CAC was shown on either side. A resolution would need blended backend revenue across the reallocation window on at least one account. GP-025 records a T4 hypothesis from the same channel that runs against the last-click framing here. GP-039 supplies the structural reason standard Shopping's own number is more trustworthy, and GP-040 shows the same warm-harvesting shape measured rather than asserted on a different Google campaign type.

### GP-006 · Ramping PMax 2x over 30 days held reported ROAS flat at 5x with no backend revenue lift; the same ramp on standard shopping dropped reported ROAS to 3-4x and produced materially better backend revenue.
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Run as parallel ramps on separate accounts, described as repeating "every single time we ran the test". The reading: a PMax ROAS that refuses to move while spend doubles means retargeting is absorbing the extra budget instead of the campaign finding new customers. Standard shopping's falling ROAS under the same ramp is the honest signal of reaching colder users. Two extra observations from the same source: PMax campaigns pushed to $100-300k/month stopped showing up in backend revenue at all, and PMax performs well on smaller accounts (roughly $15-20k/month and below) then hits a ceiling above that. This is a direct trap for anyone treating in-platform ROAS stability as evidence that a campaign is scaling. Numbers stated verbally, no dashboards or exports described, so ASSERTED rather than shown. Scope: e-commerce retail accounts.

Second-agency corroboration on the high end, added 2026-08-19. Grow My Ads reports repeated inbound from advertisers running $100k to $200k+ per month entirely through PMax who have been flat for the previous full year, and states plainly that the structure will not get them where they want to be the following year. It is offered as the everyday case rather than an outlier. That widens the $100-300k/month stall observation from one agency to two, still with no account data on either side. ### GP-007 · Google and PMax ROAS stays halo-inflated even with brand excluded; discount PMax reported ROAS by 30-40%, and falsify cheaply by adding $1,000 and checking whether the promised top line appears.
Tier: T3 · Status: active
A separate mechanism from the intra-account credit theft in GP-005. Here the claim is that Google takes credit for demand generated on other platforms and by organic presence, so brand exclusions alone do not clean the number. He is explicit that Google can acquire genuinely incremental new customers at $100-200k/month, which makes this a measurement claim about the reported figure. The test is the operator's fastest and cheapest falsification: a campaign reporting 20x should return roughly proportional top line on a marginal budget increase, and it usually does not. ASSERTED. No test output shown. Pairs with GP-020 on uncontested brand search, which is where ROAS-as-north-star produces the largest waste at 8 and 9 figure retailers.

The over-report gets a size, added 2026-08-19, and this is the first one the codex holds. The working rule the same agency applies is a theoretical 30 to 40% discount on whatever PMax reports in attribution, applied even when brand exclusions are already active on the campaign. Their worked example: a reported 5.0 is read as roughly 3.8. Every earlier statement in this file was directional, so this is the number to use when a client asks how much to knock off. Boundary on it: this is a working rule derived from managing the accounts, with no incrementality test shown to produce the 30 to 40% band, so it is a prior rather than a measured coefficient, and it should be replaced by the account's own holdout the moment one exists. The same episode set restates the falsification test at a lower multiple: a campaign reporting 5x gets another $1,000 and does not return the extra $5,000. The operator's reading of that failure is the load-bearing part, because he attributes it to misattribution rather than to a scaling ceiling. Those two diagnoses point opposite ways: a scaling ceiling tells you to restructure, a misattribution tells you to re-measure before touching anything. Both statements are described as repeated real-account outcomes with no spend figures, dates or exports shown, so ASSERTED. Read the discount alongside the 70-80% warm share at GP-005 and the measured Demand Gen holdout at GP-040.

### GP-024 · PMax's early advantage came from heavy warm-audience targeting, and once that advantage decayed the extra budget went into junk display and video traffic.
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Austin's mechanism claim, credited to John Moran raising it first. PMax does a lot of warm-audience targeting, so early adopters got a genuine competitive advantage from reaching their own warm pool cheaply before anyone else was bidding on it. Once everyone adopted, the advantage decayed and accounts plateaued. Past the plateau, additional budget produced diminishing returns because PMax started spending more on junk traffic across display and video. This is the delivery-side reason behind demoting PMax to a middle-funnel remarketing role rather than killing it, which is the move recorded at GP-005. It also supplies a mechanism for GP-013, where placement drift into Gmail and YouTube burned budget on an account spending a few hundred thousand a month. Evidence gap: practitioner episode with no placement report, no spend-by-channel split, and no before or after shown, so "junk traffic" is the speaker's judgement rather than a measured placement breakdown. A PMax channel-performance report showing display and video share rising as spend rises past the plateau would settle it.

### GP-025 · Contested: PMax may win on long-customer-journey brands by taking the first click and bidding through a 7 to 14 day consideration window, which runs against the last-click framing in GP-005.
Tier: T4 · Status: contested
Side A, this claim. Nathan's hypothesis for why PMax beats standard Shopping on the specific half of his accounts where it does: those brands have longer customer journeys, PMax gets the first click, nurtures the user across a 7 to 14 day window, then bids aggressively on their later searches. He says he can see it in the data and immediately downgrades it himself, stating he would not put it on his channel as fact. Treat 7 to 14 days as illustrative, not measured. Side B, GP-005 and GP-006: PMax does no cold acquisition, standard shopping does, and PMax takes the final click before conversion. Both cannot be the whole story on the same account. Two things weaken Side A. It is T4 by the speaker's own labelling, against a T3 asserted from a 250+ account portfolio. And it is from Feb 2025, roughly sixteen months before the same channel published the last-click framing, so it may be a superseded earlier position rather than a live disagreement. Resolution: campaign-level first-click versus last-click position data, or a new-customer versus repeat-customer split fired as a distinct conversion event, would show directly whether PMax opens journeys or closes them. Until then the file's operating assumption stays GP-005.

Second and stronger source for Side A's outcome, added 2026-08-19, and it supports a different mechanism than the one Side A proposes. From a later episode on the same channel: PMax will often outperform standard Shopping when the purchase window runs 7 to 10 days or the ticket is high. That is reported as a pattern across a client book, which makes it T3 against Side A's self-labelled T4, and the 7-10 day figure sits inside Side A's illustrative 7-14 day window. The stated mechanism is that PMax carries built-in retargeting across all Google channels, Gmail, display, search and shopping, and cranks the bid once it knows the user is in the funnel. Read carefully, that is the closing role, not the opening one. So the new source corroborates that PMax wins on long-journey brands while contradicting the first-click nurture story Side A tells to explain the win, and it is fully compatible with GP-005 and GP-008. ### GP-039 · Standard Shopping's reported numbers can be trusted more than PMax's for two structural reasons: visible search terms, and negative bid adjustments on existing visitors and customers.
Tier: T3 · Status: active
This is the mechanism under GP-005's claim that standard shopping is the honest campaign, and it converts a belief into two account features you can check in five minutes. First, standard Shopping exposes search term data, so you can read what the campaign actually bought instead of inferring it from the outcome. Second, and this is the lever most operators never pull, standard Shopping accepts audience segments with bid adjustments, so you can apply a negative adjustment to existing website visitors and existing customers and structurally push the campaign off anyone already inside the funnel. PMax offers neither at the same resolution. Two consequences. For measurement: when the two campaigns disagree about what is working, believe the one you can inspect and control, and apply the discount at GP-007 to the one you cannot. For structure: if you need a genuinely cold Google campaign that you can prove is cold, build it in standard Shopping with negative audience adjustments rather than in PMax with an audience signal, because per GP-004 the signal does not restrict delivery at all. That is a concrete build instruction the codex did not previously carry. Structural argument from operating both campaign types side by side, no comparison data shown, so ASSERTED.

## PMax and standard shopping as a pair

### GP-008 · Feeder strategy: standard shopping at a deliberately low target ROAS (as low as 50%) buys the cold query pool, and a high-target-ROAS PMax (~600%) closes on the second or third click.
Tier: T3 · Status: active
The arbitrage is auction-side. Most advertisers set high efficiency targets, so they all crowd into the same high-intent half of the query pool, while the low-intent half (first-time searchers, no site history) attracts almost no bidders and is cheap to reach. The low-tROAS campaign buys that whole pool at low cost, then the high-tROAS campaign converts the survivors on a later click. Standard shopping is the cold vehicle because it reaches cold audiences better than PMax does. This is the strategic answer to GP-005: assign PMax the closing role deliberately instead of fighting it for credit. Expect the cold campaign's own reported ROAS to look bad by design. Judge the pair together on backend revenue, never campaign by campaign. Lead-gen equivalent: substitute search for shopping. ASSERTED, no paired-account numbers shown.

Launch-end version of the same mechanism, added 2026-08-19 from a separate episode. Two practitioners independently describe running PMax on a small budget with a tROAS goal set higher than what the Shopping campaign is operating at. The high target pushes PMax toward the cheapest and most likely converters, which in practice means returning visitors, so the campaign behaves like a remarketing campaign without being built as one. Nathan names this as one of the two reasons he runs PMax on roughly half his accounts. This is the same closing role reached from the entry point rather than from a fully built feeder pair, and it makes the tROAS gap between the two campaigns the actual control lever. It also inverts GP-009 into a deliberate tool: PMax defaults to retargeting, so if you want retargeting, set the target high and let it. No spend or ROAS figures shown for the play. Feb 2025 source, so confirm current PMax tROAS behaviour before applying.

### GP-038 · Time to purchase selects the campaign type: a 7-10 day window or a high ticket favours PMax, a 6-12 hour impulse window favours standard Shopping.
Tier: T3 · Status: active
A selector the codex has been missing, and it is measurable from GA4 before any money moves. Long window or high ticket: PMax often outperforms standard Shopping, because PMax carries built-in retargeting across all Google channels and raises the bid once it knows the user is in the funnel, so it holds the placement across a consideration window that a single-surface Shopping campaign cannot follow the user through. Short window: on brands whose time to purchase runs 6 to 12 hours because the items are impulsive and low ticket, PMax overspends trying to re-reach users whose intent is already dead, because the campaign has no way to know a stale visitor is unrecoverable and keeps bidding on them. Standard Shopping is the vehicle there. Operator step: pull median time from first session to purchase out of GA4 before choosing, and re-pull it per collection wherever ticket varies widely, because one catalogue can contain both patterns and the account-level median will hide that. What this does not say: the mechanism named for PMax winning is retargeting, so a PMax that wins on this rule is winning as a closer, which is the role GP-008 assigns it deliberately and the reason its reported ROAS still needs the 30-40% discount at GP-007. Winning the head-to-head and being incremental are separate questions and this claim only settles the first. Reported as a pattern across a client book with real client purchase windows cited, no spend, CPA or ROAS figures shown, so ASSERTED. It supplies the boundary condition GP-025 was missing.

## PMax configuration

### GP-009 · A PMax campaign without brand exclusions is a retargeting campaign; run the campaign-level brand list and PMax negative keywords together.
Tier: T3 · Status: active
The brand list is built in campaign settings under brand exclusions, where Google indexes the brand and captures branded variants. PMax negative keywords are a newer addition and did not previously exist, so many accounts carry only one of the two. He runs both deliberately as redundant failsafes: a missed keyword variation is caught by the brand list, and a broken or incomplete negative list is caught by the brand list, and vice versa. Without exclusions, PMax retargets existing brand traffic and returns no incremental value. Same rule applies to Search, where brand should also be excluded from non-brand campaigns. This ranks second in the GP-010 importance ordering, which makes it the highest-leverage single fix in most accounts. ASSERTED across three separate videos, no data shown.

The tactic has a real cost, added 2026-08-19. This claim has been carried with no downside attached and there is one. Hard brand exclusions on cold campaigns vacate the branded Shopping slot, and competitors then place shopping ads above the brand's own organic branded listing. It is recorded in full at GP-034. The rule survives untouched: still run both the campaign-level brand list and PMax negative keywords on any campaign bought for cold acquisition. The remedy is additive, meaning run a dedicated branded Shopping campaign alongside the exclusions so the slot stays held at its own low bid, never to soften or remove the exclusion on the cold campaign. Anyone applying this rule to a client account should ship the branded Shopping campaign in the same change window as the exclusions, not afterwards.

### GP-014 · GMC titles: brand name last, front-load keywords proven in the search terms report, pack in every relevant attribute, and treat variants as separate listings.

The margin justification is the one most accounts get wrong, and it has a hard mechanism: Google cannot see your margin profile in a standard setup, so a shared 300% tROAS across a 70% gross margin collection and a 35% gross margin collection sends spend wherever revenue is highest, which can be the barely-profitable line. The category justification is about learning contamination, with office chairs versus plants as the extreme case: they share a buyer context and train toward different personas. Geography should always split at campaign level for multi-country selling. Commercial realities also justify campaigns that will knowingly underperform: grade C inventory you need to move, new arrivals you need surfaced for seasonality, dynamic best-seller versus zombie-product campaigns. That is why an agency needs inventory-level business knowledge before restructuring. The test he applies is whether you can steelman the reason a campaign exists. ASSERTED, no data shown.

Second agency, same gate, slightly different list, added 2026-08-19. Austin's valid reasons to segment are margin differences between product categories or brands, and pulling out proven low performers. Segmenting because it feels like best practice is the named failure case, and he warns that a broken campaign is hard to bring back, which raises the cost of getting the call wrong. He adds one gate the four justifications above do not carry: any segment you pull out must have enough conversion volume to survive on its own. Two operator consequences follow. Build the split by demoting the tail, never by promoting the head, per GP-032. And watch the step size, because the same speaker's bulk SKU exclusion at GP-033 killed a campaign even though the segmentation reason was valid. ### GP-032 · When splitting a Shopping campaign, remove the losers and leave the winners where they are, because a winner pulled into its own campaign does not reproduce its performance.
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Both speakers converge from opposite directions. Austin never extracts what is working, he extracts what is not, because the winner is already carrying campaign-level learning that a brand new campaign does not inherit. Nathan reports running the opposite play many times, taking a high performer out and giving it its own campaign at roughly $200/day, and says he has never once seen it work. Direct consequence for the Pareto split at GP-021, build it by demoting the tail, never by promoting the head out of a campaign that is already working. Repeated failures described on both sides, no numbers shown beyond the $200/day budget example, so this is banked on converging practitioner experience rather than a test. Feb 2025 source.

### GP-033 · Bulk-excluding a large block of SKUs can kill a Shopping or PMax campaign outright; reinstate them and remove in chunks of roughly 5,000 to reach the identical end state.
Tier: T3 · Status: active
A jewellery brand with roughly 20,000 SKUs was tagged into top, mid, low and zombie performers with custom labels. The team excluded the low performers and zombies in one move, roughly 18,000 SKUs, leaving about 2,000, and the campaign died. Turning the low, mid and zombie SKUs back on brought it back to life. Removing them in chunks of about 5,000 at a time eventually landed on the exact same end state that the single bulk exclusion had destroyed, at the cost of a much longer timeline. Austin also reports cases where even the slow chunking failed and the weak SKUs had to stay in permanently. Evidence caveat, and it is the reason this is T3 rather than T2: the outcome is described qualitatively with no spend, ROAS or conversion figures, no date range, and no chart, so "died" and "came back to life" are the speaker's words, not a measured delta on a single client case. Unresolved tension in the same conversation: the same speaker argues low performers drag winners down, because the bidding system knows it can hit target ROAS off the high performers and keeps spending the surplus testing the losers, which is the entire reason to exclude them. He flags the tension himself and does not resolve it. The reconciliation the case supports is about rate rather than direction, meaning the exclusion is right and the step size is what kills the campaign, but nothing in the source establishes that. Operator rule: never remove more than roughly a quarter of a large catalogue in one edit, and treat a dead campaign after a bulk feed change as recoverable by reinstating the excluded SKUs before rebuilding anything. See GP-023 for the gate on whether to segment at all.

## Demand Gen

Filed here rather than under the Google auction topic because Demand Gen is a campaign type and the claims below are structural, about what the campaign buys and what role it should hold. The geo holdout METHOD used to produce them belongs to [[Attribution & Incrementality]] and is not reproduced here.

### GP-040 · A 21-day Demand Gen geo holdout produced no statistically relevant new-customer lift and a 6.8% returning-customer lift, at an implied new-customer iCAC of $140.
Tier: T2 · Status: active
The only shown-data incrementality test in this file, and it lands on a Google campaign type sold as prospecting. Design: single geo holdout on the Australian market over 21 days. Test geo Victoria plus Queensland, control the rest of Australia. States selected by correlating the movement of monthly order patterns between states across the year, isolating p-values, then cross-correlating to find the states that best predict all the others. Baseline built from 12 months of daily orders. Confidence set at 90%. New-customer and returning-customer orders measured as separate outcomes, which is the design decision the whole result turns on.

New customers, the outcome the campaign was bought for: expected 2,771 orders, actual 2,857, a lift of 85 against the 166 required for statistical relevance at 90% confidence. Not statistically relevant. New-customer orders in fact fell in both arms across the window, down 4.5% in the control and down 1.41% in the test states, which is a market-wide or seasonal decline that the holdout design correctly separates from the treatment and that an in-platform read would have missed entirely. Implied incremental CAC on new customers is $140, unprofitable on first purchase. The operator states plainly that the number is not even statistically supportable, so $140 is an arithmetic artefact of a null result and must never be quoted as a measured iCAC.

Returning customers: a 6.8% period-on-period lift in orders in the test states while the rest of Australia dipped. Expected 1,211 returning-customer orders, achieved 1,521, at $39 cost per returning-customer order, which the operator calls very profitable.

Recorded inconsistency, unresolved by the source. Early in the walkthrough the baseline is stated as about 2,500 orders over the period with a required lift of 254 for statistical confidence. Later the expected count is 2,771 with a threshold of 166 at 90%. The most likely reconciliation is that 2,500 and 254 are the 95% confidence scenario computed at design time and 2,771 and 166 are the 90% scenario computed against the actual window, but the source never says so and the two are narrated as if they were the same figures. Both pairs are recorded here rather than one being silently chosen. The headline finding does not depend on which is right: the measured lift of 85 falls short of both thresholds.

Measurement rule the operator draws from his own result, and it generalises past Demand Gen: never run one of these tests on blended order lift, because a blended number reads as acquisition when the lift is retargeting. This test would have reported a clean positive on a blended read. Split new-customer and returning-customer outcomes before the test starts, not after. This is the empirically measured version of the shape GP-005, GP-006 and GP-007 assert about PMax without data, arriving on a different Google campaign type, which is the reason it is worth this much space. Scope limit, and it is tight: one account, one market, one campaign type, 21 days, 90% confidence. It does not transfer to PMax as evidence. It transfers as a method and as a prior.
**ONE EXPERIMENT, THREE ENTRIES. Never weigh it three times.** This single 21-day Blue Sense Demand Gen geo holdout is also recorded at [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-072|AT-072]] (the split-new-from-returning measurement rule) and [[Google Auction & Smart Bidding#GA-055|GA-055]] (the iCAC read). Three claims exist because it answers three different questions, and it is still n=1: one advertiser, one market, one campaign type, 21 days.

### GP-041 · Demand Gen harvests users already in the funnel: the lift is instantaneous, the warm pool exhausts, then the campaign burns budget on returning customers. Run it as retargeting at reduced budget.
Tier: T3 · Status: active
The operator's interpretation of his own T2 result at GP-040, plus the decision he took off the back of it. Mechanism as stated: if there is a lift, it is an instantaneous lift from Demand Gen going quickly after people who are already in the funnel and already warm, capitalising on pre-existing top-of-funnel traffic. Once that pool is exhausted the campaign either does nothing or moves to returning customers and burns budget. The interpretation is T3 because it is inference from a single test plus corroboration from other agencies and channels he names but never shows. The decision is the harder part: the campaign kept running at significantly reduced budget and was reclassified from acquisition to a retargeting campaign on returning customers. That is the same demotion GP-005 records for PMax, reached here with data rather than assertion, which makes it the strongest single piece of support in the file for the demotion pattern generally. Two supporting generalisations from the same channel, both T3 and both plain assertion: as of March 2025, Demand Gen had not delivered consistent, provable cold customer acquisition ROI across four to five larger accounts under active test, and the operator names Google rep enthusiasm for the product as a sales motion; and as of August 2025, Demand Gen significantly over-attributes with no positive impact on new customer acquisition for 90%+ of ad accounts. He time-stamps that second one himself and tells the viewer to check the publication date because Google constantly tweaks the product, so read it as a 2025 position rather than a standing fact and re-test before quoting it to a client in 2026. Operator handling: do not buy Demand Gen as acquisition on a rep's recommendation, budget it as retargeting and hold it to a retargeting cost target, and if you need to know whether it is incremental on your own account, run the holdout at GP-040 rather than reading the platform's number.

### GP-042 · PMax's "new customer acquisition" setting is overridden by the conversion action, so it skews warm anyway
Tier: T3 · Status: active
A direct operating claim against a setting whose name promises the opposite. Google specialist on Blue Sense's channel, 2025-09-10: "the algorithm is going to prioritize the conversion action over the audience every single time."
**The consequence, with a frequency estimate attached.** Where other channels are also driving repeat purchases, PMax follows the conversion signal into the warm pool: "greater than 50% of the time performance max goes after repeat".
**The structural fix is the same one that appears at [[Attribution & Incrementality#AT-104|AT-104]]:** separate cold from branded and repeat at CAMPAIGN level rather than trusting an in-campaign audience toggle, "rather than having a Pmax that encapsulates branded key terms".
**Why this belongs next to [[Meta Delivery & Andromeda#MD-019|MD-019]] and law 1a.** It is the Google-side instance of the same pattern the Meta half of this codex has spent five passes on: an audience-labelled control that turns out to be a suggestion, or in this case a signal that loses to the optimisation event. **On both platforms, the conversion event beats the audience setting.** That is close to a cross-platform rule and it is worth testing as one.
**Guards.** T3, no account shown, no data. "Greater than 50% of the time" has no denominator and no measurement behind it. He does not say whether the setting was verified as enabled in the accounts he is describing.
