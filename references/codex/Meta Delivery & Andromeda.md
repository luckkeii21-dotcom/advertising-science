# Meta Delivery & Andromeda

How Meta decides who sees an ad: the multi-stage retrieval and ranking machine (Andromeda), the GEM foundation model, and why creative became the targeting input.

Part of the [[00-Codex|Advertising Science Codex]]. Claims follow the tier system (T1 docs, T2 shown test, T3 practitioner, T4 theory).

## Claims

## Creative Is the Targeting (Andromeda Era)

### MD-001 · Meta now targets by reading the creative itself, so the avatar callout in the ad decides who sees it
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Multiple independent sources converge on the same mechanism: Meta scans the video transcript, visuals, and text of each creative and routes delivery to the matching people, so the creative maker, not the media buyer, controls who sees the ad. Fraser Cottrell reports that mentioning a specific scenario or objection in the script routes delivery to the matching audience. Sam Piliero cites a supplement brand that sold for $1B running distinct avatar angles ("Lose weight, not your metabolism", "2025 with better poops") in one broad account, and says without avatar callouts "Andromeda does not know how to deliver your ads appropriately". Matt Shiver operationalizes it: the avatar callout must appear in the image, the primary text, and the landing page, and the results column completes the loop. Consistent with Meta's documented content-based retrieval (MD-022, MD-025).
Andrew Faris supplies a candidate mechanism: when an ad is served, Meta watches who engages, then finds more users like those engagers and serves them the same ad, which amounts to a lookalike audience built in real time per creative. His read of Meta's own statements plus what he can observe, no test. Faris also reports the scaling consequence in live client accounts, that narrowing the creative message no longer costs reach efficiency the way it used to, so his agency now writes deliberately narrow ads. His illustration is a feed serving Little League drills specific to four, five and six year olds rather than nine and ten year olds, a materially different coaching problem.
Jeremy Haynes states the same principle harder, that ad-set-level targeting is an "archaic, outdated belief" because messaging controls targeting, then uses it to justify dumping every interest, behaviour and demographic trait into one giant ad set. Charley T flags the internal contradiction on camera: if messaging does the targeting, the interest and lookalike ad sets have no job to do.
The strongest first-party statement to date, and read the tier note before quoting it. Matt Steiner, a named Meta executive over monetization infrastructure, ranking and foundational AI, describes retrieval as a per-user decision made on the creative's content attributes: "if you create 25 versions of an ad, maybe only three of those would be appealing to me potentially and a bunch of them may not be appealing to me because of the types of content that I look at, because of the backgrounds that it appears on, whether the color matches, the specific context that the ad appears in." Elsewhere in the same interview he concedes budget movement and optimisation to the machine permanently and leaves novel message ideation as the human lever: "humans are never going to outperform the machines at that type of work again." TIER NOTE, and it is deliberate: this is T3 and must never be promoted to T1. He quotes no document, shows no paper and shows no data in 20 minutes, and the 25-versions-three-relevant figure is an illustrative example rather than a measurement. A named platform employee asserting on a podcast is testimony, not documentation. Treat it as high-credibility unverified testimony that converges with the T1 retrieval architecture in MD-022 and MD-025, not as a second copy of it.
First-party operator evidence, and the closest thing here to a controlled setup. Nick Theriot runs the avatar lever entirely inside the creative while the targeting layer stays untouched: United States, 18 to 65, women, purchasers excluded, no detailed targeting, no app install status. On the same reasoning he has dropped retargeting campaigns and retargeting audiences outright and now puts retargeting creatives into a new ad set on Advantage+ audience inside the main CBO, "and we let Facebook scan the creative and figure out, okay, hey, this is a product ad. Let's show it to a product audience. Or, hey, this is a discount ad." He also treats the person on camera as the audience unlock: swapping the ethnicity of the presenter in an otherwise identical ad, specifically to reach a group the previous three winners did not, which CR holds as an iteration rule. Ad-set settings shown, no A/B against a targeted alternative, so the practice is T3 and the routing mechanism behind it is T4 assertion. Ben Heath states the same mechanism as the reason creative diversity is now mandatory: post-Andromeda delivery works out "this individual should see this ad instead of this ad", so splitting one creative pool across identical parallel campaigns degrades the personalisation.
Summit relay, second-hand, and worth exactly the weight that carries. Andrew Faris reports an unnamed Meta VP of analytics presenting the ranking and retrieval stack on stage at the 2026 Performance Marketing Summit, and gives the direction as engagement over profile: what Meta is "getting better at all the time is using engagement-based signals, not just profiles of people, but engagement-based signals of who is engaging with your ads, with your content, to deliver the next piece of content. And to do that while retrieving more and more of your best ads and getting them to people more often, particularly the more diverse your ads are." His own reading of the payoff is the narrowing rule he states elsewhere in this claim: "you have the ability, and this is the critical payoff, is to match hyper-specific ad content to hyper-specific niches," and he reports that in his accounts ads with very specific angles aimed at very specific people take off fast. No slide, no deck, no engineering post shown, so it is a relay of a stage presentation and stays T3. The diversity half of that quoted line is the retrieval-side argument behind MD-003's remedy.
CREATIVE OVERRIDES ACCOUNT HISTORY, and this is the sharpest form anyone has put the law in. Sam Piliero: "Even if historically you have run all 65 plus age groups ... If you run a creative in your ad account that is geared to a younger group, it's going to get shown to a younger group." So the routing decision is made on the asset in front of the retrieval model rather than on the demographic profile the account's own purchase history would predict, which is a strictly stronger claim than "creative influences delivery" and the reason a single off-avatar test ad can pull delivery sideways in a mature account. T4 and it stays T4: ASSERTED with no age breakdown shown, no before-and-after on a real account, and no named account. If it holds it also explains part of MD-006, since a new ad with no history has nothing but its own content to be routed on. He states the inversion plainly as the operating consequence, that you now build the ad for an avatar and the ad finds the people, which is what the rest of this claim already says.
THE VOLUME SIDE of the same law, from Matt Shiver, because the standing objection to narrowing is always reach. "a lot of times people think that if I niche down too niche that like, 'How am I going to get enough leads?' ... But it's actually kind of the reverse. It's like as long as the total addressable market TAM is big enough, you're going to be fine." So the constraint he names is TAM size, not audience-setting width, which is the correct place to put it if creative does the targeting. Evidence is one account's low cost per message, and the "you probably could spend upwards of a thousand dollars plus a day" attached to it is a projection of headroom rather than observed spend, so do not quote it as a spend figure. T3, one account, no comparison against a broader avatar. Faris's reach-efficiency point above is the same finding from a different book of accounts.
Input list for the retrieval read, from Mark Builds Brands and consistent with everything above: hook, format, the on-screen demographics of the people in the ad, copy, script, thumbnail and the landing page. On casting, "if you're running an ad that is featuring a woman that is 55 years old, Facebook takes note of that and it's going to use that to power the targeting." Banked as a source rather than a finding, since it restates this claim and Theriot's presenter-swap rule. The landing-page half is MD-043. ASSERTED, nothing shown.

### MD-002 · Meta targeting is vector-embedding based: broad delivery sprinkles spend across the space, then hones into converting clusters
Tier: T2 · Status: active
Interactions no longer apply interest labels; each one nudges the user's position in a tens-of-thousands-dimension embedding space where similar users cluster. Broad targeting seeds small spend across the space, observes who interacts and buys, and concentrates delivery on those clusters, which is why broad beats interest targeting on large datasets over long periods. Interest and lookalike targeting were deprecated in July 2025. Presenter states every mechanic is sourced to Meta's own engineering posts and help documentation. Two supporting points from the same speaker's earlier session, both weaker evidence: roughly 30% of the people inside any Meta interest group were assigned wrongly, because a label records interaction and not intent (comment "I hate dogs" and every dog advertiser targets you), which he sources in his own words as "Meta put out a report or someone put out a report", so treat it as argument rather than evidence. Second, the scaling consequence: spending more expands the targeted region outward from the converting hotspot into colder people who are not convinced, which is why campaigns degrade as budget rises.
Earliest statement of the same mechanism, from the same speaker in March 2025: each interaction bundle gets numeric values and pushes the user's coordinates, users who interact similarly cluster together, and that clustering is offered as the reason two friends see near-identical Reels feeds. He sizes the space at "hundreds if not thousands of axes"; the July 2026 statement says tens of thousands. The dimension count is unsettled inside one speaker's own material, so quote the mechanism and not the number.
The interest-group critique gains a date from a second speaker. Charley T states the Facebook engineering team that maintained interest-group technology was disbanded in 2018, so the classifier is frozen, and repeats the one-third-by-mistake figure in two separate sessions with "I hate cats" as the example that puts you in the cat interest group. The disbandment date carries no cited source. Layer the share who feel positive, then the share who would ever buy, then the share not already loyal to a competitor, and the usable fraction of any interest audience is small.
Signal set, per Andrew Faris quoting a public post: likes, comments, shares, saves, video watch time, in-app search on Facebook and Instagram, and web browsing on pixel sites, with the framing that Meta stopped building a social profile from age, gender, location and interest. The auto-transcript garbles the post author's name ("Alex Skryfeld" in one place, "Alex Gryffindor" in another), so verify the attribution before citing it. Faris says openly the system stays a black box.

### MD-003 · Post-Andromeda, near-duplicate ads collapse into one entity ID and share identical delivery with zero unique reach
Tier: T3 · Status: contested
Two independent sources agree on the core. Fraser Cottrell: brands posting 100 copies of the same ad with minor headline tweaks get those ads flagged as one entity and their results collapse; he flags this especially at $70-100k/month spend, while accounts already launching net-new diverse assets saw essentially no change. Blue Sense: ads judged the same are grouped under one entity ID and all serve to the same audience pool, so 200 similar ads can register as 20; diversity must be at the concept level (angle x offer x persona), and a sufficiently different format can register as a distinct ad even with the same concept. Fraser adds that animating a static into video is very unlikely to be tagged as the same entity because the media type differs.
Two extensions from Blue Sense. First, the metric exists: Meta will give you a creative similarity score, obtainable only by asking a dedicated Meta rep, where a higher number means more similar and therefore worse. Second, the bundling trigger is broader than visual duplication. The same campaign shoot with a different model pose, a UGC script with a mid-video variation, and even formally different videos that speak to the same audience all get bundled into one delivery pool, at which point frequency rises, reach falls and ROAS decays. The remedy is three creatives that address different people, which reach separate pools with little audience overlap.
CONTESTED, scoped to hook swaps. In an earlier transcript the same Blue Sense speaker states the opposite for hook variations: "Meta will still recognize a different hook as a different creative. It will get its own creative ID. It will not get pulled together with this asset. It will go and reach a novel unique audience." Evidence offered: rotating 50 new hooks onto a fatigued ad that had already spent $50-100k bought another $100k of spend at the same efficiency. He concedes partial overlap, since 87 of 90 seconds are unchanged and the concept is identical, and frames hook rotation as worth doing mainly because it is nearly free. Note the chronology: the hook-swap position is from 2026-05-11 and the near-duplicate-collapse position from 2026-07-28, so this may be the same speaker updating rather than a two-party disagreement. MD-043 offers a candidate reconciliation: differences the system can actually parse (media type, landing-page URL, a new 3-second opener) register as distinct, metadata tweaks like a headline swap do not.
DIRECT REBUTTAL, 2026-01-08, and it is the flattest statement anyone has made against the grouping story. Blue Sense raises the Andromeda-groups-your-hooks claim and rejects it from account observation: "it might count the bodies as the same ad and group them together. Well, I can tell you it doesn't because this is still working incredibly well in our accounts." His case is a video testimonial launched 27 April with 2 bodies and 9 hooks that became the account's best ever ad, fatigued at 6 to 7 months, and was revived to the account's top two performers at the same daily spend throughput by adding 4 newly shot hooks. Boundary he attaches, and it matters: the revival hooks were filmed fresh with a different person on camera, not recut from the existing footage, because "often just a change in wording can get a lot of people in, but it's not going to get everyone in. We do need a change in visuals." So his rebuttal is scoped to a swap that changes the visual, which is a difference the parser can see and therefore consistent with MD-043's reconciliation.
On the collapse side, a third voice arrives the same month. Ben Heath states it as a live hazard: "post-Andromeda Meta will see ads with different hooks as the same ad and only enter one into the auction, which can happen." He then prescribes Meta's own ad-level creative testing tool as the workaround, because it forces each variant into delivery against a non-overlapping audience segment (MD-094).
Evidence status, stated plainly: this is assertion against assertion. Blue Sense shows an ad set on screen with the hook variants and dates and no CPA, no ROAS and no spend figure. Heath shows no delivery data. Fraser Cottrell and the July 2026 Blue Sense position show none either. Nobody has produced entity IDs, a creative similarity score, or a unique-reach breakdown for a set of near-duplicates, which is what would settle it. Resolving test: launch a set of variants that differ only in a parsable dimension and a second set that differ only in metadata, then read unique reach per ad.
A working boundary arrives from the practitioner side with the first specific number attached. Matt Shiver deliberately keeps a shared body across a pain-point test and varies only the hook, and he knows it cuts against the consensus: "since Andromeda, like again, the play was like every ad needs to be unique and we're still not doing that with testing because this is just a lot easier for us to get the data faster." His reason is speed of data, not delivery theory. What he did change post-Andromeda is the length of the varying section. The hook went from 3 seconds to "15 to 30 seconds," with the shared body and CTA running another "30 to 60 seconds," on the asserted mechanism that "this to meta it seems so unique or it seems like it's own ad because this hook over here is long enough." That is the first number anyone has put on how much difference is enough, and it is an assertion with no entity IDs, no unique-reach read and no test behind it. It is consistent with MD-043's reconciliation, since 15 to 30 seconds of different footage is a difference the parser can see.

### MD-004 · Update timeline: Lattice 2023, sequence learning Nov 2024, Andromeda Dec 2024, GEM multimodal grouping, adaptive ranking 2026
Tier: T2 · Status: active
Sequence learning (Nov 2024) optimizes which sequence of your ads a user sees across the purchase journey, which killed single-ad-per-ad-set structures. Andromeda (Dec 2024) is a retrieval-stage change that reads creative content itself. GEM, commonly conflated with Andromeda, gave the system multimodal ability (watch video, listen to audio) to group creatives by format and representation, making creative diversity mandatory. The 2026 adaptive ranking model allocates compute per impression and sped up time-to-relevancy; Meta claims a 3% platform-wide conversion lift and 5% CTR lift from it. Named, dated updates cited from Meta engineering posts.
What Lattice actually did, from Matt Steiner of Meta: it collapsed four separately trained prediction models (Facebook clicks, Instagram clicks, Facebook conversions, Instagram conversions) into one model carrying all four training sets, and the consolidated model beats every individual model it replaced "because that larger model is able to learn from more data". That is the same consolidation logic Meta later applied to the foundation model in MD-029, and it is the architectural reason cross-surface and cross-event signal now flows into a single prediction. First-party, no document quoted, no numbers given, so T3.
Meta's own 2026 framing of the stack, relayed off the Performance Summit stage by a panel Charley T hosted: Andromeda is one of five named components, alongside Lattice, GEM, "signal journey" and an adaptive model. "And like seeing Andromeda up there as like one of five... And like it's just a piece." The operator consequence is a naming correction that saves a lot of confused conversation. "Running Andromeda" is not a setting anyone opts into and an account cannot be on or off it. Signal journey is the one component with no entry above it; MD-009 carries what the panel says it does. Panel testimony about a stage presentation, no slide or deck shown, so this paragraph is T3 sitting inside a T2 claim.

### MD-089 · Meta says Andromeda was built because generative AI tooling multiplied creative variations until retrieval became computationally intractable
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Matt Steiner of Meta gives the causal origin in his own words: "That kind of Cambrian explosion of creatives made that retrieval problem much more difficult for our advertising system. There were just so many more ads to consider when determining which ones to rank." So Andromeda is the platform's answer to advertiser creative volume, not to an advertiser targeting problem. Two consequences worth carrying. First, the entity-collapse behaviour in MD-003 and the diminishing-returns position on ad count in CR read as intended outputs of a system built to sort a flood, rather than as side effects. Second, the volume arms race is the thing the retrieval stage exists to price, so adding variants to win delivery is playing against the purpose of the machine. First-party causal account from the system owner, with no volume figures, no document quoted and no data shown, which is why this is T3 and not T1. Pairs with MD-041, which gives the earlier iOS 14 half of the same story.
Independent practitioner arrival at the same story, and it lands on the right stage. Mark Builds Brands: "Andromeda what is was an update to the sorting process of the retrieval steps specifically. Mostly just because there was a massive influx of AI creative that was absolutely flooding Facebook." The retrieval-stage half is correct against MD-022 and MD-024, which document Andromeda as a retrieval-stage change. The AI-flood cause is convergent with the first-party account in the paragraph above, which is the reason it is banked rather than discarded, but he cites no Meta source and flags it himself as belief: "I believe this is one of the biggest ones." Label it what it is, a practitioner speculating his way to a conclusion Meta happens to state, with the narrower "AI creative" wording where Meta says a Cambrian explosion of creatives from generative tooling. ### MD-005 · Andromeda scores every ad on two dimensions: efficiency versus account peers AND scalability, not efficiency alone
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Meta evaluates (1) can this ad hit the account's efficiency target better than its peers and (2) can this ad actually absorb spend. This explains why some ads post very high ROAS but only get a few dollars of delivery, and why forcing spend onto them tanks results. It also explains the scaling wall: an account can sustain $100, $500, $1,000 or $5,000/day, but a jump from 100 to 200 or 1,000 to 1,200 can degrade ROAS below the lower-budget level. Speaker claims $500M lifetime and $100M managed in the Andromeda era.

### MD-042 · Audience memory lives in the Page, Instagram account, ad account and website; the pixel is a conduit that does not learn
Tier: T3 · Status: contested
Refutes the "season your pixel" folklore. The pixel is "the paper a vote is written on", and the systems counting the votes are what learn: the Page learns who interacts with it, the Instagram account learns what content people engage with, the ad account learns who buys, the website holds its own history. Cited payoff: an operator pushing $4-6M/month has built multiple Facebook Pages, each with its own personality and audience profile, and runs ads from each so Meta can match distinct personas to distinct buyers. Also offered as the explanation for why two brands running near-identical creative get different outcomes. ASSERTED only, and weakly: a third-party account, spend level asserted, nothing shown. Two risks the source does not address. The multi-Page play carries the ban-wave and Business Manager exposure of MD-038, and it stacks against the per-Page live-ad cap in MD-066.
CONTESTED 2026-08-21, and the disagreement is three-way. Matt Shiver puts the learning squarely back in the pixel and adds an account-maturity cost curve on top of it: "the pixel is the brain engine of the ad account ... your costs are often going to be higher when you get started, and as you spend more money, as your entire ad account learns, costs will go down." His own sentence carries both locations at once, the pixel as the brain and the ad account as the learner, so it is not a clean rebuttal of the paragraph above. ASSERTED, with no comparison between a new account and a mature one, no CPA-versus-cumulative-spend curve, and no account named. Note the third position, because the codex now holds all three: SC-094 in another file puts accumulated learning in the ad ACCOUNT rather than the pixel, and a separate merge on 2026-08-21 flipped that claim to contested because a second operator puts it in the pixel. So one voice says Page plus Instagram plus ad account plus website, one says ad account, and two now say pixel. Nobody has shown data on any of the three. What would resolve it is the one test none of them run: a fresh pixel installed on a mature ad account, or a fresh ad account fed a mature pixel, then read cost per result against the incumbent. Until then treat pixel seasoning as unproven in both directions and stop using either version to justify spend.
Separate from the location question, the maturity half of Shiver's statement is its own untested folklore. "Costs go down as the account spends more" is indistinguishable from survivorship, since the accounts that keep spending are the ones where costs came down. MD-035 points the opposite way at the platform level, with CPM inflation running roughly 20 to 30% a year, so any account-level cost decline has to beat that before it means anything.

### MD-070 · A creative asset carries its learned performance across campaigns, and changing the headline or primary text changes it
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Charley T says a video or static keeps the performance it has learned no matter which campaign it is placed into, so re-uploading a proven asset elsewhere does not restart it from zero. Changing the headline or the primary text attached to it does change the performance. Two operator consequences follow. A winner that fails in a new ad set is more likely losing to the ad next to it than decaying on its own, so check the neighbours before killing it. And a copy-only refresh on a proven asset is a change to that asset's delivery, not a free test. Sits in tension with MD-003, which reports that metadata tweaks like a headline swap do not register as a distinct entity. Both can hold only if a headline swap changes how an existing asset is scored without making it a new entity. Asserted, no data shown.

### MD-071 · Andromeda did not change the rules, it raised the price of a messy account
Tier: T3 · Status: contested
Charley T's one-line version: "Andromeda at a very simple level, Andromeda meant that doing a bad job hurt you more." The named patterns that got more expensive are five different offers inside one account, twenty new ads launched per week, and multiple landing pages and angles running at once. His framing is that before Andromeda you could brute-force outcomes with more ads, more audiences and more effort and outrun bad habits, and that the update amplifies those same mistakes instead of introducing new rules.
CONTESTED, with thin evidence on both sides. His supporting case is a single counter-anecdote: an interviewee whose cost per lead fell $2 while Andromeda arrived unnoticed and Reddit filled with $100k-a-month stores reporting collapse. ### MD-008 · Early-life frequency reveals learning: frequency 1.6 in the first days means 60% saw it twice in one day; frequency falling as spend rises means the ad is moving up-funnel, not dying
Tier: T3 · Status: active

He grounds high frequency as desirable through the frequency-illusion sequence: frequency bias at 3-5 impressions, selection bias at 5-10, confirmation bias well past 12, making 12+ exposures the familiarity target rather than a warning.
