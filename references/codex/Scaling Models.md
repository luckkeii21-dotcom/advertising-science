# Scaling Models

How spend scales without efficiency collapse: budget moves, consolidation, structure, cost controls.

Part of the [[00-Codex|Advertising Science Codex]]. Claims follow the tier system (T1 docs, T2 shown test, T3 practitioner, T4 theory).

## Claims

## When and how much to raise budget

### SC-001 · How big a budget step is safe is contested: prescriptions range from 5% or less to 20% per day to "the 20% rule is folklore"
Tier: T3 · Status: contested
Professor Charley T says scale at the campaign level by 5% or less, and only when 7-day performance is about 10% better than target CPA (example: target CPA $50, actual $45), so even zero incremental conversions keep you under target. Sam Piliero scales the move to the margin above target: 5-10% just above goal (2.1 vs 2.0), 20-30% comfortably above (2.5 vs 2.0), 50%+ when far exceeding (3-4x a 2.0 target), and already-scaled accounts should move in absolute increments of $500-$2,000, which may only be 5-15% of budget. Nick Theriot moves CBO budgets in 20% steps up or down gated by KPI, and when a client is under KPI he bumps 20% per day; he showed a client launched March 27 at $56 cost per purchase on $3,900 day spend falling to $33 CPP as new ad sets took spend, then scaling proceeded. Ben Heath rejects any fixed 20% rule as not nuanced: tripling $3/day to $10/day is fine, but at $100k/month even $120k in one step is a reasonably large increase and $300k would almost certainly kill the results, because the good-prospect pool is already saturated. Blue Sense Digital goes furthest: no Meta documentation supports the 20% rule, and his agency raises budgets by more than 20% routinely with no issue.

Platform split added 2026-08-18, which partly dissolves the disagreement. Blue Sense Digital says the 20% rule is real and it is a Google rule: "the rule that you've always heard, increase budgets in 20% increments. Uh, it's real. It applies on Google. On Meta, Tik Tok, you have a bit more flexibility." Google is the more stable platform, converts more consistently, and rewards slow methodical changes, so doubling a budget overnight or halving a target CPA overnight is a bad idea there. The move is asymmetric: bringing Google budgets down fast, including halving them, produces little instability or performance drop-off. Every prescription above is Meta-centric, so read the 5%-to-folklore spread as a Meta argument and the 20% step as a Google constraint. Practitioner observation across an agency book, no test data shown.

Charley T's own number as of Feb 2026 is +5% three times a week, or a flat +$10/day on an established campaign, both conditional on the business absorbing the volume. His arithmetic that 5% x3/week doubles the budget monthly is wrong: 1.05^12 = 1.80, so it is about 1.8x. The +$10/day version checks out (a $400/day campaign reaches $4,050/day in 365 days).

Four additions from four more of his streams, Dec 2025 to Feb 2026, which fix the rule's exact shape. First, it is an automated rule keyed to cost per result, not to ROAS: set the target CPA, and when actual CPA is at least 10% better than target, raise budget 5%. Second, the cadence is Monday, Wednesday and Friday, gated on trailing 7-day CPA rather than a single day. Third, he restates the doubling claim in a weaker form, "if it fires two thirds of the time it doubles budget in about a month", which is also wrong: 8 firings gives 1.05^8 = 1.48. Treat the monthly multiple as roughly 1.5x to 1.8x, never 2x. Fourth, he states the 5% step holds at every spend level including $5, $15 and $25 a day, with a supplement brand product-testing at $25/day and another account starting at $15/day both raising by 5%; the mechanism he gives is that a proportional increment is a proportional disruption to delivery, so it is size-neutral. The floor case is asserted with no test data.
Contested again 2026-08-19 by three more transcripts, and the spread widened instead of narrowing. Ben Heath's automated-rule band sits BELOW everything previously banked here: 3% per day gated on cost per result staying under target, with 5% to 7% named as the hard ceiling for a daily cadence, "I wouldn't go above much above that if you're going to be scaling daily, otherwise that could just be too fast, too aggressive a scaling plan for Meta's systems to be able to keep up." His MANUAL model is a different shape entirely and is banked separately as [[Scaling Models#SC-100|SC-100]]: step size is a decreasing function of current budget, 100% at $50/day falling to 20% at $800/day, with each step held 5 to 10 days. That reframes the whole question from "what percentage" to "what percentage at what budget over what hold", which no prescription in this claim previously captured, and it dissolves part of the apparent conflict, since a 20% daily cap and a 100% weekly step are the same envelope expressed at different cadences. Nick Theriot confirms his 20% at the CBO campaign level, gated on whether YESTERDAY hit the cost per purchase target and held flat the day it misses while a new creative is found. He adds two boundaries. Timing inside the day is explicitly irrelevant, "I may not get to my ad account or like my laptop to like 2 3:00 in the afternoon. Cool. I'm going to bump up budget by 20% if yesterday's KPIs are hitting target." And there is one live exception, roughly once a year: on Black Friday he doubles the budget two or three times in the same day because the event has a 24-hour deadline. His reason for 20% over big jumps is loss absorption rather than delivery mechanics, at 20% a bad day is survivable, and a 10x jump is "where people start playing the gambling game." None of the three shows a controlled step-size test. The banked disagreement now runs from 3% per day to 100% per step depending on budget level and hold duration, so any single percentage quoted as the rule is folklore.

Correction to the Theriot entry, 2026-08-20, and it matters because this claim currently records his 20% step as gated on yesterday's cost per acquisition. ### SC-002 · A ROAS dip after a budget raise is time-to-purchase lag from newly reached upper-funnel people, not a learning reset
Tier: T3 · Status: contested
Multiple independent sources give the same mechanism: Meta serves the cheapest, most-likely buyers first, so added impressions go to upper-funnel people who need days to convert. Sam Piliero's illustrative averages: bottom-of-funnel converts in ~0 days, middle-of-funnel 1-2 days, top-of-funnel 5-10 days, so moving $1,000 to $1,500/day shows an immediate ROAS dip that partially recovers as the new cohort matures. Blue Sense Digital makes the same argument: tripling budget today does not triple revenue today because newly reached audiences need multiple exposures over their time-to-purchase window, and people misread the lag as the algorithm punishing the change. The correct response is patience, not resetting the account. Professor Charley T extends the same mechanism from budget raises to campaign launch: a new one-campaign build reliably performs well for about three days and then degrades, because the machine harvests existing bottom-funnel demand first and then pushes delivery up-funnel. His prescription is to let it ride, and "if you can't afford that struggle, work on the business before you run ads." He adds that high CPMs at launch on this structure are normal and come down. All asserted, no shown curve.

CONTESTED 2026-08-21, and the disagreement is about where the incremental impression actually goes. Every source above assumes added budget reaches NEW, colder people who convert later. Blue Sense Digital argues the opposite on the same platform: added budget does not push product-aware creative outward to cold audiences at all, it re-serves the same warm pool harder and frequency climbs. "as you crank up budget, as you try to scale, meta goes, 'No, no, no. We're not going to push these to cold audiences. We'll just continue reservering heavier to warm.'" If he is right, the post-raise dip is not a lag that recovers, it is the leading edge of a ceiling, and waiting for the cohort to mature never pays off. The two positions are not reconcilable by patience, since one says wait and the other says the wait is wasted. His version is banked as a ceiling mechanism at [[Scaling Models#SC-124|SC-124]]. Neither side has shown a frequency, CPM or cohort curve, so the disagreement is currently between two unmeasured mechanisms. The test that would settle it is cheap: pull the audience-segment breakdown and the frequency trend before and after a raise on the same account.

### SC-003 · Scale only when target is beaten across stacked lookbacks: 30-day, 14-day, 7-day, 3-day, and yesterday, weighting recent windows most
Tier: T3 · Status: active
A 30-day view can read 8.46 against an 8.0 target while the 14-day is 7.3 and the 7-day 6.94; that regression means do not scale despite the healthy monthly number. Only when all windows, especially the most recent, sit above the goal is the account ready for a budget move. ### SC-004 · Account diagnostic is two questions: if you can spend more profitably, touch nothing and scale; if not, test one new 322 against the single worst-performing ring
Tier: T3 · Status: active
When the answer to "can I spend more money and stay profitable" is yes, launching new ads or restructuring is the single worst move. When no, identify the prospecting ring or ad set consuming disproportionate spend with the worst results relative to target, and build new 322 ads designed to do that ring's job better, run in the test ad sets. One change, one test; if the new 322 earns spend away from the underperformer it is doing the job better.

The metric that answers question one is spend concentration inside the flexible ad, and he states it as the only KPI that matters: "There is no rush to pull post IDs. You do not need to get winning ads. You need to be good enough to increase your budget. That's it." Spend allocation is the system reporting what it believes in, so a 322 that earns spend and lets you raise budget is finished work. Hunting the winning permutation inside it, or interrupting learning to extract a post ID, is named as one of the most common mistakes. Weekly review collapses to one question: can I increase the budget, yes or no. Asserted from managing "well over a million dollars a week", no data shown.

Restated in two more streams as an order of operations on creative, which is the sharper form: "If you can spend more, you don't launch a new test." Headroom is the signal. While budget can still go up profitably the current creative set is not exhausted, so a new test only adds moving parts and variance for no gain. He applies it even to a viewer whose DCT test had just produced a winner: if you are winning, move nothing, and if you can still raise the budget the test is not over. Creative testing is triggered only when the existing ads stop supporting scale, and it is then directed by the 4PI analysis rather than by launching more ads at random. Note the tension with [[Scaling Models#SC-079|SC-079]], where the same speaker argues efficiency work beats a budget increase because added spend introduces instability; he does not reconcile the two.

Stated once more as the success criterion for a creative test, added 2026-08-20, which is the same rule read from the other end: "My goal in creative testing is to establish enough efficiency that I can increase the budget. I don't want new winners." He attaches a second-order claim to it in the same breath, and that part is new: testing fewer ads means less ad fatigue and a higher creative hit rate on the ads that survive, "In fact, if I'm not testing more ads, I'm not going to have nearly as much ad fatigue, and my creative hit rate is probably actually going to go up. It's just on fewer ads." The budget-headroom criterion is already banked here. The fatigue and hit-rate consequence is asserted with no data and runs against the creative-volume doctrine in the CR topic, so do not quote it as evidence that lower volume wins.

### SC-005 · Break results down by attribution setting before any scale or pause decision; scaling view-through-inflated campaigns fails
Tier: T2 · Status: active
Advertisers who take standard-attribution results at face value scale campaigns propped up by non-incremental view-through and engage-through conversions, then are confused when scaling collapses, or they pause a genuinely better ad because its comparator is view-through-inflated. Before any scale/pause decision, check how many conversions Meta itself flags as incremental via compare attribution settings.

Documented case, SHOWN as a per-campaign attribution comparison plus a CAC-over-time chart and a gross-profit-on-first-purchase chart. An agency pushed 60-70% of account-wide daily budget into the campaign with the best default-attribution ROAS at 2.21. On 7-day click that campaign was the worst in the account. It was a sales campaign with no audience exclusions, so it retargeted warm and existing customers and collected non-incremental conversions; it looked best precisely because it was least incremental. Result was the highest blended CAC in the brand's history and a negative first-time-customer P&L. Operator move: run the attribution comparison per campaign before reallocating, not at account level.

### SC-006 · Scale an existing entity in place rather than duplicating; the duplicate-at-higher-budget trick is only a temporary bug workaround, and never run replicas simultaneously
Tier: T3 · Status: active
In-place scaling keeps accrued learning, so results are better; duplicating on every increase produces dozens or hundreds of entities and an unmanageable account; and duplicates can reshuffle delivery, for example an ad set where 80% of spend went to 2 of 20 ads can, after duplication, give those two proven winners no spend at all. During a 2026 delivery bug, the fix was duplicating the winning campaign/ad set directly at the higher budget and turning the original OFF; running the original alongside the duplicate, or 8 replica ad sets, is data fragmentation and a disaster. Explicitly framed as a temporary fix, not the normal scaling method.

Two more sources name the same anti-pattern, and both call it the most common structural mistake they see: pausing a winner in order to relaunch it inside a scaling campaign. Blue Sense Digital: "if something works, don't touch it," and the two costs are a learning-phase reset and, for operators who do not carry the post ID, the loss of all accumulated social proof on the creative. The rule extends to adding creative: injecting new ads into a performing ad set disrupts the sequencing Meta has learned there, so new creative for a winning concept goes into a NEW ad set named for the same concept, while an underperforming ad set can be edited freely because its sequencing is not working anyway. At KPI, raise budget and touch nothing else. See also [[Scaling Models#SC-028|SC-028]] for the scaling-campaign move done correctly, by post ID, with the original left running.

DATED PRE-BUG BASELINE, added 2026-08-19, and it is worth preserving precisely because the August 2026 in-place budget-raise bug is a live watch item. On 2026-05-19, three months before that bug, Ben Heath prescribed the in-place raise and rejected duplication outright on auction-overlap grounds: "option one is to leave the existing campaign running, duplicate it, and then run an identical version alongside the original. That I do not like at all. Meta does not like it either. When you use that approach, you have competing campaigns running alongside each other, you run into serious auction overlap issues." He adds the accumulation cost from audits, accounts that scaled purely by duplication carrying 11 identical campaigns running alongside one another. Neither of his 2026 transcripts in this pass touches the bug. So this is the before state: if the duplicate-at-new-budget workaround is ever endorsed by the same source, that reversal is bug-driven and the auction-overlap reasoning behind the normal rule is unchanged. Keep the date attached whenever this is quoted.

One boundary added to the do-not-inject-new-creative half, 2026-08-19. Blue Sense Digital allows new creative to go straight into an Advantage+ campaign and restricts the prohibition to testing-level ad sets inside an ABO or CBO, "I wouldn't recommend just throwing it in here. It's going to mess with the learning spend distribution a little bit." Sam Piliero states the strictest version of the same rule as an absolute across roughly 100 brands: "Every time we launch new ads, we launch them into a new pack or a new ad set. We never inject new ads into old packs. Let that sit with you. That is critical." That is banked with his pack system in [[Scaling Models#SC-024|SC-024]]. All three positions are asserted, and no source has shown a before-and-after on injecting versus not injecting.

Two dated Heath additions from 2026-06-03, added 2026-08-20, which sit between his 2026-05-19 baseline above and the August bug. First, one scoped case where he endorses duplicating over editing: Meta's AI assistant recommended duplicating an ad set to switch the performance goal to maximize value, and he agreed with the instruction as given, "It's interesting it says how to do it as well. It says duplicate it. It's like it's a good recommendation there." That is an OPTIMISATION-EVENT change, not a budget raise, so it does not reverse his in-place budget prescription. Hold the two apart whenever he is quoted on duplication. Second, the don't-touch-a-winner rule applied to his own account and to a setting he believes is better: he refused to move a long-running campaign onto the newer default and runs separate campaigns to test it instead, "We want clear uh demonstrable improvement performance from optimizing for value in this instance before we switch over." The operating shape is that a settings upgrade is a test you run beside the winner, never on it.

### SC-044 · A concept test needs 3x target CPA of spend before a verdict, 5x preferred, run over about a week
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Multiply target CPA by three; that is the spend floor before deciding on or off. At 3x, three or more conversions is a pass, two is a yellow light worth another increment, zero or one is a kill. He prefers 5x because early spend is the algorithm learning who to target and efficiency improves after that, and at 5x the decision is binary with no yellow light. The one-week duration floor exists for two separate reasons: launching on a strong or weak weekday biases the read, and buyers typically need 2-4 impressions across 3-4 days, so a one-day test kills top-of-funnel openers whose conversions land later on other creative. Adjust to 3-4 days for large stable businesses and 10-14 days for small budgets with long purchase cycles. Asserted, no shown test.

Second independent operator on the same multiple, added 2026-08-20, and his is a lead-gen account rather than e-commerce, which is the useful part for our book. Matt Shiver gates every ad-set change on 3x the target cost per result: "typically uh let's just say your cost per call is 200 dollars that we're shooting for, we need to spend $600, okay, before we make any changes on this ad set." His launch budget for a five-pain-point ad set is $100 to $250 a day, so the gate clears in roughly three to six days at that spend. He states 3x flatly with no 5x preference and no yellow-light band. Two operators, two verticals, one number, and neither has shown why 3x rather than 2x or 5x. He re-runs the same gate on whatever ad takes over spend next, which is the enforcement mechanism behind the quality-triggered kill recorded in [[Scaling Models#SC-008|SC-008]].

### SC-045 · Percentage of spend equals percentage of change equals percentage of confidence, so size every account action by the entity's spend share
Tier: T4 · Status: active
"If an ad spends 1% of your budget, turning it off is a 1% change with 99% confidence. But if an ad spends 50% of your budget, turning that off is a 50% change with 50% confidence and could very well reset everything." This puts pausing and budget moves on one scale and generalises [[Scaling Models#SC-008|SC-008]] from rank (never kill the top two spenders) to spend share (never make a change larger than your confidence). He restates that a +/-20% budget change will almost certainly disrupt learning. The operating instruction is to make the smallest move with the highest confidence of a net positive impact, then measure, then repeat: audit, plan, optimize, measure, repeat. Reasoning only, no test.

The sequencing corollary, added 2026-08-19: fix one thing at a time, ordered by expected size of effect, and never run five tests in parallel. His argument is budget dilution rather than statistics, "you're going to spend like 20 bucks a day on all of them", so five simultaneous tests split the budget into slices too small to read. ### SC-014 · Campaign/ad-set structure exists for data siloing; hyper-segmentation fragments conversion data and hurts performance on Meta, Google and TikTok alike
Tier: T3 · Status: active

Most accounts over-segment for reporting comfort, spreading conversions too thin per silo. The siloing is not uniform by level, which is the operationally important part. Conversion data is siloed hardest at the campaign level and shared far more freely at the ad-set level, so ad-set segmentation swings efficiency only 5-10% while campaign segmentation fragments the signal outright: four campaigns on 100 conversions/month leaves each with 25 and guarantees small-sample bias. Professor Charley T states a stronger version, that campaigns do not merely dilute each other but actively cannibalise: "campaigns don't talk to each other. That version is reasoning only, and it should be scoped to undifferentiated duplication, because the same speaker deliberately runs multiple differentiated ad sets in [[Scaling Models#SC-021|SC-021]] and [[Scaling Models#SC-022|SC-022]].
Charley T's own stated reason for the one-campaign structure is data volume and stability in one place rather than signal dilution per se. His analogy is a sales manager: one well-trained employee spending the majority of the budget beats a hundred untrained ones. He says there is no valid reason to run many campaigns and cites remediating an account that had spent a couple of hundred thousand dollars under a split testing-campaign and scaling-campaign structure. ### SC-052 · Consolidation's second mechanism is reach, not conversion data: campaign reach does not sum, and fewer ad sets lowers frequency, lowers CPMR, and buys more reach at the same spend

### SC-054 · Segment by product-level unit economics, because bidding on lowest CPA or even on ROAS routes spend to the worse contribution-margin product
Tier: T3 · Status: active
Worked example on Meta: a $90 hoodie with $21 COGS and a $45 CAC yields $24 contribution margin; a t-shirt with $27 gross margin and a $15 CAC yields $12. Maximize conversions funds the t-shirt because its CPA is lower, and switching to conversion value does not fix it because the t-shirt's ROAS is also better. Only campaign segmentation forces spend onto the higher-contribution product. A second question falls out of the same table: a product acquiring that cheaply may have organic pull and would sell anyway, in which case the right move is to stop paying for it entirely and redirect the budget to the product that needs paid support to hit sell-through. Kiel and Faris reach the same conclusion from AOV: "a product with a $200 AOV and a product with a $300 AOV require a really different CAC to be successful. And even if it's the same ROAS," combining them in one volume-maximising ad set buys one of them at the wrong price. Their mental model is a department store, one campaign per section. Both agree the legitimate reason to split is economic (different AOV, margin, inventory constraint, or genuinely distinct audiences like menswear vs kids vs pets) and never a "sandbox" reason. The cost of that structure is that it needs stronger exclusions and active CPMR, frequency and new-visitor-percentage monitoring. This is the main commercial argument against the consolidation default. Reasoning plus worked arithmetic, no shown test.
Third voice added 2026-08-19, and it is the mechanism stated plainly. Professor Charley T: a conversion campaign asks for the highest COUNT of purchases, not the highest value, so products at different prices in one campaign hand delivery to whatever converts most often. His analogy is a stick of gum in a Lamborghini showroom, where selling the gum satisfies the instruction and sells no Lamborghinis. His rule is separate campaigns per product, then cut the campaigns you no longer need, which sits against the consolidation-first position he otherwise argues, and he does not reconcile the two. His converse is the useful boundary: when products share a price point and customer-journey value the SKU distinction does not matter to delivery, so keep them in one campaign and split by market tier instead (see [[Scaling Models#SC-063|SC-063]]).

Faris's implementation, and the cap constraint that comes with it. He inherited an account running a ~$250 AOV SKU and a ~$400 AOV SKU in the same highest-volume campaign with spend concentrated on the $250 SKU. The constraint he draws from it is a hard precondition for any cap or bid to function: "an ad set must be AOV coherent." That means one product or offer per ad set, which also makes inventory velocity manageable because you can slow one SKU in one place, or a cluster whose AOVs sit within a few dollars, as he runs on a jewelry client. A $25 AOV product beside a $100 AOV product in one ad set breaks the cap. Outcome asserted, no numbers shown.

Michael Nadalin takes the middle position between the two loud camps and names the price of the split: excess segmentation dilutes conversion data across campaigns and weakens each one, so he rejects both "run two campaigns and that is it" and "run eight campaigns, one per category". His test for whether a split is worth paying for is whether it maps to a stated business objective, for example a stocked SKU the client needs pushed or a paid partnership. He adds that most performance marketers lack the business acumen to make that call, which is why they default to a rule of thumb in either direction. Reasoning, no test shown.

### SC-025 · Whether a new test ad set gets a spend MINIMUM or a spend MAXIMUM is contested, though both sides cap testing at 20% of campaign budget
Tier: T3 · Status: contested

Restated 2026-08-19 with the arithmetic made explicit and the verdict rule stated as a rule. The minimum is set at exactly 1x target CPA, "if my target CPA is $100, I set this to 100." The read after the minimum comes off is behavioural and it is the operationally useful part: "If the ad set is functioning well and it's crushing it, it's going to keep spending over that minimum anyway. If it's a dud and it's under spending against that minimum, like if you've set it for $100 and it's hitting just $100 every single day, then once you take this away, it's probably going to fade down." Note what that is, a kill rule that never touches a high spender. The algorithm's own allocation is the verdict and the operator only removes an artificial floor, which is why this belongs in the doom-cycle record at [[Scaling Models#SC-085|SC-085]] as well as here. Formula and cap both asserted with worked arithmetic, no data showing 1x or 20% are the right numbers.

Charley T side, maximum, added 2026-08-19. ### SC-092 · Budget allocation weights the ad's own CTR history and the ad set's exclusions on top of the per-user prediction, so an exclusion list mechanically depresses predicted CTR

### SC-121 · Ads are not turned off between weekly launches, so the LIVE ad count inside a CBO accumulates every Monday while the LAUNCH RATE stays fixed, and this is why operators quoting ad counts are not talking about the same number

### SC-144 · TAM saturation is a hard scaling ceiling and it can be computed before the spend increase, not after
